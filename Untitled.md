Chapter: I
Adivasis and Plantation Labour: An Introduction
 
The thesis offers an ethnographic account of contemporary plantations and Adivasi plantation labourers in Wayanad, Kerala, and Kodagu, Karnataka. Adivasi plantation labourers from the Kattunayakan, Adiya, Paniya, and Bettakurumba communities in Wayanad are no longer working on plantations; they have become mobile labourers, travelling to plantations in Kodagu. Labour records from three plantations in the Thirunelli panchayat of Wayanad indicate that Adivasis have been moving to plantations in the Kodagu borderlands since the 1980s. For example, Adivasis made up 80 per cent of the permanent labour force on the plantations in Thirunelli, Wayanad, during the 1980s. During the fieldwork period, the three large plantations of Thirunelli had fewer than 5 per cent Adivasi plantation labourers in permanent positions. In Bargiri plantations, Adivasi permanent labourers accounted for less than 3 per cent; there was an Adivasi labourer named Chomi. In Brahamagiri A plantation, the number of Adivasi labourers in the permanent labour force was zero. In PeeVeeS plantation, there is a Kattunayakan couple in the permanent labour force, settled in the plantation's padi[1]. When it comes to casual plantation labourers, there are Adivasis working in the plantations of Wayanad. Namely, older women and men, young mothers with infants and those who find it difficult to travel to Kodagu for health reasons. The labour records clearly show that Adivasis are leaving both permanent and casual plantation work in Wayanad to take up the same jobs under a more vulnerable labour regime in the Kodagu borderlands. 
 
The question of why Adivasis would leave their permanent jobs with all benefits, such as a provident fund, paid leave, sick leave, medical reimbursements, a bonus and travel, sometimes even travelling a hundred kilometres a day to plantations, where wage theft, verbal and physical assaults, and even Adivasi killings are routinised, is surprising. This labour pattern does not fit classical migration theories, which posit that labourers move for better wages, labour conditions, or perks. Drawing on fieldwork insights presented in the thesis, it is evident that the Adivasi plantation labourers of Wayanad had a different political imagination of labour, one that went beyond class politics. Adivasis felt “stifled’ and “chained” to labour on the Wayanad plantations. This was an attempt to sedentarise and fix them into a category of “class subjects”. Adivasi labourers felt that the labour process and concomitant accumulation were alienating and estranging them from the teleological process inherent in labour, its potential and consciousness. Adivasis became mobile as an expression of labour teleology and as a form of dissent against class politics and capital accumulation on the plantations of Wayanad. 
 
Mobility offered Adivasis the chance to uphold their idea of labour rooted in Adivasi consciousness. By migrating to the borderlands of Kodagu, they chose to remain true to their identity rooted in consciousness rather than become alienated beings in the class. Migration to the Kodagu borderland plantations enabled the Adivasis to preserve their cultural and social practices, such as ucchal[2] and adiyanthiram[3], which are closely intertwined with their community, identity, and ethos. For Adivasis, plantation labour in Kodagu enabled them to address material, corporeal, and mundane living conditions and needs, such as saving money to obtain a drinking water pipe connection, and to establish and maintain cosmic relationships with the spirit world. 
 
Plantations in Kodagu operate on a contradictory duality – at one end, they cherish and foster life within their governing logic, so that, at the other end, extraction can be routinely carried out. Accumulation on Kodagu plantations takes new forms through bondage, immobilisation, and death. The journey with Adivasi labourers to Kodagu broadened the scope of the study by engaging not only with Adivasi plantation labourers from Wayanad but also with respondents from the Yerava and Jenukuruba communities who worked as bonded labourers on plantations in Kodagu. The Adivasis of the Nagarhole forests moved to nearby plantations owned by Kodava planters as plantation labourers after being evicted from their forest in the Nagarhole Tiger Reserve following the state's formation. There, they were immobilised, abused, disabled, or killed. 
 
By being mobile, Adivasis avert these risks lurking on Kodagu plantations. Mobility enabled Adivasis to keep extraction at arm’s length, even when they were engaged in plantation labour. They moved among multiple plantations and modes of production, such as foraging, gathering, and shifting cultivation, which helped keep capital’s penetration and attempts to hegemonise Adivasi labour at bay. The Jenukuruba and Yerava communities of Kodagu are reclaiming their autonomy by leaving the plantations and returning to the forests. By returning to the forest in Nagarhole as a dissent from aggressive plantation capitalism, the Adivasi community is rooting for the teleological positing of their labour. Rather than being shaped by the realities of capitalist labour relations and alienating themselves from the teleological positing, Jenukurubas are returning to the forests of Nagarhole. The forests are not merely a landscape for the Jenukurubas; they offer a sense of meaning, autonomy, and belonging.
 
The thesis explores the dynamics and complexities of Adivasi migration across multiple scales. By ethnographically illustrating the reasons for Adivasi migration, the thesis foregrounds migration as an independent, creative choice that shapes and is shaped by labour, consciousness, and autonomy, rather than categorising it as a survival mechanism. For Adivasis, labour is not merely a means of subsistence; it is a meaning-making process closely linked to their autonomy and Adivasi belonging. A subjectivity emerges from and in labour, defining and redefining the lifeworld for Adivasis. Plantation labour becomes a conscious experience that shapes the everyday, mobility, and dissent of Adivasi labourers. Labour is an original form of practice, closely intertwined with the Adivasi idea of identity, autonomy, and the will to prevail. Lukács, interpreting Marx, argues that labour and its fundamental function become the founding element of sociality and the genesis of society (Lukács, 1978). With the help of Marx’s celebrated example of ‘the bee and the architect’ from Volume 1 of Capital, Lukács (1978) elaborates on conscious labour, which gives purpose to the human activity called labour (Marx, 1867/1990). The key idea is that the nature of social being and their primary activities are not determined by biological capacities, such as engaging in plantation labour, but are socially mediated. 
 
Labour is a teleological positing realised within material being as the realisation of a new objectivity (Lukács, 1978). Adivasi plantation labourers in Wayanad realised that the essence of labour is the consciousness that emerges in the process, and this realisation helped them reimagine their lives and labour through mobility. The question was whether to alienate the purpose of labour and the consciousness within the plantation labour force in Wayanad through extraction, casualisation, and unscrupulous accumulation, or to migrate to Kodagu and engage with capital on their own terms. Adivasis chose to be mobile as a form of dissent against the alienation of labour and its teleology in the process of accumulation through class formation and sedentarisation within the permanent and casual labour forces of plantations in Wayanad. Adivasi plantation labourers felt that rather than being employed as permanent or casual labourers in Wayanad plantations, where extraction is the by-product of labour, Kodagu offered labour and freedom. Kodagu plantations were never devoid of extraction; however, Adivasis determined the extent of extraction they had to bear by moving into and out of plantations. This choice was conscious mobility from incessant extraction to extraction on the labourer’s terms of engagement, which Adivasis felt as autonomy. They had the chance to labour and, at the same time, to engage in their traditional livelihoods, such as foraging and gathering, and to strengthen their relationships with their multispecies kin and spiritworld. For Adivasis, labour is also a meaning-making process that shapes their existence and identity, rooted in their Adivasi belonging.
 
For Lukács, labour is a complex act of consciousness and material activity, distinctive to human beings. The teleological positing of labour is rooted in this consciousness, which makes human beings distinct from other animals and makes them social beings. Labour has a desired goal that is not merely subsistence but flourishing, creativity, and endurance of what matters to their social existence, which is part of consciousness. In Marx’s words, this is what it is to be a “species-being” (Gattungswesen) (Marx, 1844/1977). However, in the process of capital accumulation, labour is alienated from species consciousness and estranged within the labour process, as with Adivasi labourers on Wayanad plantations. The dialectical character of labour is its teleology, which encompasses the activity and consciousness of species (Marx, 1844/1977). The thesis narrates the experiences of Adivasis in circumventing the homogenising influence of the global capital system by migrating to Kodagu; not just an expression of dissent against exploitation, it is also about expending labouring power on their own terms for multiple existences. 
 
The thesis is a critical reflection on the idea of Adivasi mobilities as a means of autonomy. Adivasis of Wayanad are moving to the plantations as a form of dissent and a means of engaging with capitalist labour relations on their own terms. Adivasis of Kodagu are moving from the plantations to their ancestral forest land, resisting capitalist accumulation and calling it ‘homecoming’. Autonomy has many meanings and is shaped by political and economic realities, mediated by Adivasis’ social and cultural relations. Access to territory and self-governance are customary approaches to autonomy worldwide. However, in situations such as the landlessness that Adivasis of the Wayanad plantation belt have faced since colonial times, due to the expansion of plantation cultivation and monocropping, autonomy takes multiple forms. For instance, the demand for self-rule in Wayanad has been evident since the 1990s. The Kudilketti Samaram (Hut Strike) of 2001 in front of the Kerala Secretariat was the first state-wide protest that Adivasis of Wayanad staged for autonomy and self-rule. The Adivasi Gotra Maha Sabha (AGMS) was built on the solidarity of Adivasis in pursuit of their autonomy and self-rule. AGMS reiterated the close link between land and Adivasi identity as a cultural assertion of self-rule and autonomy. The 2003 Muthanga incident in Wayanad was a protest by Adivasis who had been marginalised and exploited in production zones such as plantations and wetland cultivation for decades. The demand for a piece of land was an assertion of autonomy and a will to live independently on their own terms, within their cultural, social, economic, and political imaginations. In the words of C.K. Janu, the leader of the Muthanga struggle and President of AGMS, the “demand for land was the first step to materialise autonomy so that Adivasis do not have to depend on the exploitative labour system for their survival”. However, the demand for land materialised as ‘Adivasi colonies’ with stacked houses; sometimes even less than 5 cents[4] of land was allotted to the family. The Adivasis continued to depend on the plantation for their subsistence. However, rather than being structured by the homogenising global capital and its modes of accumulation in plantations, Adivasis became mobile. 
 
Mobility kept governance, control, and the operation of capital at arm’s length. It ensured that Adivasis did not bear the brunt of the unscrupulous extraction of capitalism on the plantations of Wayanad and Kodagu. The thesis examines Adivasi mobility as a form of dissent against the logic of capital, seeking to move away from oppression, extraction, and the sedentarisation of class politics within the permanent and casual labour force on plantations. The thesis delves into how the Jenukurubas and Yeravas of Nagarhole envision their territory and autonomy through reverse migration and by refusing to be controlled and exploited as bonded plantation labourers. This thesis documents Adivasis’ notions and acts of establishing autonomy within the contemporary plantation belt of South India through mobility. For Adivasis, mobility meant ‘moving on’ without being hegemonised by capital, hierarchical structures such as caste and racial division, or anything that controlled the community and its sociality. The thesis unfolds the phases of Adivasi mobility as different modes of engagement with the logic of capital in postcolonial times.
