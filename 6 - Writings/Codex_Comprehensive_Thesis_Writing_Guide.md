**Thesis:** Land Records Modernisation in Odisha: A Study of History, Policy and Implications  
**Researcher:** Dinesh Mahapatra  
**Discipline:** Development Studies  
**Field site:** Two villages in Nayagarh district, Odisha  
**Core empirical object:** Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme (DILRMP), Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, mutation, registration, RoR, cadastral maps, and village/parcel-level discrepancies

## How To Use This Guide

This guide is designed as a working manual for writing the thesis. It is not a literature review and not a substitute for chapter drafts. It is a map of what each chapter must do, what it must avoid, which references carry the argument, what evidence belongs where, and how the chapters should speak to one another.

The guiding principle is simple: each chapter must answer a specific institutional and empirical question. The thesis is not about digitisation in general. It is about the modernisation of a historically inherited land records system in Odisha, and about whether digital tools can resolve, reproduce, or intensify the gap between record, map, title, possession, and social claim.

The guide draws on four sources of context:

- The PhD proposal, especially the research questions, methodology, and problem statement.
- `PHD_LENS.md`, especially the relevance filters and chapter map.
- The Claude-generated chapter guide, treated as useful context but not as binding.
- Codex's chapter-specific bibliographic and structural recommendations.

The central advice throughout is to keep the thesis disciplined. Do not let it become a general thesis on neoliberalism, technology, development, or Odisha. Those are contexts. The object is land records modernisation, and the strongest original contribution is the movement from policy to village to parcel.

## The Thesis In One Sentence

India's land records were historically built as revenue instruments and remain presumptive rather than conclusive; DILRMP attempts to modernise this inherited system through digitisation, integration, mutation reform, cadastral mapping, and ULPIN, but in Odisha the gap between textual record, spatial map, legal title, social possession, and ground reality persists because it is rooted in historical land tenure, inheritance, tenancy, caste, gender, commons, and bureaucratic practice.

This sentence should guide every chapter. If a section does not help explain some part of this sentence, cut it or move it elsewhere.

---

## The Core Argument

The thesis should make five linked arguments.

First, Indian land records are historically misaligned with the contemporary uses now imposed on them. They were not created as guaranteed title documents. They emerged as fiscal, administrative, and revenue instruments. The postcolonial state has repeatedly used them for land reform, planning, welfare, credit, acquisition, and market exchange, but their legal status remains presumptive.

Second, DILRMP represents an important but limited reform. It improves access, speed, transparency, retrieval, integration, and administrative visibility. It does not automatically resolve deeper problems of outdated cadastral maps, pending mutation, informal partition, unrecorded tenancy, inheritance disputes, land-use conversion, encroachment, commons, and possession without title.

Third, Odisha is a particularly revealing case because its land system is historically heterogeneous. It carries the imprint of Bengal-zamindari, Madras-ryotwari, Central Provinces tenure, princely-state arrangements, post-independence land reforms, consolidation, tenancy restrictions, tribal land protections, and contemporary digital governance.

Fourth, the village is where the programme meets social reality. Households do not encounter DILRMP as an abstract policy. They encounter it through RoR copies, Bhulekh printouts, mutation applications, RI visits, Amin measurements, Tehsil delays, intermediaries, family partition disputes, welfare eligibility, and credit or sale requirements.

Fifth, the parcel is the decisive empirical scale. A state can report near-complete digitisation while particular parcels still show mismatch between RoR, Bhunaksha, paper record, mutation history, and ground possession. The parcel-level method is the thesis's most distinctive contribution.

## Research Questions Rewritten As Chapter Tasks

The proposal lists six research questions. For writing purposes, translate them into chapter tasks.

**RQ1: What is the progress of DILRMP in Odisha: motivation, design, and implementation?**  
Chapter 3 explains design and motivation at the national level; Chapter 4 explains Odisha implementation.

**RQ2: Are programme objectives and outcomes aligned? How do different social sections use or misuse updated RoRs?**  
Chapter 3 identifies official objectives; Chapters 5 and 6 test outcomes.

**RQ3: How does history of ownership and tenancy shape current ownership through inheritance, sale, parcelisation, and partition?**  
Chapter 4 gives historical background; Chapter 5 documents household-level continuity; Chapter 6 demonstrates parcel-level effects.

**RQ4: What is the source of disjuncture between records, cadastral maps, and actual possession/ownership?**  
Chapter 6 is the main answer, supported by Chapters 4 and 5.

**RQ5: Are recent modernisation efforts technocratic? Does computerisation resolve ground-level conflicts or restate them digitally?**  
Chapter 3 frames the technocratic question; Chapters 5 and 6 answer it empirically.

**RQ6: How far have alternatives to Survey and Settlement brought parity and uniformity?**  
Chapter 3 discusses integration and DILRMP design; Chapter 4 explains Odisha's Survey and Settlement framework; Chapter 6 tests whether parity exists at parcel scale.

## Thesis Architecture

The thesis should be written as a movement across scale.

**Chapter 1: Introduction**  
States the problem, theoretical frame, research questions, methodology, and contribution.

**Chapter 2: History and Evolution of Land Records**  
Explains how land records became what they are: revenue records, cadastral systems, deeds/title systems, presumptive/conclusive title, and Indian legal-institutional history.

**Chapter 3: Political Economy of Land Records Modernisation in India**  
Explains why modernisation arises now, how DILRMP is designed, how it links to conclusive titling, markets, welfare, DBT, and technocratic reform.

**Chapter 4: Evolution of Land Rights and Land Records in Odisha**  
Explains why Odisha is a specific and analytically important case, with its layered revenue history, statutes, revenue administration, Bhulekh/Bhunaksha architecture, and welfare linkages.

**Chapter 5: Village-Level Realities of Land Governance**  
Shows how households experience records, possession, mutation, bureaucracy, and welfare.

**Chapter 6: Parcel-Level Findings**  
Tests whether digital records correspond to physical land, using parcel-level comparison.

**Chapter 7: Conclusion**  
Synthesises what the thesis has established and explains its theoretical, methodological, and policy contribution.

# Chapter 1: Introduction

## What Chapter 1 Must Do

Chapter 1 is not a literature review in miniature. It is the chapter that tells the reader why the study exists, what exact problem it addresses, how it addresses that problem, and why the evidence matters.

It must accomplish seven tasks:

1. Establish the land records problem in India.
2. Explain why DILRMP matters.
3. Explain why Odisha matters.
4. State the research questions clearly.
5. Present the theoretical lenses without overloading the reader.
6. Explain the mixed-methods design.
7. State the contribution of the thesis.

## Opening Strategy

Open with the contradiction at the heart of the thesis.

India is moving toward a digital and eventually conclusive land-titling regime, but the underlying record system remains historically presumptive, socially uneven, and often misaligned with possession on the ground. Odisha has made strong digital progress under DILRMP, yet the persistence of old cadastral maps, incomplete resurvey, informal partition, unrecorded tenancy, and welfare dependence on RoR makes it an ideal site for examining whether modernisation resolves or merely digitises inherited ambiguity.

Avoid beginning with a generic statement like "land is an important resource." That is true but weak. Begin with the institutional contradiction.

## Suggested Section Structure

### 1.1 Background and Problem Statement

Introduce land records as instruments that now perform multiple roles:

- Revenue assessment.
- Ownership presumption.
- Mutation and transfer.
- Credit and collateral.
- Welfare eligibility.
- Land acquisition and compensation.
- Dispute evidence.
- Planning and administration.

Then state the problem: the same record is asked to perform functions it was not historically designed to perform.

### 1.2 DILRMP and the Modernisation Promise

Explain DILRMP's official objectives:

- Computerisation of land records.
- Digitisation of cadastral maps.
- Integration of textual and spatial data.
- Integration of registration and mutation.
- Computerisation of registration.
- Modern record rooms.
- Movement toward conclusive title.
- ULPIN / Bhu-Aadhaar.

Make clear that the thesis does not deny the administrative value of these reforms. It asks whether these reforms address the deeper disjuncture between record and ground.

### 1.3 The Odisha Case

Introduce Odisha as a strong case because:

- It has a complex land-revenue history.
- It inherited multiple tenure systems.
- It has significant rural and agrarian dependence.
- It has made visible progress in Bhulekh/Bhunaksha/DILRMP.
- It has substantial tenancy, landlessness, sharecropping, and commons issues.
- It uses land records in welfare schemes.
- Nayagarh offers village-level sites where historical ownership, welfare, and land records can be examined.

### 1.4 Research Questions

Use the six research questions from the proposal, but present them in polished form. After each question, add one sentence explaining how the thesis answers it.

### 1.5 Theoretical Framework

Keep this section disciplined. Do not write a full theoretical essay here. Introduce the four lenses:

**Institutional economics and property rights:** explains the standard argument that secure, transferable, enforceable property rights reduce transaction costs, improve tenure security, support credit, and enable markets.

**Agrarian political economy:** asks who owns, who possesses, who cultivates, who is excluded, and how class, caste, gender, tenancy, and landlessness shape access to land.

**Historical institutionalism:** explains how colonial and postcolonial land institutions persist, creating path-dependent constraints on contemporary reform.

**Commons/capability lens:** prevents the thesis from treating private alienable ownership as the only meaningful land relation; land also supports livelihood, security, freedom, and social membership.

### 1.6 Methodology

Present methodology as a multi-scalar design:

- Policy and document analysis.
- Legal and institutional analysis.
- State-level Odisha analysis.
- District-level contextualisation.
- Household survey.
- Semi-structured interviews.
- Revenue-office interviews.
- Bhulekh/Bhunaksha record extraction.
- Parcel-level physical measurement using Gunter's chain.
- Comparison between RoR, cadastral map, paper record, and ground possession.

Stress that the study is observational, not causal inference.

### 1.7 Contribution

State three contributions:

**Empirical contribution:** village and parcel-level evidence on DILRMP in Odisha.

**Methodological contribution:** combining household survey, institutional analysis, and parcel-level measurement.

**Theoretical contribution:** showing that digital modernisation cannot be evaluated only through access or dashboard progress; it must be evaluated through the relation between record, possession, and social power.

# Chapter 2: History and Evolution of Land Records

## What Chapter 2 Must Do

Chapter 2 must establish the historical and institutional foundations of the thesis. Its main job is to show how land records evolved from revenue, cadastral, and administrative instruments into documents now used for ownership, welfare, credit, markets, and state recognition.

The chapter should not become a full history of land in India. It should focus specifically on land records, survey, settlement, registration, title, and the legal-institutional status of records.

## Chapter 2 Central Argument

Indian land records are not failed title documents; they are historically successful revenue and administrative documents that are now being asked to function as title, welfare, credit, and market instruments. DILRMP inherits this contradiction. The programme's difficulty lies not simply in poor implementation but in the attempt to modernise a system whose underlying legal and historical basis remains presumptive.

## Suggested Section Structure

### 2.1 Land Records and Their Functions

Start conceptually. Define land records as institutional instruments that classify, measure, record, tax, transfer, and govern land.

Functions to discuss:

- Fiscal: identifying revenue liability.
- Cadastral: mapping parcels and boundaries.
- Juridical: offering presumptive evidence of rights.
- Administrative: enabling mutation and record maintenance.
- Economic: supporting sale, mortgage, credit, and land markets.
- Welfare: determining eligibility for schemes such as PM-Kisan and CM-Kisan.
- Planning: supporting acquisition, infrastructure, and development.

Key conceptual distinction: land records are not land rights themselves. They are state-produced representations of rights, possession, classification, and claims.

### 2.2 Evolution of Land Records and Titling Systems Globally

Keep this section comparative but concise. Its purpose is not to survey the whole world. Its purpose is to clarify institutional models.

Subsections:

**2.2.1 Pre-modern and early cadastral practices**  
Discuss land measurement, tax rolls, village records, terriers, Domesday-like records, and fiscal mapping.

**2.2.2 Deed registration**  
Explain how deed registration records transactions but does not guarantee title. This is crucial because India largely works through registered deeds plus revenue mutation, not guaranteed title.

**2.2.3 Title registration and Torrens system**  
Explain mirror, curtain, and indemnity principles. Use Niblack and Simpson. Show why conclusive title requires a very different institutional foundation.

**2.2.4 Contemporary land administration**  
Discuss cadastral systems, GIS, integrated land information systems, and the World Bank formalisation argument.

### 2.3 History of Land Records in India

Organise this section around institutional shifts.

**2.3.1 Pre-colonial records**  
Use Habib and Appu. Show that pre-colonial records were primarily fiscal and administrative. Avoid romantic claims about community harmony; focus on revenue, assessment, cultivation, and state extraction.

**2.3.2 Colonial settlements**  
This is the most important section. Explain Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, Mahalwari, survey, settlement, cadastral mapping, and record-of-rights. Use Baden-Powell, Rothermund, Guha, Stokes, Washbrook, and Appu.

The key point: colonial records fixed revenue responsibility and administratively recognised interests in land; they did not create a simple guaranteed ownership system.

**2.3.3 Post-independence land reform and records**  
Use Appu, Wadhwa, Planning Commission, and land reform literature. Show that land reform required accurate records, but record inaccuracy also weakened reform.

### 2.4 Legal and Institutional Infrastructure for Land Records in India

Explain the relationship among:

- Revenue records.
- Record of Rights.
- Cadastral maps.
- Registered deeds.
- Mutation.
- Courts.
- Presumptive title.
- Conclusive title.

Discuss:

- Transfer of Property Act, 1882.
- Registration Act, 1908.
- Indian Stamp Act, 1899.
- Indian Evidence Act, 1872.
- Limitation Act, 1963.
- Constitutional property-rights trajectory.
- Land acquisition laws.

The key argument: in India, ownership is established through a chain of documents, revenue records, possession, and judicial determination, not by a single guaranteed title certificate.

### 2.5 Genesis of Land Records Modernisation

This section should bridge to Chapter 3.

Trace:

- Concerns about outdated records and land reform implementation.
- Wadhwa Committee.
- SRA&ULR.
- Computerisation of Land Records.
- NLRMP 2008.
- DILRMP 2016.
- ULPIN and conclusive title push.

End by saying Chapter 3 examines the political economy of this transition.

# Chapter 3: Political Economy of Land Records Modernisation in India

## What Chapter 3 Must Do

Chapter 3 explains why land records modernisation takes the form it does in contemporary India. Chapter 2 explained the historical and institutional inheritance. Chapter 3 explains the policy project: DILRMP, conclusive title, market-making, welfare targeting, DBT, and technocratic reform.

The chapter must avoid becoming a descriptive policy report. It should be an argument about political economy.

## Chapter 3 Central Argument

DILRMP is not only an administrative reform. It is part of a broader post-1991 transformation in which land is made more legible for markets, welfare targeting, credit, acquisition, and governance. The programme promises transparency and reduced disputes, but its design works through inherited presumptive records, fragmented institutions, old cadastral maps, and unequal bureaucratic access. Therefore, the political economy question is not whether digitisation is efficient, but whose claims become visible, whose claims remain invisible, and which disputes are transferred into digital form.

## Suggested Section Structure

### 3.1 Land Governance Under Neoliberalism

This section explains the macro context. It should not become a general essay on neoliberalism. It should focus on land.

Key points:

- Post-1991 reforms changed the role of land in development.
- Land became increasingly central to infrastructure, industrialisation, real estate, mining, and financialisation.
- Secure and transferable titles are framed as necessary for land markets and investment.
- International institutions, especially the World Bank, shaped the policy language of land markets, tenure security, and formalisation.

Use Kohli, Bardhan, Patnaik, Levien, Deininger, World Bank, and land acquisition literature.

### 3.2 Land Records Modernisation and DILRMP

This is the institutional core of the chapter.

Explain DILRMP components:

- Computerisation of RoR.
- Digitisation of cadastral maps.
- Integration of textual and spatial data.
- Integration of registration and mutation.
- Computerisation of Sub-Registrar Offices.
- Modern record rooms.
- Survey/resurvey.
- ULPIN.
- Movement toward conclusive title.

Then analyse the design problem:

- RoR, cadastral maps, and registration systems were historically separate.
- Mutation does not automatically equal title.
- Old maps can be digitised without being corrected.
- Integration can connect inconsistent records.
- Dashboard progress can show completion without ground-level accuracy.

### 3.3 Conclusive Titling and the Presumptive Record Problem

This section should explain why conclusive titling is institutionally radical.

Discuss:

- Deed registration vs title registration.
- Presumptive title in India.
- Torrens principles.
- NITI Aayog Model Conclusive Land Titling Act.
- Title insurance alternatives.
- Risks of converting flawed presumptive records into conclusive titles.

Key question: what happens when a system with unresolved possession and record discrepancies moves toward state-guaranteed title?

### 3.4 State Welfare, DBT, and RoR As Eligibility Infrastructure

This is one of the thesis's original arguments. Land records are not just market instruments; they are welfare instruments.

Discuss:

- JAM architecture.
- PM-Kisan's reliance on landholding farmer families as per land records.
- KALIA/CM-Kisan as Odisha-specific welfare context.
- Land seeding, e-KYC, Aadhaar, NPCI, bank linkage.
- RoR as a gateway to inclusion and exclusion.

Make a careful argument: cash transfers may have real welfare value, but land-record-dependent schemes reproduce ownership bias. Tenants, sharecroppers, landless households, women without recorded title, and households with unresolved mutation can be excluded or forced into bureaucratic struggle.

### 3.5 Critical Reflections on Technocratic Reform

This section answers the proposal's technocracy question.

Use Ferguson, Li, Gupta, Scott, Winner, Benjamin, Saxena, and Bromley.

The key point: computerisation can make records accessible, searchable, and administratively efficient, but it cannot by itself adjudicate inheritance, possession, tenancy, commons, encroachment, or boundary disputes.

End the chapter by setting up Odisha: Chapter 4 will show how these national processes enter a specific state with a historically layered land regime.

# Chapter 4: Evolution of Land Rights and Land Records in Odisha

## What Chapter 4 Must Do

Chapter 4 must justify Odisha and prepare the reader for the villages. It is not background. It is the state-level bridge between national policy and fieldwork.

The chapter must answer:

- What makes Odisha's land records historically specific?
- How did post-independence reforms reshape land rights?
- How does Odisha's revenue administration actually work?
- What is the digital architecture: Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, e-registration, RCCMS, e-Pauti?
- How do welfare schemes depend on records?
- Why do the selected villages matter?

## Chapter 4 Central Argument

Odisha's land records system rests on a historically heterogeneous land-tenure structure inherited from multiple colonial and princely regimes, reshaped by post-independence reforms, and now overlaid by digital systems. Bhulekh and Bhunaksha present a unified digital interface, but underneath that interface are uneven histories of settlement, consolidation, tenancy, mutation, cadastral mapping, and social possession.

## Suggested Section Structure

### 4.1 Odisha's Historical Land-Tenure Inheritance

Discuss four historical inheritances:

**Coastal/northern Odisha:** Bengal-zamindari influence and Permanent Settlement-style arrangements.  
**Southern Odisha:** Madras-ryotwari inheritance, especially Ganjam and Koraput.  
**Western Odisha:** Central Provinces and malguzari/village tenure influence.  
**Garhjat/princely areas:** ex-princely states with varied settlement histories.

Do not over-describe political history. Focus on how these inheritances affected records, rights, revenue administration, and later reform.

### 4.2 Post-Independence Land Reforms and Statutory Framework

Discuss:

- Orissa Estates Abolition Act, 1951.
- Orissa Survey and Settlement Act, 1958.
- Orissa Land Reforms Act, 1960.
- Orissa Survey and Settlement Rules, 1962.
- Orissa Mutation Manual, 1962.
- Orissa Consolidation of Holdings and Prevention of Fragmentation Act, 1972.
- OSATIP Regulation, 1956.
- FRA 2006 and PESA 1996 where relevant.

The point is not to summarise each statute mechanically. Explain what each statute attempted to do and what kinds of record problems persisted.

### 4.3 Revenue Administration and Mutation in Odisha

Explain the chain:

- Villager / landholder.
- Revenue Inspector.
- Amin.
- Tahasildar.
- Sub-Collector.
- Collector.
- Revenue and Disaster Management Department.
- Sub-Registrar / IGR system.

Explain mutation:

- Sale deed registration.
- OLR Form No. 3 where relevant.
- Transmission to Tahasil.
- RI inquiry.
- Amin measurement/map correction.
- Tahasildar order.
- Record keeper update.
- RoR generation.
- Bhulekh update.

This section should directly prepare Chapter 5's revenue-office encounters and Chapter 6's mutation-chain analysis.

### 4.4 Digital Architecture in Odisha

Explain:

- Bhulekh: textual RoR.
- Bhunaksha: spatial/cadastral map.
- e-Pauti: land revenue payment.
- e-Registration/IGR.
- RCCMS: revenue court cases.
- Modern Record Rooms.
- DILRMP MIS.
- ULPIN / Smart RoR where relevant.

Discuss the difference between access and accuracy. Odisha may show strong digital progress, but digitisation of a cadastral map does not mean resurveyed accuracy.

### 4.5 Land Records and Welfare in Odisha

Discuss:

- PM-Kisan.
- KALIA.
- CM-Kisan.
- Subhadra where relevant.
- Farmer registration.
- Land seeding.
- Aadhaar/e-KYC/NPCI.

Frame RoR as an administrative document that has acquired welfare consequences.

### 4.6 Nayagarh and Field-Site Logic

This is where the chapter must justify the study sites.

Include:

- District-level land profile.
- Agriculture and irrigation.
- Landholding pattern.
- Caste/community composition.
- Revenue administration.
- DILRMP status if district data are available.
- Why the two villages were chosen.

Avoid overclaiming that the villages represent all Odisha. Instead, state they are analytically chosen to examine specific mechanisms: inheritance, partition, record-possession mismatch, welfare dependence, and parcel-level discrepancy.

### 4.7 Conclusion

End by saying: the next two chapters move from state architecture to lived and measured reality. Chapter 5 studies households; Chapter 6 studies parcels.

# Chapter 5: Village-Level Realities of Land Governance

## What Chapter 5 Must Do

Chapter 5 is the household and village chapter. It shows how land records are used, avoided, trusted, disputed, corrected, manipulated, and required in everyday life.

This chapter must avoid two mistakes:

- It must not become a general village monograph.
- It must not become a list of anecdotes.

It should organise field evidence around the thesis problem: the relation between record, possession, ownership, bureaucracy, and welfare.

## Chapter 5 Central Argument

At the village level, land records modernisation produces differentiated outcomes. Households with clear ownership, updated records, literacy, resources, and bureaucratic access benefit most. Households with inherited but unmutated land, informal partitions, tenancy, sharecropping, commons dependence, women's claims, or unresolved possession disputes experience digital records as partial, confusing, exclusionary, or bureaucratically demanding. Digital access improves visibility, but not all households can convert visibility into security.

# Chapter 6: Parcel-Level Findings

## What Chapter 6 Must Do

Chapter 6 is the most distinctive empirical chapter. It moves from household experience to parcel-level verification.

The chapter must answer: do Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, paper records, mutation history, and ground possession match?

This is where the thesis becomes more than a policy critique. It tests the record against the land.

## Chapter 6 Central Argument

Parcel-level comparison shows that land records modernisation improves digital access to records but does not automatically produce correspondence between textual record, spatial map, and actual possession. Discrepancies arise from inheritance, informal partition, delayed mutation, tenancy invisibility, encroachment, outdated cadastral maps, land-use conversion, and boundary change. These discrepancies are institutionally and socially patterned, not merely technical errors.

# Chapter 7: Conclusion

## What Chapter 7 Must Do

The conclusion should not merely summarise. It should state what the thesis has established.

The reader should finish Chapter 7 with a clear sense of:

- What is now known about DILRMP in Odisha.
- What the village evidence shows.
- What the parcel evidence proves or suggests.
- What the thesis contributes to theory, method, and policy.
- What limits remain.

## Chapter 7 Central Argument

Land records modernisation in Odisha has improved access, visibility, and administrative efficiency, but it has not resolved the deeper institutional disjuncture between record, map, title, and possession. DILRMP modernises an inherited presumptive and revenue-oriented record system through digital tools, but the core problems remain embedded in history, social relations, tenancy, inheritance, partition, commons, and bureaucratic discretion.

## Suggested Section Structure

### 7.1 Recap of Research Objectives and Questions

Answer each research question directly. Do not summarise chapters chronologically.

For each question:

- Restate the question.
- Give the answer in one paragraph.
- Mention where evidence came from.

### 7.2 Synthesis of Key Findings

Organise by findings:

**Finding 1: Odisha's digital progress is real but uneven.**  
Bhulekh and Bhunaksha improve access, but access is not accuracy.

**Finding 2: RoR remains presumptive but has expanded functions.**  
It now serves welfare, credit, sale, acquisition, and bureaucratic verification.

**Finding 3: Record-ground disjuncture is institutional, not accidental.**  
It arises from old maps, incomplete mutation, informal possession, tenancy, inheritance, and social relations.

**Finding 4: Digital systems reproduce unequal capacity.**  
Households with clearer records and better access benefit more.

**Finding 5: Welfare schemes make land records more consequential.**  
PM-Kisan, CM-Kisan, and KALIA turn records into inclusion/exclusion infrastructure.

**Finding 6: Parcel-level comparison reveals what dashboards cannot.**  
Digital progress indicators cannot detect parcel-level mismatch.

### 7.3 Implications for Policy and Governance

Recommendations should follow evidence.

Possible implications:

- Digitisation should not substitute for resurvey and settlement.
- RoR-Bhunaksha integration must be ground-verified.
- Mutation processes must address inheritance and informal partition.
- Welfare schemes need pathways for tenants/sharecroppers.
- Women's land claims need proactive mutation support.
- Commons and government land require village-level verification.
- DILRMP evaluation should include parcel-level audits.
- Revenue staff capacity and accountability matter as much as software.

### 7.4 Theoretical Contribution

State contributions to:

**Institutional economics:** land records reduce transaction costs only when records are accurate, accessible, and socially enforceable.

**Agrarian political economy:** land records reproduce caste, class, gender, tenancy, and possession relations.

**Historical institutionalism:** Odisha demonstrates path dependence in land administration.

**Digital governance:** digitisation relocates conflict; it does not automatically resolve it.

### 7.5 Methodological Contribution

Emphasise the multi-scalar method:

- Policy documents.
- Laws and administrative rules.
- State-level DILRMP progress.
- Village household evidence.
- Revenue-office interactions.
- Bhulekh/Bhunaksha comparison.
- Physical parcel measurement.

State the core methodological lesson: a land-record reform cannot be evaluated only at the dashboard level; it must be tested at the parcel level.

### 7.6 Limitations

Be clear:

- Two villages only.
- Nayagarh only.
- Purposive parcel sample.
- Gunter's chain measurement is not official resurvey.
- Some records may be incomplete.
- Sensitive disputes may limit disclosure.
- Findings are not statistically generalisable to all Odisha.

Then state the strength: the study offers institutional diagnosis rather than statewide causal inference.

### 7.7 Future Research

Suggest:

- Comparative districts within Odisha.
- Scheduled Area/FRA-focused land records.
- Gender and inheritance after digitisation.
- Longitudinal tracking of parcels.
- ULPIN/Bhu-Aadhaar impact.
- Revenue bureaucracy ethnography.
- Welfare exclusion through land-record errors.
- Comparison with Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Maharashtra.

### 7.8 Final Reflection

Return to the core contradiction:

The land record is no longer only a revenue document. It is a document of ownership presumption, welfare eligibility, credit access, market exchange, acquisition compensation, and state recognition. When the record does not match possession, digitisation does not remove ambiguity; it gives ambiguity a more official form.

Possible final sentence:

> The central lesson of this thesis is that land records modernisation cannot succeed by making old records digital; it must first confront the social, historical, and institutional reasons why those records diverged from the land.

Most paragraphs should do one of five things:

- State an argument.
- Explain a mechanism.

Use citations to support claims, not to replace argument.

DILRMP inherits this logic, but its implementation in Odisha shows that lower information costs do not automatically resolve possession disputes or inheritance ambiguity.

# Viva Preparation Through The Thesis

The thesis should prepare answers to these likely questions.

## Why Odisha?

Because Odisha combines historical land-tenure heterogeneity, significant agrarian dependence, strong formal progress in land-record digitisation, unresolved survey/settlement problems, tenancy and sharecropping, welfare schemes linked to land records, and village-level conditions where record-ground mismatch can be studied concretely.

## Why Nayagarh?

Because the selected villages allow examination of inheritance, partition, possession, mutation, welfare use of RoR, and parcel-level discrepancy in a manageable but analytically meaningful field setting. The claim is not statistical representativeness; it is institutional diagnosis.

## Why two villages?

Because the study is not a statewide survey. It is a mixed-methods institutional and field study that combines household evidence with parcel-level verification. Two villages allow depth, comparison, and measurement.

## Why 15 parcels per village?

Because the parcel study is purposive and diagnostic. It is designed to identify types and sources of discrepancy, not estimate statewide rates.

## Why Gunter's chain?

Because it allows ground-level physical verification of parcel extent and boundary claims in a field setting. It is not a legal resurvey; it is an empirical check against digital and paper records.

## What is original?

The original contribution is the combination of:

- Odisha-specific DILRMP analysis.
- Village-level household evidence.
- RoR-welfare linkage.
- Bhulekh/Bhunaksha-ground comparison.
- Parcel-level discrepancy typology.
- Political-economic interpretation of digital land records.

## Are you anti-digitisation?

No. The thesis recognises the administrative benefits of digitisation. It argues that digitisation cannot substitute for resolving underlying record-ground discrepancies.

## Can your findings be generalised?

Not statistically. They can be analytically generalised as mechanisms: inheritance delays, informal partition, tenancy invisibility, map-record mismatch, and welfare exclusion through records are not unique to the villages, though their incidence varies.

## Chapter 1 Extended Manual

### The Real Job Of Chapter 1

Chapter 1 must make the reader believe that the thesis is necessary. It should not simply announce a topic. It should reveal a contradiction that requires investigation.

The contradiction is this: land records in India remain presumptive, historically revenue-oriented, and often misaligned with possession, yet the state increasingly uses them as if they were reliable instruments for title, welfare, credit, market transactions, acquisition, and digital governance. Odisha is a strong case because it combines formal digital progress with unresolved historical and ground-level complexities.

The chapter should move from problem to case to method to contribution. It should not begin with theory. Theory enters after the reader understands the empirical puzzle.

Use this as a model, not as fixed prose:

> In contemporary India, a Record of Rights is no longer only a revenue document. Yet the same record remains legally presumptive and historically rooted in a revenue administration that was never designed to guarantee title. This thesis begins from that institutional tension. ## Chapter 2 Extended Manual

### The Real Job Of Chapter 2

Chapter 2 must explain how the institutional object called "land records" came to have its current contradictions. It should show that the present problem is historically produced.

The chapter is not a general history of land. It is a history of recording, surveying, registering, mutating, and evidencing land rights.

### Chapter 2 Model Opening

> The contemporary debate on land records modernisation in India often begins with digitisation: whether RoRs have been computerised, cadastral maps scanned, registration offices networked, and mutations integrated. This chapter begins earlier. It argues that the difficulty of modernisation lies not only in incomplete digitisation but in the historical character of the records being digitised. Indian land records emerged primarily as fiscal and administrative instruments, were reshaped through colonial survey and settlement, and entered the postcolonial period as presumptive records of rights rather than guaranteed titles. The chapter traces this history to show why DILRMP inherits a structural disjuncture between record, title, map, and possession.

### Section 2.1: Land Records And Their Functions

Start with a definitional paragraph:

> A land record is a state-recognised representation of claims, responsibilities, classification, and spatial information relating to land. It is not identical to land rights themselves. It records, evidences, classifies, and administers rights, but the relationship between record and right varies across legal systems.

Then create a functional typology:

- Fiscal: identifies who pays revenue and on what basis.
- Spatial: represents parcels, boundaries, and area.
- Juridical: provides evidence in disputes.
- Transactional: supports sale, mortgage, inheritance, and mutation.
- Administrative: helps the state classify land and update transfers.
- Welfare: determines eligibility for schemes.
- Developmental: supports acquisition, compensation, infrastructure, and investment.

The important move is to show that functions accumulated over time. A record built for one function now carries many functions.

### Section 2.2: Global Evolution Without Losing Focus

Do not attempt a universal history. Use global material only to explain institutional types:

- Pre-modern fiscal records.
- Cadastres.
- Deed registration.
- Title registration/Torrens.
- Modern land administration.

Always relate back to India: India has modernised registration and records without fully moving from presumptive evidence to guaranteed title.

### Section 2.3: India Historical Narrative

Divide Indian history into three parts.

**Pre-colonial:** Use Habib and Appu. Explain that land revenue systems recognised cultivation, assessment, and revenue rights. Do not claim that modern ownership existed in the same form.

**Colonial:** This is the core. Explain that the British systematised survey, settlement, revenue assessment, and juridical categories. Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, and Mahalwari differed, but all connected land, revenue, and administrative recognition.

**Postcolonial:** Land reforms required accurate records. Ceiling laws, tenancy reform, abolition of intermediaries, consolidation, and redistribution all depended on records. But the records themselves remained incomplete, outdated, or socially contested.

### Section 2.4: Legal Infrastructure

Explain how land ownership is evidenced in India:

- Sale deed.
- Registration.
- Mutation.
- RoR.
- Cadastral map.
- Tax/rent receipts.
- Possession.
- Court decree.
- Inheritance documents.

Then explain the problem: registration records a transaction; mutation updates revenue records; RoR provides presumptive evidence; the cadastral map shows spatial location. None alone functions as guaranteed title.

### Section 2.5: From Records To Modernisation

Write this as a bridge. Outdated records weakened land reform. The state responded through SRA&ULR, CLR, NLRMP, and DILRMP. The modernisation sequence increasingly shifted from updating records to integrating records and eventually imagining conclusive title.

End with: Chapter 3 examines this shift not as a technical chronology but as a political-economic project.

### Chapter 2 Common Mistakes

Avoid letting Scott/Foucault dominate if not directly tied to records; writing broad agrarian history without records; overstating that colonialism "created property" in a simple way; treating Mughal records as modern property records; treating DILRMP as merely administrative without showing historical inheritance.

### Chapter 2 Completion Criteria

Chapter 2 is ready when it explains why Indian records are presumptive, why that matters for conclusive titling, why digitising old records is not the same as resolving land rights, and how Chapter 3 follows naturally.

## Chapter 3 Extended Manual

### The Real Job Of Chapter 3

Chapter 3 must explain the political economy of the modernisation project. It is where the thesis stops asking "what are land records?" and starts asking "why modernise them now, in this way, and with what consequences?"

### Chapter 3 Model Opening

> Land records modernisation in India is usually presented as an administrative reform: a programme to computerise records, digitise maps, integrate registration and mutation, reduce disputes, and improve service delivery. This chapter reads it differently. It argues that DILRMP belongs to a wider post-1991 transformation in which land is made more legible for markets, welfare targeting, credit, acquisition, and state administration. The programme's technical design therefore carries political-economic consequences. By seeking to integrate RoR, cadastral maps, registration, mutation, and parcel identification, DILRMP attempts to convert a fragmented presumptive record system into an infrastructure for marketability, welfare eligibility, and eventual conclusive title.

### Section 3.1: Land Governance Under Neoliberalism

This section should answer: what changed in the policy environment after liberalisation?

Write around mechanisms:

- Land becomes important for infrastructure and industrialisation.
- Land markets become policy objects.
- Title security becomes linked to investment and credit.
- International institutions promote formalisation.
- Land acquisition conflicts intensify the need for clear records.
- Rural distress and welfare targeting create another use for land records.

Do not define neoliberalism abstractly for pages. Define it through land.

### Section 3.2: DILRMP Design

This section should be document-heavy. Use official documents to explain what the programme says it will do. Then interpret what each component assumes.

Component: digitisation of cadastral maps.  
Assumption: the existing map is a valid spatial base.  
Problem: if the map is outdated, digitisation reproduces old boundaries.

Component: automatic mutation after registration.  
Assumption: land transfers pass through registered transactions.  
Problem: inheritance, informal partition, tenancy, and encroachment may not.

Component: integration of RoR and Bhunaksha.  
Assumption: textual and spatial records correspond.  
Problem: if they do not, integration reveals mismatch but does not adjudicate it.

### Section 3.3: Conclusive Titling

Treat this as the chapter's legal-institutional high point.

Explain:

- What is presumptive title?
- What is conclusive title?
- What does the NITI model propose?
- What prerequisites are needed?
- What risks arise if old records become conclusive?

Key sentence:

> The policy ambition is not simply to make records digital; it is to move toward a system in which the state can guarantee title. This is a qualitatively different legal project.

### Section 3.4: Welfare And DBT

Make the argument in steps:

1. DBT requires beneficiary identification.
2. PM-Kisan defines beneficiaries through landholding farmer families.
3. Landholding is identified through state land records.
4. Therefore, RoR becomes welfare infrastructure.
5. Any record error can become welfare exclusion.
6. Tenants and sharecroppers are structurally disadvantaged in ownership-based schemes.

Then bring Odisha: KALIA was more inclusive in recognising cultivators and landless agricultural households; CM-Kisan documents need to be examined for land seeding and verification; welfare schemes bring land records into everyday household politics.

### Section 3.5: Technocratic Reform

Distinguish between problems technology can address and problems technology cannot address alone.

Technology can improve access, retrieval, data sharing, tracking, transparency, and speed.

Technology cannot alone adjudicate inheritance disputes, possession conflicts, informal tenancy, boundary disputes, commons, gendered exclusion, encroachment, or historical survey errors.

### Chapter 3 Common Mistakes

Avoid calling everything neoliberal without showing how; treating DILRMP as a simple government programme; ignoring official documents; discussing DBT without showing the RoR link; overstating that welfare schemes are only electoral; forgetting that modernisation has real benefits.

### Chapter 3 Completion Criteria

Chapter 3 is ready when it explains why land records modernisation matters after 1991, what DILRMP actually does, why conclusive title is a major shift, how land records became welfare infrastructure, and why technocratic reform has limits.

## Chapter 4 Extended Manual

### The Real Job Of Chapter 4

Chapter 4 must make Odisha intellectually necessary. It should answer the examiner's likely question: why should we read Odisha as more than a local case?

The answer is not "Odisha is important." The answer is that Odisha exposes the contradiction between digital uniformity and historical heterogeneity.

### Chapter 4 Model Opening

> Odisha's land records system cannot be understood as a single administrative inheritance. The state was formed through the integration of territories governed under different revenue regimes, including Bengal-influenced zamindari areas, Madras ryotwari regions, Central Provinces territories, and princely states. Post-independence reforms attempted to standardise this field through estate abolition, tenancy reform, survey and settlement, consolidation, and restrictions on tribal land alienation. DILRMP now overlays this complex history with a digital architecture of Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, e-registration, mutation tracking, and parcel identification. This chapter argues that Odisha's digital land records system is unified at the portal level but historically uneven at the level of records, rights, and possession.

### Section 4.1: Historical Tenure

Use a regional structure but keep it analytic. For each region, answer: what was the revenue regime, who was recognised as the revenue-paying subject, what kind of records did the system produce, and what long-term implication might this have for present records?

### Section 4.2: Post-Independence Reform

For each law, use the same structure:

- What problem did it address?
- What did it change legally?
- What record-keeping process did it require?
- What implementation problem persisted?
- How does this matter for DILRMP?

Example: the Orissa Land Reforms Act aimed to regulate tenancy, ceilings, and occupancy rights. But if tenancy remains informal and unrecorded, a digitised RoR cannot show actual cultivation.

### Section 4.3: Revenue Administration

Write this from the citizen's perspective: if a villager needs mutation, where do they go? Who handles the file? Who visits the field? Who updates the map? Who signs the order? When does Bhulekh change?

This prepares the reader for Chapter 5.

### Section 4.4: Digital Architecture

Treat each portal as an institution, not just a website. For each system, ask what data it holds, who maintains it, how the citizen uses it, what it does not show, and how it relates to other systems.

Bhulekh may show the textual RoR, but it cannot by itself prove physical possession. Bhunaksha may show the parcel polygon, but it may not reflect current boundary changes. e-registration records transactions, but unregistered inheritance partitions remain outside it.

### Section 4.5: Welfare Linkage

Show how Odisha welfare schemes require identification: who is a farmer, who is a landholder, who is a cultivator, how is landholding verified, and what happens when the land record is old?

### Section 4.6: Nayagarh

Use district census material, district statistical handbooks, agricultural statistics, revenue maps if available, local administrative data, and field selection logic. Do not overload. The point is to explain why the two villages are suitable for examining the thesis questions.

### Chapter 4 Common Mistakes

Avoid writing "background on Odisha"; including all Odisha history; ignoring Nayagarh until the end; treating Bhulekh and Bhunaksha as neutral websites; failing to connect laws to records.

### Chapter 4 Completion Criteria

Chapter 4 is ready when it answers why Odisha, what historical layers shape its records, how revenue administration works, what the digital architecture is, and how this sets up the villages.

## Chapter 5 Extended Manual

### The Real Job Of Chapter 5

Chapter 5 is where the thesis becomes grounded. It should make the reader feel that the argument has entered village life.

The chapter must show how land records affect households differently. The key word is differentiation.

### Chapter 5 Model Opening

# Model Analytical Paragraphs

## Model Paragraph: Record vs Possession

> The mismatch between record and possession in Village 1 was most visible in inherited parcels. In several households, the RoR continued to show the name of a deceased father or grandfather, while possession had been divided among sons through informal family arrangement. This did not prevent cultivation because the village recognised the division. However, the mismatch became consequential when households attempted mutation, sale, or welfare verification. The record therefore did not fail in everyday cultivation but failed at moments of formal state interaction.

## Model Paragraph: Digitisation And Access

> Bhulekh has changed the accessibility of land records. Households no longer need to visit the Tahasil office merely to obtain a copy of the RoR; kiosk operators and digitally literate younger men can access records online. But this improvement in retrieval should not be confused with correction. When the record is wrong, the household still enters the older bureaucratic chain of application, inquiry, measurement, hearing, and order. Digitisation has simplified access to the record but not necessarily access to record correction.

## Model Paragraph: Welfare Link

> PM-Kisan demonstrates the expanded role of land records in welfare governance. Because eligibility is tied to landholding as recorded by the state, the RoR becomes more than an agrarian document. It becomes a welfare filter. Households with clear and updated records can convert ownership into cash transfer eligibility. Tenants, sharecroppers, and heirs whose names have not been mutated may cultivate land but remain weakly visible to the scheme. The land record thus mediates not only property but welfare.

## Model Paragraph: Parcel Discrepancy

> Parcel V2-P09 shows why cadastral digitisation cannot be equated with ground accuracy. The Bhulekh RoR records the parcel as agricultural land under the name of the household head's father. Bhunaksha shows the plot boundary as part of an older undivided block. On the ground, however, the parcel has been informally divided among three brothers, with a homestead structure occupying one portion. The discrepancy is not a simple data-entry error. It is the result of inheritance, informal partition, land-use change, and absence of formal mutation. Digitisation preserves the old record but does not resolve the social history that altered the parcel.

# Final Writing Advice

The thesis will be strongest if it stays close to documents and land. The temptation will be to over-theorise. Resist that. Your best evidence is concrete:

- A RoR that names an ancestor.
- A Bhunaksha plot whose boundary does not match the bund.
- A household cultivating land it does not own on paper.
- A woman excluded from mutation.
- A tenant excluded from PM-Kisan.
- A mutation file delayed.
- A parcel whose measured area differs from the recorded area.
- A villager who trusts the old patta more than the printout.

The theory should help explain these facts. It should not float above them.

The thesis should leave the reader with one lasting idea: land records modernisation is not merely a technical matter of putting records online. It is an institutional encounter between history, law, bureaucracy, social power, and land itself.
