## 2.1 Land Records and Their Functions (~1,800–2,200 words)

### Section Goal & Core Argument
Establish that land records are not neutral mirrors of ground reality, but active legal-administrative technologies of state power. Unsettle the technocratic definition by demonstrating that records perform four distinct, often conflicting functions: fiscal, juridical, economic, and governmental.

---

### Paragraph-by-Paragraph Writing Blueprint

#### §2.1.0 Opening Synthesis (2 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 1**: Define land records beyond technical descriptions (cadastre + register). Frame them as state-mediated information architectures where state building, capital accumulation, and social struggles intersect. Cite Scott (1998) and Zevenbergen (2002).
* **Paragraph 2**: Deploy Karl Polanyi’s (1944) *fictitious commodity* concept. Explain that land is nature, not a produced commodity; land records are the primary administrative technology used to construct and maintain the fiction of land as a market commodity. Introduce the four-function typology (fiscal, juridical, economic, governmental).

#### §2.1.1 The Fiscal and Governmental Functions: Legibility and State Power (4 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 3**: *The Fiscal Imperative*. Trace the origin of records to tax extraction. Use Scott’s (1998) *Seeing Like a State*—explain pre-modern customary illegibility and the necessity of state administrative simplifications. Use Scott's metaphor of the cadastral map as a 'still photograph of a river'.
* **Paragraph 4**: *Constitutive Legibility*. Argue that legibility is not merely descriptive but constitutive. Backed by state coercion, records remake reality. Introduce Foucault’s *governmentality* (Burchell et al., 1991)—records as population-management databases (taxing, policing, welfare targeting). Link briefly to DILRMP (*Bhulekh* and *Bhunaksha*).
* **Paragraph 5**: *Selective Legibility*. Cite Sánchez-Talanquer (2020) on the 'one-eyed state'—landholders seek registration for security but manipulate valuations to evade tax. 
* **Paragraph 6**: *Strategic Citizen Deployment*. Cite Ferree, Honig, and Lust (2023)—legibility is not just top-down imposition; citizens deploy records strategically across local administrative arenas to defend claims.

#### §2.1.2 The Juridical and Economic Functions: Property Rights and Capital Accumulation (4 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 7**: *Classical Political Economy*. Examine the juridical function (legal definition of rights). Engage Adam Smith (1776, Book III) and John Locke (1689)—secure, alienable, individual property as the precondition for agricultural improvement and investment.
* **Paragraph 8**: *New Institutional Economics (NIE)*. Trace the NIE reformulation: Coase (1960) on positive transaction costs, Demsetz (1967) on the evolutionary emergence of private property, North (1990) on formal institutions reducing economic uncertainty.
* **Paragraph 9**: *The International Development Orthodoxy*. Discuss Hernando de Soto’s (2000) *The Mystery of Capital*—informal holdings as 'dead capital' unlocked by formal titling. Connect to World Bank land policy (Deininger & Feder, 1998, 2009; Deininger, 2003).
* **Paragraph 10**: *The Agrarian Political Economy Rebuttal*. Challenge the NIE consensus using Karl Marx (1867, Part VIII) and David Harvey (2003). Argue that titling is an instrument of primitive accumulation and 'accumulation by dispossession', enclosing the commons and converting land into a financial asset for corporate capital (Levien, 2018).

#### §2.1.3 The Property Fiction: Commodification, Access, and the Bundle of Rights (3 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 11**: *Bundle of Rights*. Unpack liberal institutionalism's 'bundle of rights' (Schlager & Ostrom, 1992)—disaggregating ownership into access, withdrawal, management, exclusion, alienation.
* **Paragraph 12**: *Right to Benefit vs. Ability to Benefit*. Contrast property rights with Jesse Ribot and Nancy Lee Peluso’s (2003) *Theory of Access*. Explain that legal title is a *right to benefit*, whereas *access* is the real *ability to benefit* (mediated by capital, technology, caste, gender, and political patronage).
* **Paragraph 13**: *Negotiated Claims*. Cite Sara Berry (1993)—registration does not eliminate contestation; it shifts contestation into a unequal administrative arena favoring literate elites.

#### §2.1.4 Legal Distinctions and Institutional Limits (3 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 14**: *Deeds vs. Titles*. Distinguish Deeds Registration (recording transactions, presumptive status) from Title Registration / Torrens System (state-guaranteed title; mirror, curtain, and indemnity principles) (Merryman, 1985; Larsson, 1991; Wadhwa, 1989, 2002).
* **Paragraph 15**: *Presumptive Status of Indian Records*. Explain why Indian revenue entries (*RoRs*) under the Registration Act 1908 provide presumptive proof, not state-guaranteed ownership (Rothermund, 1971).
* **Paragraph 16**: *The Technocratic Trap*. Show that attempting conclusive titling without resolving ground-level errors creates an 'illegibly legible' system (Pratyush, 2023)—digitising analogue inaccuracy. Conclude §2.1 and transition to §2.2.

## 2.2 Evolution of Land Records and Titling Systems (Global) (~2,200–2,800 words)

### Section Goal & Core Argument
Reject the linear, teleological narrative of cadastral progress (informal $\rightarrow$ deed $\rightarrow$ digital Torrens title). Demonstrate that global shifts in land records were driven by state formation, fiscal crises, imperial expansion, and capitalist accumulation.

---

### Paragraph-by-Paragraph Writing Blueprint

#### §2.2.1 Pre-colonial Land Records and Practices (3 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 1**: Reject the linear progression narrative (Ting & Williamson, 1999) using critical historiography (Kain & Baigent, 1992; Tan, 1999). Establish that pre-modern records were fiscal/tributary, not private property titles.
* **Paragraph 2**: Comparative European and Asian examples:
  * Norman England: *Domesday Book* (1086)—inventory of royal feudal dues, no parcel maps.
  * Continental Europe: French medieval *terriers* and 18th-century *plans parcellaires*.
  * Ottoman Empire: *Tahrir defterleri* (15th–17th c.) for assigning *timars* (İnalcık, 1994).
  * Ming China: *Yu lin tu* ('fish-scale' registers) for grain taxation.
* **Paragraph 3**: Synthesise pre-colonial record features: (1) taxation without titling, (2) recognition of overlapping/plural rights, (3) absence of spatial cadastres.

#### §2.2.2 Colonial Approaches to Land Records (4 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 4**: *The Napoleonic Watershed*. Detail the French *cadastre parcellaire* (1807)—the founding template of the modern parcel-based fiscal-juridical cadastre (Kain & Baigent, 1992).
* **Paragraph 5**: *Cartographic Imperialism*. Explain how scientific survey was repurposed for imperial conquest (Edney, 1997; Home, 2006). Detail the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India (1802) as territorial grid-mapping.
* **Paragraph 6**: *Colonial Objectification*. Use Bernard Cohn’s (1996) 'survey modality'—colonial administrators translating fluid customary tenures into rigid European legal categories ('landlord', 'tenant', 'wasteland'), objectifying caste and property (Dirks, 2001; Bhandar, 2018).
* **Paragraph 7**: *Enclosure of the Commons*. Show how the binary distinction between cultivated private land and 'state wasteland' enabled Crown land appropriation in Egypt and settler colonies (Mitchell, 2002; Bhandar, 2018).

#### §2.2.3 Post-colonial Developments (3 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 8**: *Evolutionary Theory of Land Rights (ETLR)*. Outline post-WWII development orthodoxy (Feder & Feeny, 1991; Feder & Nishio, 1998)—claiming population growth and market expansion naturally require individual formal titles.
* **Paragraph 9**: *The Sub-Saharan Africa Critique*. Mobilise Jean-Philippe Platteau (1996)—titling eroded customary flexibility, dispossessed women, and failed to increase credit access.
* **Paragraph 10**: *Security without Title & Institutional Failure*. Cite Sjaastad and Bromley (1997, 2009) and Bromley (2009)—formal title does not cause tenure security; insecurity stems from structural poverty and arbitrary state power.

#### §2.2.4 Contemporary and Neoliberal Approaches: A Brief Introduction (3 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 11**: *De Soto and the Washington Consensus*. Outline the late 1990s neoliberal turn (de Soto, 2000; Deininger, 2003; World Bank, 2007).
* **Paragraph 12**: *Key Features of Neoliberal Titling*: (1) Shift from revenue/redistribution to market facilitation (Nayak, 2013), (2) Fit-for-Purpose digital infrastructure (Enemark et al., 2014).
* **Paragraph 13**: *Accumulation by Dispossession via Digital Legibility*. Synthesise critical views (Harvey, 2003; Borras & Franco, 2010)—digital legibility standardises land for corporate acquisition while erasing customary claims. Transition to §2.3.

## 2.3 History of Land Records in India (~6,000–7,500 words)

### Section Goal & Core Argument
Reconstruct Indian land records as an institutional **palimpsest**: Mughal tributary records $\rightarrow$ British colonial property settlements $\rightarrow$ Post-independence land reforms $\rightarrow$ Neoliberal digital e-governance. Prove that British colonial settlements failed to generate a genuine capitalist agrarian transition, leaving an inherited regime of presumptive revenue records.

---

### Paragraph-by-Paragraph Writing Blueprint

#### §2.3.1 Pre-colonial Record-Keeping: The Fiscal-Tributary Architecture (5 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 1**: Debunk the colonial myth that pre-British India lacked records. Frame pre-colonial records within a *tributary mode of production* (Habib, 1999).
* **Paragraph 2**: *The Mughal Zabt-Dahsala (1580)*. Detail Raja Todar Mal’s 10-year assessment under Akbar. Land measurement (*tanab* rod) and 4-fold classification (*polaj, parauti, chachar, banjar*). Revenue rates (*dastur-ul-amal*) (Habib, 1999; Moosvi, 1987).
* **Paragraph 3**: *Administrative Hierarchy*. Define the roles of the *Patwari* (village accountant, *khasra* and *khatauni* registers), *Qanungo* (pargana record keeper), and *Zamindar* (revenue collector holding *haqq-i zamindari*, not European landlord) (Habib, 1999; Bhattacharya, 2021).
* **Paragraph 4**: *Maratha/Deccan Inheritances*. Discuss Maratha *kird* (day-books) and *miras* (hereditary occupancy registers for *mirasdars* vs *uparis*) (Guha, 1995; Fukazawa, 1991). Note relevance to Western Odisha.
* **Paragraph 5**: *Pre-colonial Synthesis*: (1) Fiscal primacy, (2) Layered interests (*haqq*), (3) Unassigned village commons (*gochar*).

#### §2.3.2 Colonial Settlements and Land Administration: The Failed Capitalist Installation (8 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 6**: *Intellectual Foundations*. Deploy Ranajit Guha’s (1963) *A Rule of Property for Bengal*. Show Cornwallis and Philip Francis were driven by French Physiocracy, Adam Smith, and Whig doctrines of absolute property (Stokes, 1959).
* **Paragraph 7**: *The Permanent Settlement (1793)* (Bengal, Bihar, Coastal Odisha). Colonial state recognized zamindars as absolute proprietors; raiyats demoted to tenants. Failure to survey individual fields $\rightarrow$ sub-infeudation (*patnidars*) (Guha, 1963; Rothermund, 1971).
* **Paragraph 8**: *The Ryotwari Settlement* (Madras, Bombay, Southern Odisha). Thomas Munro’s direct settlement with *ryots*, influenced by Ricardo’s rent theory. First detailed cadastral maps (*pahanis* / *jamabandis*). High taxation $\rightarrow$ rural indebtedness and moneylender land alienation (Stokes, 1959; Kumar, 1965).
* **Paragraph 9**: *The Mahalwari Settlement* (North-West, Punjab, Central Provinces). Revenue settled with village *mahal* or *pattidars*; boundary maps (*hadbast*) and customary logs (*wajib-ul-arz*).
* **Paragraph 10**: *The Great Agrarian Conquest*. Use Neeladri Bhattacharya (2018)—Survey and Settlement operations actively constructed village boundaries, codified custom, enclosed 'wasteland', and transformed *patwaris* into state civil servants.
* **Paragraph 11**: *Agrarian Political Economy: Bypassed Transition*. Apply Terence J. Byres (1986, 1996)—failure of the 'English path' (Bengal) and 'peasant path' (Madras) due to colonial surplus extraction and unfree labour (Banaji, 1977; Washbrook, 1981).
* **Paragraph 12**: *New Institutional Economics Empirical Legacy*. Engage Abhijit Banerjee and Lakshmi Iyer (2005)—econometric proof that colonial landlord areas (Permanent Settlement) suffer long-run lower productivity and public investment compared to non-landlord areas.
* **Paragraph 13**: *Presumptive Colonial Legal Status*. Reiterate Rothermund (1971) and Wadhwa (2002)—colonial records were tax liability proofs, not state-guaranteed titles.

#### §2.3.3 Post-independence Reforms and the Survey & Settlement Framework (6 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 14**: *Post-1947 Reform Legislation*. Four pillars: Intermediary abolition, tenancy reform, ceiling limits, and holding consolidation (Appu, 1996).
* **Paragraph 15**: *Subversion and Absence of Records*. Use Planning Commission's *Task Force Report* (1973; Appu, 1996)—landlords used *benami* transfers and evicted tenants because records were un-updated and unrecorded.
* **Paragraph 16**: *The Mode of Production Debate*. Engage Patnaik (1990), Bhaduri (1973; semi-feudal usury preventing investment), and Banaji (1977).
* **Paragraph 17**: *Gendered Disinheritance*. Deploy Bina Agarwal’s (1994) *A Field of One's Own*—reforms and RoR entries recorded land exclusively under male heads of household, disinheriting women.
* **Paragraph 18**: *Jens Lerche's Bypassed Agrarian Transition*. Use Lerche (2013)—post-1991 industrialisation failed to absorb rural labour; smallholders hold micro-parcels for subsistence while working informally.
* **Paragraph 19**: *Abandonment of Resurveys*. Explain that ground Survey and Settlement operations were abandoned post-1970s, entering the 21st century with obsolete analogue registers. Transition to §2.4.

## 2.4 Legal and Institutional Infrastructure for Land Records in India (~2,000–2,500 words)

### Section Goal & Core Argument
Map the inherited legal and administrative architecture. Prove that Indian land administration suffers from **constitutional division, statutory bifurcation, and departmental fragmentation**, which generate high transaction costs and legal ambiguity.

---

### Paragraph-by-Paragraph Writing Blueprint

#### §2.4.1 Constitutional Framework and Jurisdictional Division (3 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 1**: *Seventh Schedule Split*. Entry 18 State List (land, tenure, revenue) vs Entries 6 & 7 Concurrent List (property transfer, contracts). Explains why India has 28 distinct state revenue codes and no single national land law (Goswami et al., 2022).
* **Paragraph 2**: *Role of Central Government*. Centre issues funding schemes (DILRMP) and model bills (NITI Aayog 2020), but states retain legislative veto.
* **Paragraph 3**: *Constitutional Right to Property*. Downgrading of property from Fundamental Right (Articles 19/31) to Constitutional Right under Article 300A (44th Amendment 1978; Austin 1999, Wahi 2013).

#### §2.4.2 Statutory Framework: Registration, Transfer, and Evidentiary Status (3 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 4**: *Registration Act, 1908 (Section 17)*. Registration records the *deed/document*, not the *title*. Sub-Registrar does not verify seller's ownership (Wadhwa, 1989, 2002).
* **Paragraph 5**: *Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (Section 54)*. Principle of *caveat emptor* ('let the buyer beware')—buyer must trace 30-year deed chain.
* **Paragraph 6**: *Evidence Act 1872 / Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 (Section 35)*. Courts consistently rule that **revenue entries (RoRs) do not confer title**—they are presumptive evidence of tax payment and possession (Rothermund, 1971; Wadhwa, 2002).

#### §2.4.3 Institutional Fragmentation: The Four Institutional Silos (4 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 7**: *The 4 Silos Diagram & Breakdown*:
  1. Revenue Dept (Textual RoR, Tahasildar, Patwari/RI, mutation).
  2. Survey & Settlement Dept (Spatial maps, FMB, Director of Survey).
  3. Registration Dept (Deeds, Sub-Registrar, stamp duty).
  4. Judicial & Revenue Courts (Civil title suits vs Revenue mutation appeals).
* **Paragraph 8**: *Contradictions Between Silos*. Deed registered $\neq$ RoR mutated $\neq$ Map updated. Unrecorded family partitions.
* **Paragraph 9**: *Transaction Costs and Litigation*. Deininger (2008) and Burman (2019)—siloed operations cause high transaction costs and rent-seeking.
* **Paragraph 10**: *Litigation Burden*. CPR Land Rights Initiative evidence—land disputes constitute ~30% of all Supreme Court litigation (Wahi et al., 2018; Goswami et al., 2022).

## 2.5 Genesis of DILRMP: A Brief Introduction (~1,200–1,500 words)

### Section Goal & Core Argument
Trace the 40-year policy shift from post-independence land reform to neoliberal land market facilitation. Formulate the four unresolved historical contradictions carried forward by DILRMP.

---

### Paragraph-by-Paragraph Writing Blueprint

#### §2.5.1 The Policy Lineage: From CLR to DILRMP (4 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 1**: *Phase 1 (1988–2007)*. Computerisation of Land Records (CLR 1988–89) & SRA & ULR (1987–88) for tehsil kiosks (Nayak, 2015).
* **Paragraph 2**: *Phase 2 (1989–2002)*. Wadhwa Committee Report (1989)—first official call to shift from presumptive deeds to conclusive Torrens title (Wadhwa, 1989, 2002).
* **Paragraph 3**: *Phase 3 (2008–2015)*. National Land Records Modernization Programme (NLRMP 2008)—3-tier technical integration (RoR + Cadastral map + Deed registration) (DoLR, 2008).
* **Paragraph 4**: *Phase 4 (2016–Present)*. Digital India Land Records Modernization Programme (DILRMP)—100% central funding, satellite/drone resurveys, ULPIN/Bhu-Aadhaar, NGDRS, AgriStack (DoLR, 2016, 2022).

#### §2.5.2 The Four Unresolved Historical Propositions (4 Paragraphs)
* **Paragraph 5**: *Contradiction 1: Digitisation vs Substantive Updating*. Digitising inaccurate paper records produces 'authoritative inaccuracy' / 'illegibly legible' records (Pratyush, 2023; Ramakumar & Ramesh, 2023).
* **Paragraph 6**: *Contradiction 2: Integration vs Legal Fragmentation*. Software APIs cannot resolve statutory presumptive limits without legislative reform (Burman, 2019).
* **Paragraph 7**: *Contradiction 3: Parcel Precision vs Socially Layered Claims*. Individual digital polygons erase commons (*gochar*), Adivasi customary tenure, sharecropping, and women's unrecorded rights.
* **Paragraph 8**: *Contradiction 4: Market Alienation vs Social Welfare*. DILRMP is framed as farmer welfare, but functions primarily to create frictionless land markets for corporate accumulation (Levien, 2018; Nayak, 2013).

## TISS Style & Formatting Reminders for Writing
- **UK English**: *organise*, *colour*, *behaviour*, *per cent*, *prioritise*.
- **Numbers**: Spell out 1–99 in words; 100+ in numerals; never start a sentence with a numeral.
- **Per cent**: Write as two words ("per cent"), never use `%` in text.
- **Citations**: APA format with comma `(Scott, 1998)`; `&` inside parentheses, `and` in prose.
- **Latin Abbreviations**: Avoid *e.g.*, *i.e.*, *etc.* in body text—write *for example*, *that is*, *and so on*.
