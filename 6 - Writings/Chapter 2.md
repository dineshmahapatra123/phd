# Chapter 2: History and Evolution of Land Records: Global and Indian Contexts

## 2.1 Land Records and Their Functions

Land records are not merely administrative registries or technical repositories of geographical coordinates; they constitute a complex, state-mediated information system that mediates the relationship between physical space, social classes, and political authority. In any agrarian or urbanising society, the recording of rights in land serves as a crucial locus where state building, economic accumulation, and social struggles intersect. This section conceptualises the functions of land records by establishing a four-function typology: fiscal, juridical, economic, and governmental. It argues that while these functions are analytically distinct, they operate simultaneously in the Indian context, producing a structured institutional arrangement that shapes the distribution of resources.

To understand how land—which is naturally a physical entity—becomes an object of state administration and market exchange, it is necessary to deploy Karl Polanyi’s conceptualisation of land as a 'fictitious commodity' (Polanyi, 1944). Polanyi argues that land is merely another name for nature, which is not produced for sale or consumption. Treating land as a commodity is therefore an ideological and administrative fiction. For this fiction to function, the state must construct a robust institutional infrastructure that partitions, measures, and represents land in a standardised format. Land records, maps, and registers are the primary instruments through which this fictitious commodification is achieved and maintained.

### 2.1.1 The Fiscal and Governmental Functions: Legibility and State Power

The historical genesis of land records is inextricably linked to state formation and the extraction of revenue. This represents the fiscal and governmental functions of land administration. As James C. Scott (1998) demonstrates, pre-modern agrarian landscapes were characterised by local, fluid, and highly complex customary arrangements that were virtually illegible to external authorities. The state did not know who held what interest in which parcel of land, making systematic taxation impossible.

To govern and extract revenue, the modern state had to create a standardised grid of legibility. The cadastral survey and the land register were developed to simplify this local complexity into uniform, readable categories. Scott employs the metaphor of the cadastral map as a still photograph of a river: it freezes a dynamic, socially embedded reality into a static geometric representation, deliberately ignoring customary shared rights, seasonal commons, and local ecology to focus exclusively on the land as a taxable asset (Scott, 1998).

This legibility is not merely descriptive; it is constitutive. Backed by the coercive power of the state, these administrative simplifications remake reality in the image of their categories. The record of rights does not just record tenure; it enforces a specific form of property relations. Michel Foucault’s concept of governmentality further clarifies this governmental function (Burchell, Gordon, & Miller, 1991). Land records function as a technology of population management, transforming land into a manageable database that allows the state to locate, tax, police, and deliver welfare to its subjects. In contemporary India, this legibility project continues under the Digital India Land Records Modernization Programme (DILRMP), where digital databases like *Bhulekh* and *Bhunaksha* serve as the infrastructural foundation for targeting state benefits and facilitating land acquisition.

However, state legibility is neither complete nor uncontested. As Mariano Sánchez-Talanquer (2020) argues, state visibility is often selective. While landowners may seek cadastral registration to secure state protection of their claims, they simultaneously manipulate valuations and boundaries to evade tax liabilities. This produces a 'one-eyed state' that is capable of recognising ownership but remains fiscally blind to the true value and usage of the land. Furthermore, as Ferree, Harris, and Honig (2023) note, legibility is not merely imposed from above; it is a strategic resource that citizens actively deploy across multiple forums to defend their claims against local competitors, even when the state's central institutions are weak.

### 2.1.2 The Juridical and Economic Functions: Property Rights and Capital Accumulation

The juridical function of land records concerns the legal definition, allocation, and protection of rights in land. In a modern capitalist economy, this juridical framework is the prerequisite for the economic function of land as capital. Historically, classical economists like Adam Smith and John Locke argued that secure, individual property rights are a precondition for investment and agricultural productivity. In this paradigm, land records provide the legal certainty that encourages landowners to make long-term capital investments without the threat of arbitrary dispossession.

In contemporary development policy, this argument has been revitalised by new institutional economists. Hernando de Soto (2000) contends that the primary barrier to development in the Global South is not the absence of assets, but the absence of formal property records. Without formal titles, assets remain 'dead capital' because they cannot be easily transacted, divided, or used as collateral to secure institutional credit. Formalisation reforms, through land registration, are seen as the mechanism to unlock this capital, lower transaction costs, and integrate the poor into the formal market economy (de Soto, 2000; Deininger & Feder, 2009).

This institutionalist view, however, is challenged by the agrarian political-economy tradition. From a Marxist perspective, the formalisation of individual property rights and the creation of clear land records are instruments of primitive accumulation and class differentiation (Marx, 1867; Harvey, 2003). By dismantling customary, communal, and overlapping tenures in favour of exclusive private property, formal land records facilitate the commodification of land and its integration into global circuits of capital. In this view, secure title is not a neutral instrument of growth, but a mechanism that enables the alienation of land from smallholders and facilitates accumulation by dispossession (Levien, 2018).

Moreover, the transition from deeds-based systems to title-based systems reveals the structural limits of this economic model. In a deeds registration system, which India inherited from the British under the Registration Act of 1908, the state merely records transactions without guaranteeing the validity of the underlying title. The record of rights remains presumptive, meaning that entries are presumed correct only until proven otherwise in a court of law. Shifting to a conclusive titling system, such as the Torrens system, requires the state to guarantee the title and maintain an assurance fund to indemnify losses (Wadhwa, 1989). As Deininger and Feder (2009) observe, such a transition is exceptionally expensive and requires robust administrative capacity. When implemented in contexts with low land values or weak institutions, it often results in institutional failure, where the digitised record merely registers an inherited regime of ground-level inaccuracy.

### 2.1.3 The Synthesis: Land Records as a Palimpsest of Contested Relations

In the Indian context, the fiscal, governmental, juridical, and economic functions of land records do not operate in isolation; they are layered upon one another like a palimpsest. Each historical regime—from the Mughal fiscal-tributary structures to the British colonial settlements, post-independence land reforms, and the contemporary neoliberal digital push—has inscribed its own logic onto the records. 

The record of rights is therefore not a neutral administrative mirror of reality, but the product of historical contestation. When DILRMP attempts to digitise and integrate these records, it does not encounter a clean slate. It encounters legacy issues, unresolved disputes, and informal customary arrangements that resist technocratic categorisation. The friction between the state's drive for legibility, capital's demand for clear assets, and the rural population's reliance on informal and shared tenures produces the ground-level contradictions that characterise contemporary land governance in India.
