### Ch. 1.1 — Conceptual and Analytical Framework

> "The more I examined these efforts at sedentarization, the more I came to see them as a state's attempt to make a society legible, to arrange the population in ways that simplified the classic state functions of taxation, conscription, and prevention of rebellion." (p. 2)
> **Supports**: Grounds the legibility concept in concrete state functions — taxation first — establishing the framework's relevance to land records as instruments of state extraction.

> "Certain forms of knowledge and control require a narrowing of vision. The great advantage of such tunnel vision is that it brings into sharp focus certain limited aspects of an otherwise far more complex and unwieldy reality." (p. 11)
> **Supports**: The opening analytical claim of Ch. 1, establishing the epistemological logic behind all state simplification projects, including land records and cadastral surveys.

> "These state simplifications, the basic givens of modern statecraft, were, I began to realize, rather like abridged maps. They did not successfully represent the actual activity of the society they depicted, nor were they intended to; they represented only that slice of it that interested the official observer. They were, moreover, not just maps. Rather, they were maps that, when allied with state power, would enable much of the reality they depicted to be remade. Thus a state cadastral map created to designate taxable property-holders does not merely describe a system of land tenure; it creates such a system through its ability to give its categories the force of law." (p. 3)
> **Supports**: The governing theoretical claim of the thesis framework: land records are not mirrors but moulds. Central to Ch. 1.1 as a conceptual anchor.

> "In sum, the legibility of a society provides the capacity for large-scale social engineering, high-modernist ideology provides the desire, the authoritarian state provides the determination to act on that desire, and an incapacitated civil society provides the leveled social terrain on which to build." (p. 5)
> **Supports**: Scott's four-element formula for disaster — directly applicable to colonial land settlements and, at lower intensity, to technocratic modernisation programs such as DILRMP.

> "Designed or planned social order is necessarily schematic; it always ignores essential features of any real, functioning social order. This truth is best illustrated in a work-to-rule strike, which turns on the fact that any production process depends on a host of informal practices and improvisations that could never be codified. By merely following the rules meticulously, the workforce can virtually halt production." (p. 6)
> **Supports**: Theoretical warrant for the argument that DILRMP's formal categories cannot capture the informal land economy — the metis that sustains tenure in practice.

> "Much of this book can be read as a case against the imperialism of high-modernist, planned social order. I stress the word 'imperialism' here because I am emphatically not making a blanket case against either bureaucratic planning or high-modernist ideology. I am, however, making a case against an imperial or hegemonic planning mentality that excludes the necessary role of local knowledge and know-how." (p. 6)
> **Supports**: Scott's self-positioning — not against state action per se but against the assumption that formal systems can replace practical local knowledge. Relevant for Ch. 1.1 framing: the thesis is similarly not anti-state but anti-technocratic.

> "Radically simplified designs for social organization seem to court the same risks of failure courted by radically simplified designs for natural environments. The failures and vulnerability of monocrop commercial forests and genetically engineered, mechanized monocropping mimic the failures of collective farms and planned cities." (p. 7)
> **Supports**: The structural parallel between ecological and social simplification failures — useful as a rhetorical and conceptual anchor in Ch. 1.1.

> "No administrative system is capable of representing any existing social community except through a heroic and greatly schematized process of abstraction and simplification." (p. 22–23)
> **Supports**: The foundational epistemological claim: the gap between records and reality is inherent in the administrative enterprise, not a remediable design flaw. Central to Ch. 1.1.

### Ch. 2.1 — Land Records and Their Functions

> "The crowning artifact of this mighty simplification is the cadastral map. Created by trained surveyors and mapped to a given scale, the cadastral map is a more or less complete and accurate survey of all landholdings. Since the driving logic behind the map is to create a manageable and reliable format for taxation, the map is associated with a property register in which each specified (usually numbered) lot on the map is linked to an owner who is responsible for paying its taxes." (p. 36)
> **Supports**: Establishes land records as instruments of revenue, not title — the foundational claim of Ch. 2.1.

> "The cadastral map and property register are to the taxation of land as the maps and tables of the scientific forester were to the fiscal exploitation of the forest." (p. 36)
> **Supports**: Clarifies the revenue purpose of land records by analogy with scientific forestry — both reduce complex realities to single fiscal variables.

> "Land maps in general and cadastral maps in particular are designed to make the local situation legible to an outsider. For purely local purposes, a cadastral map was redundant. Everyone knew who held, say, the meadow by the river, the value of the fodder it yielded, and the feudal dues it carried; there was no need to know its precise dimensions." (p. 44)
> **Supports**: Identifies the cadastral map as an outsider instrument — for tax officials, not for the cultivators whose land it represents. Directly supports the Ch. 2.1 argument that land records serve the state, not the farmer.

> "A national tax administration requires the same logic: a legible, bureaucratic formula which a new official can quickly grasp and administer from the documents in his office." (p. 44)
> **Supports**: The record-as-revenue-instrument: designed for administrative portability, not local accuracy.

> "The cadastral survey was but one technique in the growing armory of the utilitarian modern state.... The modern state increasingly aspired to 'take in charge' the physical and human resources of the nation and make them more productive." (p. 51)
> **Supports**: Contextualises land records within the broader expansion of state capacity — relevant to the genesis of DILRMP in Ch. 2.5.

> "The only agents of taxation who regularly reached to the level of the household and its cultivated fields were the local nobility and clergy in the course of collecting feudal dues and the religious tithe. For its part, the state had neither the administrative tools nor the information to penetrate to this level." (p. 37–38)
> **Supports**: The premodern state's fiscal blindness — historical context for why land records were created and what they replaced.

### Ch. 2.2.2 — Colonial Approaches to Land Records

> "The shorthand formulas through which tax officials must apprehend reality are not mere tools of observation. By a kind of fiscal Heisenberg principle, they frequently have the power to transform the facts they take note of." (p. 47)
> **Supports**: The theoretical mechanism explaining how colonial land settlements created property regimes rather than describing pre-existing ones.

> "The permanent settlement in India, for example, created a new class who, because they paid the taxes on the land, became full owners with rights of inheritance and sale where none had existed earlier. At the same time, literally millions of cultivators, tenants, and laborers lost their customary rights of access to the land and its products." (p. 48)
> **Supports**: The paradigm case for Ch. 2.2.2 and 2.3.2: fiscal assignment → property creation → dispossession. This is the direct India reference.

> "Those in the colonies who first plumbed the mysteries of the new tenure administration enjoyed unique opportunities. Thus the Vietnamese secrétaires and interprètes who served as intermediaries between the French officials in the Mekong Delta and their Vietnamese subjects were in a position to make great fortunes." (p. 48)
> **Supports**: Information asymmetry under colonial tenure: those who could decode the new system — not local cultivators — captured rents. Relevant comparative case for understanding revenue intermediaries in Indian settlements.

> "What was simplifying to an official was mystifying to most cultivators." (p. 48)
> **Supports**: The distribution of costs of colonial legibility projects: administrative clarity for the state, confusion and dispossession for cultivators. Central to Ch. 2.2.2.

> "Where the new tenure system was a colonial imposition — that is, where it was totally unfamiliar, where it was imposed by alien conquerors using an unintelligible language and institutional context, and where local practices bore no resemblance to freehold tenure — the consequences were far-reaching." (p. 48)
> **Supports**: Identifies the conditions under which legibility projects are most destructive — precisely the conditions of British colonial land settlements in India.

> "It was claimed, although the evidence is not convincing, that common property was less productive than freehold property. The state's case against communal forms of land tenure, however, was based on the correct observation that it was fiscally illegible and hence fiscally less productive." (p. 39)
> **Supports**: Explains the colonial state's hostility to communal tenure: not efficiency but legibility. Directly relevant to the colonial destruction of common lands and common rights in India.

> "The cadastral map is an instrument of control which both reflects and consolidates the power of those who commission it.... The cadastral map is partisan: where knowledge is power, it provides comprehensive information to be used to the advantage of some and the detriment of others, as rulers and ruled were well aware in the tax struggles of the 18th and 19th centuries. Finally, the cadastral map is active: in portraying one reality, as in the settlement of the new world or in India, it helps obliterate the old." (p. 47, citing Kain and Baigent)
> **Supports**: The most direct statement of the political character of cadastral mapping — directly names India. Central to Ch. 2.2.2.

> "Where, by contrast, civil society was well organised and the state relatively weak, cadastral mapping was late, often voluntary, and fragmentary. Thus Napoleonic France was mapped much earlier than England, where the legal profession managed for a long time to stymie this threat to its local, income-earning function. It followed from the same logic that conquered colonies ruled by fiat would often be cadastrally mapped before the metropolitan nation that ordered it." (p. 49)
> **Supports**: Explains why India was more thoroughly cadastrally mapped than England — colonial state power met weaker resistance. Relevant to the pre-DILRMP history in Ch. 2.3.2.

### Ch. 2.3.2 — Colonial Settlements and Land Administration

> "The permanent settlement in India, for example, created a new class who, because they paid the taxes on the land, became full owners with rights of inheritance and sale where none had existed earlier. At the same time, literally millions of cultivators, tenants, and laborers lost their customary rights of access to the land and its products." (p. 48)
> **Supports**: The Permanent Settlement as fiscal Heisenberg — tax assignment created property; see note under Ch. 2.2.2.

> "The shorthand functioned, as did Beckmann's Normalbaume, as not just a description, however inadequate. Backed by state power through records, courts, and ultimately coercion, these state fictions transformed the reality they presumed to observe, although never so thoroughly as to precisely fit the grid." (p. 25)
> **Supports**: The mechanism of colonial land settlements: state categories, backed by legal force, reshape the reality they purport to describe. The caveat — "never so thoroughly as to precisely fit the grid" — is the origin of the records-ground gap.

> "The novel state-imposed form of land tenure was far more revolutionary than a door-and-window tax. It established a whole new institutional nexus. However simple and uniform the new tenure system was to an administrator, it flung villagers willy-nilly into a world of title deeds, land offices, fees, assessments, and applications. They faced powerful new specialists in the form of land clerks, surveyors, judges, and lawyers whose rules of procedure and decisions were unfamiliar." (p. 48)
> **Supports**: The institutional disruption of colonial land settlements — introducing legal specialists whose expertise was opaque to cultivators and whose services extracted rents.

> "The historical solution, at least for the liberal state, has typically been the heroic simplification of individual freehold tenure. Land is owned by a legal individual who possesses wide powers of use, inheritance, or sale and whose ownership is represented by a uniform deed of title enforced through the judicial and police institutions of the state." (p. 36)
> **Supports**: Identifies freehold as a political-administrative choice, not an economic necessity — the colonial state imposed it for fiscal legibility.

### Ch. 2.4 — Legal and Institutional Infrastructure for Land Records

> "Freehold title and standard land measurement were to central taxation and the real-estate market what central bank currency was to the marketplace. By the same token, they threatened to destroy a great deal of local power and autonomy." (p. 48)
> **Supports**: Positions the formal land registration system as the currency of a national market in land — with the same homogenising and local-autonomy-destroying effects.

> "The increasing intensity of interaction with the state and statelike structures exactly parallels the development of permanent, heritable patronyms." (p. 68, surnames chapter)
> **Supports**: Illustrates a general principle — the more the state penetrates daily life, the more its simplified categories displace local practices — applicable to the expansion of formal land titling in Ch. 2.4.

### Ch. 3.4 — Critical Reflections on Technocratic Reforms

> "Designed or planned social order is necessarily schematic; it always ignores essential features of any real, functioning social order." (p. 6)
> **Supports**: Core critique applicable to DILRMP: the formal scheme cannot capture the informal practices — tenancy, multi-owner arrangements, encroachment — that constitute actual land relations.

> "The formal scheme was parasitic on informal processes that, alone, it could not create or maintain. To the degree that the formal scheme made no allowance for these processes or actually suppressed them, it failed both its intended beneficiaries and ultimately its designers as well." (p. 6)
> **Supports**: DILRMP depends on the informal tenure arrangements it doesn't recognise: unregistered tenants, verbal partition agreements, and common use rights sustain villages whose formal records are outdated.

> "If I were asked to condense the reasons behind these failures into a single sentence, I would say that the progenitors of such plans regarded themselves as far smarter and farseeing than they really were and, at the same time, regarded their subjects as far more stupid and incompetent than they really were." (p. 343–344)
> **Supports**: Characterises the technocratic stance: planners bracket what they cannot measure; DILRMP's design assumptions (complete digitisation → complete legibility → resolved conflicts) exemplify this.

> "The plans that they hatched bore a family resemblance to the schemes of legibility and standardization devised by the absolutist kings of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. What was wholly new, however, was the magnitude of both the plans for the wholesale transformation of society and the instruments of statecraft — censuses, cadastral maps, identity cards, statistical bureaus, schools, mass media, internal security apparatuses — that could take them farther along this road than any seventeenth-century monarch would have dreamed." (p. 343)
> **Supports**: DILRMP is the latest iteration of a centuries-old legibility logic; the digital instruments are new but the categorical structure is colonial.

> "Such maps of legibility and control, especially when they are backed by an authoritarian state, do partly succeed in shaping the natural and social environment after their image. To the degree that such thin maps do manage to impress themselves on social life, what kind of people do they foster?" (p. 349)
> **Supports**: The concession that legibility projects do work — partially — with destructive side effects. DILRMP will succeed in some dimensions (welfare inclusion) while failing in others (conflict resolution).

> "All socially engineered systems of formal order are in fact subsystems of a larger system on which they are ultimately dependent, not to say parasitic. The subsystem relies on a variety of processes — frequently informal or antecedent — which alone it cannot create or maintain. The more schematic, thin, and simplified the formal order, the less resilient and the more vulnerable it is to disturbances outside its narrow parameters." (p. 352)
> **Supports**: The structural argument against DILRMP as a solution: it is a thin formal system parasitic on informal tenure arrangements it does not acknowledge.

### Ch. 5.5 — Land Ownership vs. Possession

> "We must never assume that local practice conforms with state theory." (p. 49)
> **Supports**: Theoretical warrant for the ownership-possession gap the village studies document.

> "The land-office records may serve as the basis for taxation, but they may have little to do with the actual rights to the land. Paper owners may not be the effective owners." (p. 49, drawing on Sally Falk Moore)
> **Supports**: Directly theorises the Ch. 5.5 empirical question: who is on the RoR vs. who actually possesses and uses the land.

> "Russian peasants, as we saw, might register a 'paper' consolidation while continuing to interstrip. Land invasions, squatting, and poaching, if successful, represent the exercise of de facto property rights which are not represented on paper." (p. 49)
> **Supports**: Historical comparative evidence for the structural persistence of informal possession alongside formal records — relevant to Ch. 5.5.1 (inheritance and partition), 5.5.2 (encroachment).

> "There will always be a shadow land-tenure system lurking beside and beneath the official account in the land-records office." (p. 49)
> **Supports**: The thesis's empirical programme in Ch. 5 is to document precisely this shadow system — informal tenancy, multi-owner records, encroachment-by-consent.

> "Local land tenure practice is perfectly legible to all who live within it from day to day. Its details may often be contested and far from satisfactory to all its practitioners, but it is completely familiar; local residents have no difficulty in grasping its subtleties and using its flexible provisions for their own purposes. State officials, on the other hand, cannot be expected to decipher and then apply a new set of property hieroglyphs for each jurisdiction." (p. 35–36)
> **Supports**: Legibility is relational — what is opaque to the revenue official is transparent to the village. The RoR's illegibility to its subjects and legibility to outsiders is exactly this problem inverted.

> "Customary systems of tenure should not be romanticized; they are usually riven with inequalities based on gender, status, and lineage. But because they are strongly local, particular, and adaptable, their plasticity can be the source of microadjustments that lead to shifts in prevailing practice." (p. 35)
> **Supports**: Scott's caution against romanticising the informal — important for the thesis, which must acknowledge caste and gender inequality within the informal tenure system while still arguing that formalisation destroys beneficial complexity.

### Ch. 5.7 — Divergence Between Records and Ground Reality

> "The cadastral map is very much like a still photograph of the current in a river. It represents the parcels of land as they were arranged and owned at the moment the survey was conducted. But the current is always moving, and in periods of major social upheaval and growth, a cadastral survey may freeze a scene of great turbulence. Changes are taking place on field boundaries; land is being subdivided or consolidated by inheritance or purchase; new canals, roads, and railways are being cut; land use is changing." (p. 46)
> **Supports**: The theoretical explanation for why Odisha's cadastral maps (last resurveyed in most villages in the 1960s) are structurally inadequate: the maps froze a post-independence scene that subsequent land fragmentation, encroachment, and use-change has transformed beyond recognition.

> "These state simplifications, like all state simplifications, are always far more static and schematic than the actual social phenomena they presume to typify. The farmer rarely experiences an average crop, an average rainfall, or an average price for his crops." (p. 46)
> **Supports**: Records represent averages and snapshots; ground truth is dynamic and particular — the fundamental source of records-ground divergence.

> "Governed by a practical, concrete objective, the cadastral lens also ignored anything lying outside its sharply defined field of vision.... The most significant instance of myopia, of course, was that the cadastral map and assessment system considered only the dimensions of the land and its value as a productive asset or as a commodity for sale. Any value that the land might have for subsistence purposes or for the local ecology was bracketed as aesthetic, ritual, or sentimental values." (p. 46–47)
> **Supports**: The cadastral map systematically excludes common land uses, subsistence values, and social meanings — the exact values that explain discrepancies between records and possession in the fieldwork villages.

> "The accumulation of annotations and marginalia at some point render the map illegible, whereupon a more up-to-date but still static map must be drawn and the process repeated." (p. 46)
> **Supports**: The built-in obsolescence of cadastral records: they always lag behind ground reality, and updating them produces only another frozen snapshot.

> "Surveyors, one recent Swedish study found, made the fields more geometrically regular than they in fact were. Ignoring small jogs and squiggles made their job easier and did not materially affect the outcome." (p. 46–47)
> **Supports**: Surveyors simplify as they record — introducing small distortions that accumulate over time. The Amin's measurement may have done the same, producing a cadastral geometry that never quite matched the ground.

### Ch. 5.8 — Political Economy of State Interventions / Welfare Linkages

> "Freehold title and standard land measurement were to central taxation and the real-estate market what central bank currency was to the marketplace." (p. 48)
> **Supports**: The RoR-as-welfare-gate argument: once the RoR becomes the currency of entitlement (PM-Kisan, CM-Kisan), those with imperfect or absent RoR records are excluded just as those without valid currency cannot transact.

> "Those in the colonies who first plumbed the mysteries of the new tenure administration enjoyed unique opportunities." (p. 48)
> **Supports**: The welfare exclusion argument in Ch. 5.8: those who can navigate the formal system (with land, literacy, and connections) benefit; those who cannot are excluded.

### Debate 3 — Technocratic Modernisation vs. Political Economy of Reform

> "Designed or planned social order is necessarily schematic; it always ignores essential features of any real, functioning social order. This truth is best illustrated in a work-to-rule strike, which turns on the fact that any production process depends on a host of informal practices and improvisations that could never be codified." (p. 6)
> **Position**: Directly supports the argument that digitisation of land records cannot resolve the informal tenure complexity it has not been designed to recognise.

> "The formal scheme was parasitic on informal processes that, alone, it could not create or maintain. To the degree that the formal scheme made no allowance for these processes or actually suppressed them, it failed both its intended beneficiaries and ultimately its designers as well." (p. 6)
> **Position**: Challenges the technocratic optimism of DILRMP: the formal scheme depends on informal arrangements it brackets.

> "If I were asked to condense the reasons behind these failures into a single sentence, I would say that the progenitors of such plans regarded themselves as far smarter and farseeing than they really were and, at the same time, regarded their subjects as far more stupid and incompetent than they really were." (p. 343–344)
> **Position**: Characterises the epistemic failure of technocratic planners — directly applicable to DILRMP's design assumptions.

> "All socially engineered systems of formal order are in fact subsystems of a larger system on which they are ultimately dependent, not to say parasitic." (p. 352)
> **Position**: Structural argument against the sufficiency of DILRMP: formal legibility systems cannot replace the informal tenure substrate on which they depend.

It was, accordingly, uncritical, unskeptical, and thus unscientifically optimistic about the possibilities for the comprehensive planning of human settlement and production." (p. 4)

### Debate 4 — Colonial Revenue Record → Postcolonial Title Document

> "The permanent settlement in India, for example, created a new class who, because they paid the taxes on the land, became full owners with rights of inheritance and sale where none had existed earlier. At the same time, literally millions of cultivators, tenants, and laborers lost their customary rights of access to the land and its products." (p. 48)

> "The cadastral map is an instrument of control which both reflects and consolidates the power of those who commission it.... Finally, the cadastral map is active: in portraying one reality, as in the settlement of the new world or in India, it helps obliterate the old." (p. 47, citing Kain and Baigent)
> **Position**: The cadastral map obliterates the prior tenure reality — customary, communal, layered — and replaces it with a new fiscal-legal one that then becomes the baseline for all subsequent administration.

> "The novel state-imposed form of land tenure... flung villagers willy-nilly into a world of title deeds, land offices, fees, assessments, and applications." (p. 48)
> **Position**: The colonial record system created a new bureaucratic-legal terrain alien to cultivators, whose dealings with it were mediated by intermediaries — a structural feature that persists into the DILRMP era.

> "The shorthand functioned, as did Beckmann's Normalbaume, as not just a description, however inadequate. Backed by state power through records, courts, and ultimately coercion, these state fictions transformed the reality they presumed to observe, although never so thoroughly as to precisely fit the grid." (p. 25)
> **Position**: State categories backed by legal force reshape the reality they describe — but always incompletely. The gap between record and ground is the trace of the incompleteness.

### Debate 5 — Spatial-Textual Disjuncture

> "The cadastral map is very much like a still photograph of the current in a river. It represents the parcels of land as they were arranged and owned at the moment the survey was conducted. But the current is always moving." (p. 46)
> **Position**: The theoretical statement of Debate 5: textual records can be updated (and DILRMP has updated them) but spatial records cannot be updated without resurvey, which requires enormous resources.

> "The accumulation of annotations and marginalia at some point render the map illegible, whereupon a more up-to-date but still static map must be drawn and the process repeated." (p. 46)
> **Position**: Describes the lifecycle of cadastral maps — they are born already aging. The 1960s Odisha maps are at the end of this lifecycle.

> "No operating land-revenue system can stop at the mere identification of parcel and ownership. Other schematic facts, themselves static, must be created to arrive at some judgment of a sustainable tax burden. Land may be graded by soil class, how well it is watered, what crops are grown on it, and its presumed average yield, which is often checked by sample crop-cuttings. These facts are themselves changing, or they are averages that may mask great variation." (p. 46)
> **Position**: Textual records contain averages that mask the variability and change that characterise actual land use — even a frequently updated Bhulekh misrepresents ground reality.

> "Surveyors made the fields more geometrically regular than they in fact were. Ignoring small jogs and squiggles made their job easier and did not materially affect the outcome." (p. 46–47)
> **Position**: The spatial record introduced distortions at the point of creation — distortions that may have compounded over decades to produce significant spatial-textual gaps.

### Debate 7 — NIE Efficiency Logic vs. Agrarian Social Relations

> "Customary systems of tenure should not be romanticized; they are usually riven with inequalities based on gender, status, and lineage. But because they are strongly local, particular, and adaptable, their plasticity can be the source of microadjustments that lead to shifts in prevailing practice." (p. 35)

> "The state's case against communal forms of land tenure was based on the correct observation that it was fiscally illegible and hence fiscally less productive. Rather than trying to bring the map into line with reality, the historical resolution has generally been for the state to impose a property system in line with its fiscal grid." (p. 39)
> **Position**: Challenges NIE efficiency logic: the state's preference for freehold is driven by fiscal legibility, not demonstrated productivity gains.

> "The mode of production in such communities was simply incompatible with the assumption of individual freehold tenure implicit in a cadastral map. It was claimed, although the evidence is not convincing, that common property was less productive than freehold property." (p. 38–39)
> **Position**: Scott directly disputes the efficiency claim for freehold — the claim was asserted to justify fiscal goals, not demonstrated empirically.

## C. By Concept

---

### Concept: Legibility

> "The more I examined these efforts at sedentarization, the more I came to see them as a state's attempt to make a society legible, to arrange the population in ways that simplified the classic state functions of taxation, conscription, and prevention of rebellion." (p. 2)
> **Function**: Definition — legibility as the product of state simplification projects, serving fiscal and coercive state functions.

> "The premodern state was, in many crucial respects, partially blind; it knew precious little about its subjects, their wealth, their landholdings and yields, their location, their very identity." (p. 2)
> **Function**: Extension — legibility projects arise to resolve fiscal blindness; the demand for legibility is driven by the limits of premodern state administration.

> "Local land tenure practice is perfectly legible to all who live within it from day to day.... State officials, on the other hand, cannot be expected to decipher and then apply a new set of property hieroglyphs for each jurisdiction." (p. 35–36)
> **Function**: Complication — legibility is relational, not absolute. What is illegible to the state is perfectly legible to its subjects. The state's demand for uniform legibility destroys the locally sufficient legibility of customary systems.

> "Such maps of legibility and control, especially when they are backed by an authoritarian state, do partly succeed in shaping the natural and social environment after their image." (p. 349)
> **Function**: Extension — legibility is not only a description but a productive force. The RoR creates the tenure it purports to record.

> "Historically, the relative illegibility to outsiders of some urban neighborhoods... has provided a vital margin of political safety from control by outside elites." (p. 54, Ch. 2)
> **Function**: Counter-evidence — illegibility can be protective. Customary tenure's opacity to the state is not only a limit; it insulates local communities from extraction.

### Concept: State Simplifications / Thin Simplifications

> "These state simplifications, the basic givens of modern statecraft, were, I began to realize, rather like abridged maps. They did not successfully represent the actual activity of the society they depicted, nor were they intended to; they represented only that slice of it that interested the official observer. They were, moreover, not just maps. Rather, they were maps that, when allied with state power, would enable much of the reality they depicted to be remade." (p. 3)
> **Function**: Definition — state simplifications are not neutral representations but performative instruments that remake the reality they claim to describe.

> "No administrative system is capable of representing any existing social community except through a heroic and greatly schematized process of abstraction and simplification." (p. 22–23)
> **Function**: Grounds the thin-simplification concept in the inherent limits of administrative knowledge.

> "Certain forms of knowledge and control require a narrowing of vision. The great advantage of such tunnel vision is that it brings into sharp focus certain limited aspects of an otherwise far more complex and unwieldy reality." (p. 11)
> **Function**: Epistemological basis — thin simplifications are cognitively necessary for management; their limitation is the information discarded in the narrowing.

> "These state simplifications, like all state simplifications, are always far more static and schematic than the actual social phenomena they presume to typify." (p. 46)
> **Function**: Identifies the specific failure mode of thin simplifications: they are static and schematic applied to dynamic and complex realities.

### Concept: Cadastral Map

> "The crowning artifact of this mighty simplification is the cadastral map. Created by trained surveyors and mapped to a given scale, the cadastral map is a more or less complete and accurate survey of all landholdings. Since the driving logic behind the map is to create a manageable and reliable format for taxation, the map is associated with a property register in which each specified (usually numbered) lot on the map is linked to an owner who is responsible for paying its taxes." (p. 36)
> **Function**: Definition — the cadastral map is defined by its revenue purpose, not its representational accuracy.

> "Land maps in general and cadastral maps in particular are designed to make the local situation legible to an outsider. For purely local purposes, a cadastral map was redundant." (p. 44)
> **Function**: Identifies the cadastral map's intended user: the state official, not the cultivator.

> "The value of the cadastral map to the state lies in its abstraction and universality. In principle, at least, the same objective standard can be applied throughout the nation, regardless of local context, to produce a complete and unambiguous map of all landed property." (p. 44)
> **Function**: Explains why the cadastral map is a state instrument: it enables comparison, aggregation, and centralised management across diverse localities.

> "The cadastral map is very much like a still photograph of the current in a river." (p. 46)
> **Function**: The key metaphor for the spatial-textual disjuncture argument: the map captures a moment; the ground continues to move.

> "The cadastral map is an instrument of control which both reflects and consolidates the power of those who commission it.... The cadastral map is partisan." (p. 47, Kain and Baigent via Scott)
> **Function**: Extension — the cadastral map is not neutral; it encodes the interests of the commissioning state.

> "The completeness of the cadastral map depends, in a curious way, on its abstract sketchiness, its lack of detail — its thinness. Taken alone, it is essentially a geometric representation of the borders or frontiers between parcels of land. What lies inside the parcel is left blank — unspecified — since it is not germane to the map plotting itself." (p. 44)
> **Function**: Counter-intuitive extension — the cadastral map's completeness as a fiscal instrument is achieved through deliberate thinness.

### Concept: Fiscal Heisenberg Principle

> "The shorthand formulas through which tax officials must apprehend reality are not mere tools of observation. By a kind of fiscal Heisenberg principle, they frequently have the power to transform the facts they take note of." (p. 47)
> **Function**: Definition — the principle that administrative observation transforms the observed reality.

> "A state cadastral map created to designate taxable property-holders does not merely describe a system of land tenure; it creates such a system through its ability to give its categories the force of law." (p. 3)
> **Function**: The flagship illustration — a cadastral map with the force of law is a world-making device, not a descriptive one.

> "The permanent settlement in India, for example, created a new class who, because they paid the taxes on the land, became full owners with rights of inheritance and sale where none had existed earlier." (p. 48)
> **Function**: Paradigm empirical case — fiscal assignment creates property regime. The tax record became the title deed.

> "The shorthand functioned, as did Beckmann's Normalbaume, as not just a description, however inadequate. Backed by state power through records, courts, and ultimately coercion, these state fictions transformed the reality they presumed to observe." (p. 25)
> **Function**: Extension — "state fictions" backed by power become real. The record's legal force makes it world-constituting.

### Concept: Freehold Tenure as Heroic Simplification

> "The historical solution, at least for the liberal state, has typically been the heroic simplification of individual freehold tenure. Land is owned by a legal individual who possesses wide powers of use, inheritance, or sale and whose ownership is represented by a uniform deed of title enforced through the judicial and police institutions of the state." (p. 36)
> **Function**: Definition — freehold is presented as a state-administrative choice, not a natural tenure form.

> "Just as the flora of the forest were reduced to Normalbaume, so the complex tenure arrangements of customary practice are reduced to freehold, transferrable title." (p. 36)
> **Function**: The central analogy of the book, applied to land tenure: freehold is the Normalbaum of human tenure — a radical reduction to a single administratively convenient variable.

> "The mode of production in such communities was simply incompatible with the assumption of individual freehold tenure implicit in a cadastral map." (p. 38–39)
> **Function**: Extension — communal and layered tenures cannot be expressed within the freehold grid; they are not simplified by it but obliterated.

> "Modern freehold tenure is tenure that is mediated through the state and therefore readily decipherable only to those who have sufficient training and a grasp of the state statutes. Its relative simplicity is lost on those who cannot break the code, just as the relative clarity of customary tenure is lost on those who live outside the village." (p. 35–36)
> **Function**: Complication — freehold is simple only for the administrator. For the farmer without legal literacy, the formal system is no more legible than customary tenure was to the colonial official.

### Concept: High Modernism

> "High modernism must not be confused with scientific practice. It was fundamentally, as the term 'ideology' implies, a faith that borrowed, as it were, the legitimacy of science and technology. It was, accordingly, uncritical, unskeptical, and thus unscientifically optimistic about the possibilities for the comprehensive planning of human settlement and production." (p. 4)
> **Function**: Definition — high modernism as ideology, not science.

> "The carriers of high modernism tended to see rational order in remarkably visual aesthetic terms. For them, an efficient, rationally organised city, village, or farm was a city that looked regimented and orderly in a geometrical sense." (p. 4)
> **Function**: Identifies the visual-aesthetic dimension of high modernism — the preference for legibility as an aesthetic quality, not merely a functional one. Relevant to how DILRMP presents itself: maps that look complete, dashboards that look uniform.

> "High modernism was about 'interests' as well as faith. Its carriers, even when they were capitalist entrepreneurs, required state action to realise their plans." (p. 4–5)
> **Function**: Extension — high modernism is not merely ideological; it aligns with the interests of planners, engineers, and state officials whose careers depend on large-scale projects.

### Concept: Metis

> "Broadly understood, metis represents a wide array of practical skills and acquired intelligence in responding to a constantly changing natural and human environment." (p. 315)
> **Function**: Definition.

> "Metis is most applicable to broadly similar but never precisely identical situations requiring a quick and practiced adaptation that becomes almost second nature to the practitioner. The skills of metis may well involve rules of thumb, but such rules are largely acquired through practice and a developed feel or knack for strategy. Metis resists simplification into deductive principles which can successfully be transmitted through book learning." (p. 317)
> **Function**: Extension — metis is distinguished from both improvisation and rule-following; it is practiced judgment in complex, non-repeatable situations.

> "My aim in this chapter is to conceptualise these practical skills, variously called know-how (savoir faire or arts de faire), common sense, experience, a knack, or metis. What are these skills? How are they created, developed, and maintained? What is their relation to formal epistemic knowledge? I hope to show that many forms of high modernism have replaced a valuable collaboration between these two dialects of knowledge with an 'imperial' scientific view, which dismisses practical know-how as insignificant at best and as dangerous superstitions at worst." (p. 311)
> **Function**: Positions metis as what high modernism destroys — the "two dialects" metaphor is directly applicable to the DILRMP vs. informal tenure argument.

> "For the Greeks and particularly for Plato, episteme and techne represented knowledge of an order completely different from metis. Technical knowledge, or techne, could be expressed precisely and comprehensively in the form of hard-and-fast rules, principles, and propositions." (p. 320, Chapter 9)
> **Function**: Defines the contrast class: episteme (formal deductive knowledge) and techne (codified technical knowledge) versus metis (practical, situational, irreducibly experiential knowledge).

> "The practice and experience reflected in metis is almost always local. Thus a guide on mountain climbing may be best at Zermatt, which she has scaled often; an airplane pilot best on Boeing 747s, on which he was trained; and the orthopedic surgeon best at knees, where her surgical experience has given her a certain expertise." (p. 317)
> **Function**: The local and particular character of metis — directly relevant to why the RI's ground-level knowledge of a specific village's land tenure is not transferable to a centralised system.

> "That authoritarian social engineering failed to create a world after its own image should not blind us to the fact that it did, at the very least, damage many of the earlier structures of mutuality and practice that were essential to metis." (p. 350)
> **Function**: Complication — even failed high-modernist projects leave damage behind. The destruction of informal tenure practices under digitisation may be irreversible even if DILRMP itself is never completed.

### Concept: Facts on Paper vs. Facts on the Ground

> "We must never assume that local practice conforms with state theory." (p. 49)
> **Function**: The maxim — the thesis's fieldwork programme is the empirical demonstration of this claim.

> "The land-office records may serve as the basis for taxation, but they may have little to do with the actual rights to the land. Paper owners may not be the effective owners." (p. 49)
> **Function**: Definition — paper ownership and effective possession as systematically divergent categories.

> "There will always be a shadow land-tenure system lurking beside and beneath the official account in the land-records office." (p. 49)
> **Function**: Extension — the shadow system is not anomalous; it is structurally produced by the limits of formal legibility.

> "Customs are better understood as a living, negotiated tissue of practices which are continually being adapted to new ecological and social circumstances — including, of course, power relations." (p. 34)
> **Function**: The ground-truth of tenure is not a stable fact but a dynamic social negotiation — the cadastral map's snapshot can never fully capture it.

> "The gulf between land tenure facts on paper and facts on the ground is probably greatest at moments of social turmoil and revolt. But even in more tranquil times, there will always be a shadow land-tenure system lurking beside and beneath the official account in the land-records office." (p. 49)
> **Function**: Extension — the paper-ground gap is not exceptional but permanent; it is magnified by social disruption and reduced (not eliminated) by relative stability.
