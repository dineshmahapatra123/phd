# Quote Bank — Land Reforms to Land Titling: Emerging Paradigms of Land Governance in India

---

### A. By Thesis Chapter

**Ch. 1.1 — Conceptual and analytical framework**

> "By secure property rights, it is understood as the clearly measurable, enforceable, transferable and litigation-free property rights in land." (p. 16)
> **Supports**: Defines the neoliberal property rights frame used in the thesis framework.

> "many individuals will work, live and play in the next century will be governed by and managed by mixed systems of communal and individual property rights." (p. 295)
> **Supports**: Grounds the mixed-property critique of exclusive individualisation.

**Ch. 2.1 — Land records and their functions**

> "Land Records are ... data information or maps regarding physical, legal, economic or environmental characteristics concerning land, water, groundwater, sub-surface, resources or air within a particular area." (p. 15)
> **Supports**: Establishes the multi-dimensional scope of land records beyond ownership lists.

**Ch. 2.2.4 — Contemporary and neoliberal approaches**

> "The Torrens system is 'defined as a system of registering land which registers title instead of instruments as evidences of title'." (p. 40)
> **Supports**: Defines the legal model that underpins conclusive title reforms.

> "The Torrens system is not state guaranteeing of land title, rather the state declares indefeasibility of title so that it cannot fail." (p. 41)
> **Supports**: Shows the doctrinal logic and its limits.

> "The Torrens system cannot register all the interests in the land." (p. 52)
> **Supports**: Identifies structural incompleteness in title registration.

**Ch. 2.3.2 — Colonial settlements and land administration**

> "The RoR of land in India, as a legacy of the colonial system, remains presumptive," (p. 13)
> **Supports**: Connects presumptiveness to colonial institutional legacies.

> "It means that entries in the RoR or title documents such as ownership, classification and extent of land and boundary are presumed to be correct until contrary is proved." (p. 13)
> **Supports**: Captures the evidentiary nature of presumptive records.

> "Thus, all the land reform or survey settlement laws governing the RoR declare the record unambiguously as presumptive value but with the value of conclusive evidence in any court of law and government institution." (p. 223)
> **Supports**: Shows statutory grounding for presumptiveness across states.

> "No conclusive evidence has so far come out against the working of the existing presumptive nature of land records and ownership document that warrants a radical change." (p. 294)
> **Supports**: Challenges the empirical case for abandoning presumptiveness.

**Ch. 2.4 — Legal and institutional infrastructure for land records**

> "The provisions of the draft land titling bill clearly take the interests of the propertied classes solely into account and ignore the interests of landless persons such as tenants, women and others secondary interests holders." (p. 239)
> **Supports**: Shows distributive bias in the proposed legal architecture.

> "The gender issues in the family and personal laws have appeared as the biggest constraint to land rights for women in all land title regimes." (p. 226)
> **Supports**: Points to legal barriers embedded in personal law regimes.

> "One of the biggest challenges to the secure and clear individual land title in tribal areas is the persistence of legal sanctity of communitybased landownership despite growing individualization of landownership." (p. 229)
> **Supports**: Highlights legal pluralism in Scheduled Areas.

**Ch. 2.5 — Genesis of DILRMP**

> "On 21 August 2008, India took a historic step to move from the present system of presumptive property titles to the system of the so-called clear property titles known as the 'Torrens system'." (p. 2)
> **Supports**: Marks the formal policy pivot toward conclusive title.

> "The main objective of the NLRMP is to develop a modern, comprehensive and transparent land records management system in the country with the aim to implement the conclusive land-titling system with title guarantee." (p. 8)
> **Supports**: Sets the programme's declared purpose.

> "The Union government asserts that the NLRMP is the biggest e-governance programme in the world given its geographical size, population, existence of millions of landholdings and parcels in digitization exercise." (p. 9)
> **Supports**: Positions NLRMP/DILRMP as a large-scale state project.

**Ch. 3.1 — Land governance under neoliberalism**

> "The era of 'land liberalization' has been ushered in the Indian policy process, thanks to economic liberalization." (p. 6)
> **Supports**: Frames land policy within broader neoliberal reforms.

> "The rules and regulations restricting the transfer or alienation of lands are being liberalized." (p. 6)
> **Supports**: Indicates policy movement toward market facilitation.

> "failure of Indian land reforms, then, resulted from a failure to institutionalise elements of a lower class interests within the state." (p. 7)
> **Supports**: Connects land reform failure to class-institutional limits.

> "the land reform in India as 'one of continuous political failure'." (p. 7)
> **Supports**: Summarises the political economy argument of reform failure.

> "The World Bank's land policy approaches land as commodity and the role of the state is to secure and enforce property contracts and liberalize land market." (p. 56)
> **Supports**: Articulates the neoliberal policy template.

> "Evidence of improved access of credit due to formalization of land is scant". (p. 55)
> **Supports**: Challenges the Bank's credit-access promise.

**Ch. 3.2.1 — Design of DILRMP**

> "The programmes initiated since 1989 by the government cover several components such as digitization of various types of land records and property ownership registers." (p. 8)
> **Supports**: Identifies the programme's structural components.

**Ch. 3.2.2 — Integration of RoR, cadastral maps, registration**

> "The main objective of the NLRMP is to develop a modern, comprehensive and transparent land records management system in the country with the aim to implement the conclusive land-titling system with title guarantee, which will be based on four basic principles, i.e., (i) a single window to handle land records (including the maintenance and updating of textual records, maps, survey and settlement operations and registration of immovable property), (ii) the mirror principle, which refers to the fact that cadastral records mirror the ground reality, (iii) the curtain principle which indicates that the record of title is a true depiction of the ownership status, mutation is automated and automatic following registration and the reference to past records is not necessary, and (iv) title insurance, which guarantees the title for its correctness and indemnifies the title holder against loss arising on account of any defect therein." (p. 8)
> **Supports**: Sets the technical integration logic and its assumptions.

> "Integrated Land Information Management System with automated updation of land records on mutation (process of correction of land records owing to sale, gift, etc.,)" (p. 9)
> **Supports**: Captures the promised technical workflow of integration.

> "The mirror principle, in which the document relating to a land recorded in the registry is an exact reflection of ground reality. The curtain principle, in which the prospective buyer of the land need not search the history of transactions as is done in the deeds system as the land recorded in the registry gives a clear picture of ownership. In the insurance principle, the government would indemnify the losses incurred due to the errors in the registry to the claimant." (p. 43)
> **Supports**: States the internal theory of title registration.

**Ch. 3.2.3 — Policy shift to conclusive titling and its challenges**

> "The state in India has made a 'historic decision' to switch over from the presumptive nature of land records and ownership to that of a guaranteed title to land or conclusive land title regime by ushering the 'land liberalization' regime." (p. 4)
> **Supports**: Defines the shift as a state-led policy choice.

> "The conclusive land title or guaranteed title to land implies that the state provides title guarantee, indefeasibility and indemnification of the title to the registered title-holder and the prospective buyer." (p. 16)
> **Supports**: Defines the policy target of conclusive titling.

> "There is no such 'magic' of securing the land to the registered owner and nothing like guaranteed title to land." (p. 50)
> **Supports**: Challenges the core promise of title security.

> "We have considered the question of introducing the Torrens system, the essence of which is the state guaranteeing of title of landowners. Considering the heavy load of work, expense and risks involved in introducing the system with over 971 million landowners owning some 500 million parcels of land, we do not recommend the adoption of the Torrens system." (p. 221)
> **Supports**: Signals feasibility limits in India.

**Ch. 3.4 — Technocratic reform critique**

> "Land market such as land rental and collateral cannot be developed through digitization and conclusive titling." (p. 244)
> **Supports**: Separates digitisation from market outcomes.

> "Informal sources of getting finance are popular due to lower paper work and transaction cost." (p. 245)
> **Supports**: Shows why formal titles do not shift finance behaviour.

> "The discourse of land titling policy is quintessentially a political project of creating market-oriented land institutions but to be attained through apolitical and techno-managerial strategies." (p. 292)
> **Supports**: Names the depoliticised character of reform strategy.

> "The sole objective is, to refer to James C. Scott, to improve the legibility of the property system and to ultimately switch over from the presumptive land records system to a guaranteed title to land which would improve the functioning of land market in India." (p. 291)
> **Supports**: Frames DILRMP as a legibility project.

**Ch. 4.5 — Progress of DILRMP and challenges**

> "In Himachal Pradesh and Maharashtra, the sample studies on concordance with digital records and field verification on ownership, possession, extent of land parcels and land uses and classification reveal wide discrepancies." (p. 219)
> **Supports**: Shows gaps between digital records and field reality.

> "Since the mid-fifties when measures of land reform were initiated, this work has been badly neglected. The neglect was in some cases deliberate... in several states the Record of Rights (RoRs) is invariably out-of-date because mutations are not effected promptly...." (p. 221)
> **Supports**: Documents institutional decay that digitisation must confront.

> "The Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme (DILRMP) is a technology driven programme involving application of diverse technologies. Being a technology driven and complex programme, the states and Union Territories are facing the challenge of finalizing technology and applying it in the field." (p. 289)
> **Supports**: State acknowledgement of implementation constraints.

**Ch. 3.1.2 — International institutions and land**

> "The World Bank is not a simply an ordinary bank, rather, through its conditional donor policy and advocacy studies, it has come to occupy a critical position in shaping the land policy for India and for the developing countries." (p. 243)
> **Supports**: Directly links institutional agenda-setting to India.
