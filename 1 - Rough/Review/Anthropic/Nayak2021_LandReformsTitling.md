# Land Reforms to Land Titling: Emerging Paradigms of Land Governance in India
**Author(s)**: Pradeep Nayak | **Year**: 2021 | **Type**: Book

---

### 1. Core Argument

Nayak argues that India is quietly moving from a presumptive records regime to a conclusive title regime through a market-oriented, technocratic reform agenda that has bypassed democratic debate. He frames this as a neoliberal reorientation of land policy that prioritises legibility, marketability, and title security over redistributive reform and social justice. The book insists that this policy shift cannot be understood as a purely legal or administrative update, because it reorders land relations, redistributes risks, and reshapes state power. Its governing claim is that “The state in India has made a ‘historic decision’ to switch over from the presumptive nature of land records and ownership to that of a guaranteed title to land or conclusive land title regime by ushering the ‘land liberalization’ regime.” (p. 4)

---

### 2. Conceptual Architecture

**Presumptive title**: “It means that entries in the RoR or title documents such as ownership, classification and extent of land and boundary are presumed to be correct until contrary is proved.” (p. 13) This defines the evidentiary, non-final character of current land records and underpins the book’s critique of a sudden shift to conclusiveness.

**Conclusive title**: “The conclusive land title or guaranteed title to land implies that the state provides title guarantee, indefeasibility and indemnification of the title to the registered title-holder and the prospective buyer.” (p. 16) This is the institutional ideal that the DILRMP and draft titling bills aim to realise.

**Secure property rights**: “By secure property rights, it is understood as the clearly measurable, enforceable, transferable and litigation-free property rights in land.” (p. 16) This operationalises the neoliberal vision of land as a tradable asset.

**Land records**: “Land Records are … data information or maps regarding physical, legal, economic or environmental characteristics concerning land, water, groundwater, sub-surface, resources or air within a particular area.” (p. 15) This expansive definition makes clear that land records are more than ownership lists.

**Land liberalization**: “The era of ‘land liberalization’ has been ushered in the Indian policy process, thanks to economic liberalization.” (p. 6) This concept positions the titling shift inside broader political economy change.

**Reform by stealth**: “The land titling as a neoliberal reform is being pursued by ‘cloaking change in the guise continuity’” (p. 12) This captures the political strategy that displaces public debate.

**Commons and mixed property**: “The commons scholars argue that ‘all the world is a semi commons’ in which we find a mixed system of property rights.” (p. 39) This grounds the book’s scepticism of rigid individualisation.

**Torrens system**: “The Torrens system is ‘defined as a system of registering land which registers title instead of instruments as evidences of title’.” (p. 40) This is the legal model guiding India’s conclusive title ambitions.

**Mirror, curtain, insurance principles**: “It has three principles ... These are mirror principle, in which the document relating to a land recorded in the registry is an exact reflection of ground reality. The curtain principle, in which the prospective buyer of the land need not search the history of transactions as is done in the deeds system as the land recorded in the registry gives a clear picture of ownership. In the insurance principle, the government would indemnify the losses incurred due to the errors in the registry to the claimant.” (p. 43) These principles organise the promised benefits and the risks of title registration.

---

### 3. Empirical Terrain

The book works across Indian policy documents, five-year plans, legal acts, land revenue reports, and international case studies to show how titling is embedded in neoliberal governance. Its primary empirical anchor is the Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme and its predecessor schemes, captured in the statement: “The main objective of the NLRMP is to develop a modern, comprehensive and transparent land records management system in the country with the aim to implement the conclusive land-titling system with title guarantee.” (p. 8) It supplements this with administrative evidence of implementation constraints and policy admissions, for example: “The Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme (DILRMP) is a technology driven programme involving application of diverse technologies. Being a technology driven and complex programme, the states and Union Territories are facing the challenge of finalizing technology and applying it in the field.” (p. 289) The book also mobilises committee reports to show institutional bottlenecks and the limits of digitisation as reform.

---

### 4. Theoretical Lineage

Nayak situates the work against neoliberal property-rights theory, World Bank land policy, Hernando de Soto’s formalisation thesis, and commons scholarship. The genealogy is explicit: “Hernando de Soto’s work on The Mystery of Capital: Why Capitalism Triumphs in the West and Fails Everywhere Else (2000) is the most cited and read book in the recent times about a regime of secure property rights in land or land titling in developing countries.” (p. 32) He places the World Bank at the centre of policy diffusion: “The World Bank’s land policy approaches land as commodity and the role of the state is to secure and enforce property contracts and liberalize land market.” (p. 56) He also borrows from James C. Scott to explain state legibility: “The sole objective is, to refer to James C. Scott, to improve the legibility of the property system and to ultimately switch over from the presumptive land records system to a guaranteed title to land which would improve the functioning of land market in India.” (p. 291) Finally, he counters neoliberal exclusivity with commons theory: “many individuals will work, live and play in the next century will be governed by and managed by mixed systems of communal and individual property rights.” (p. 295)

> **Gloss**: Land reform is embedded in wider economic policy change.

> **Gloss**: Policy change is portrayed as stealthy and politically evasive.

> “The main objective of the NLRMP is to develop a modern, comprehensive and transparent land records management system in the country with the aim to implement the conclusive land-titling system with title guarantee.” (p. 8)
> **Gloss**: The programme’s stated purpose is an explicit move toward conclusive title.

> “The mirror principle, which refers to the fact that cadastral records mirror the ground reality, (iii) the curtain principle which indicates that the record of title is a true depiction of the ownership status, ... and (iv) title insurance, which guarantees the title for its correctness and indemnifies the title holder against loss arising on account of any defect therein.” (p. 8)
> **Gloss**: The internal logic of the DILRMP is built on these title-registration promises.

**Theme: Record regimes and definitions**

> “Land Records are … data information or maps regarding physical, legal, economic or environmental characteristics concerning land, water, groundwater, sub-surface, resources or air within a particular area.” (p. 15)
> **Gloss**: Land records are multi-dimensional, not merely ownership registers.

> “It means that entries in the RoR or title documents such as ownership, classification and extent of land and boundary are presumed to be correct until contrary is proved.” (p. 13)
> **Gloss**: Presumption, not conclusiveness, is the historical legal status of RoRs.

> “The conclusive land title or guaranteed title to land implies that the state provides title guarantee, indefeasibility and indemnification of the title to the registered title-holder and the prospective buyer.” (p. 16)
> **Gloss**: Conclusive title is defined by state-backed assurance and indemnity.

> “By secure property rights, it is understood as the clearly measurable, enforceable, transferable and litigation-free property rights in land.” (p. 16)
> **Gloss**: The concept is explicitly tied to marketability and enforceability.

**Theme: Torrens system and its limits**

> “The Torrens system is ‘defined as a system of registering land which registers title instead of instruments as evidences of title’.” (p. 40)
> **Gloss**: The shift is from deeds to title, with the register itself as proof.

> “The Torrens system is not state guaranteeing of land title, rather the state declares indefeasibility of title so that it cannot fail.” (p. 41)
> **Gloss**: Even its advocates conceptualise the system as legal declaration, not infallibility.

> “There is no such ‘magic’ of securing the land to the registered owner and nothing like guaranteed title to land.” (p. 50)
> **Gloss**: The system’s promise of absolute security is directly rejected.

> “The Torrens system cannot register all the interests in the land.” (p. 52)
> **Gloss**: Unregistered interests remain a structural problem for conclusiveness.

**Theme: World Bank advocacy and critique**

> “Evidence of improved access of credit due to formalization of land is scant”. (p. 55)
> **Gloss**: Even the Bank’s core promise lacks empirical support.

> “The World Bank’s land policy approaches land as commodity and the role of the state is to secure and enforce property contracts and liberalize land market.” (p. 56)
> **Gloss**: The policy thrust is market construction, not redistribution.

> “The World Bank is not a simply an ordinary bank, rather, through its conditional donor policy and advocacy studies, it has come to occupy a critical position in shaping the land policy for India and for the developing countries.” (p. 243)
> **Gloss**: The Bank is positioned as a decisive agenda-setter in land governance.

**Theme: Legal and institutional constraints in India**

> “We have considered the question of introducing the Torrens system, the essence of which is the state guaranteeing of title of landowners. Considering the heavy load of work, expense and risks involved in introducing the system with over 971 million landowners owning some 500 million parcels of land, we do not recommend the adoption of the Torrens system.” (p. 221)
> **Gloss**: A major committee rejects Torrens on administrative feasibility grounds.

> “Thus, all the land reform or survey settlement laws governing the RoR declare the record unambiguously as presumptive value but with the value of conclusive evidence in any court of law and government institution.” (p. 223)
> **Gloss**: Presumptiveness is embedded across state statutes.

> “The provisions of the draft land titling bill clearly take the interests of the propertied classes solely into account and ignore the interests of landless persons such as tenants, women and others secondary interests holders.” (p. 239)
> **Gloss**: Legal design privileges property holders over secondary claimants.

**Theme: Social constraints, gender, and tribal tenure**

> “The gender issues in the family and personal laws have appeared as the biggest constraint to land rights for women in all land title regimes.” (p. 226)
> **Gloss**: Gendered personal law is a structural barrier to secure title.

> “One of the biggest challenges to the secure and clear individual land title in tribal areas is the persistence of legal sanctity of communitybased landownership despite growing individualization of landownership.” (p. 229)
> **Gloss**: Communitarian tenure is legally entrenched and resistant to titling.

**Theme: Land markets and collateral limits**

> “Land market such as land rental and collateral cannot be developed through digitization and conclusive titling.” (p. 244)
> **Gloss**: Title reform does not automatically generate land markets.

> “Informal sources of getting finance are popular due to lower paper work and transaction cost.” (p. 245)
> **Gloss**: Credit systems do not pivot to formal titles even where records improve.

**Theme: Techno-managerialism and legibility**

> “The Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme (DILRMP) is a technology driven programme involving application of diverse technologies. Being a technology driven and complex programme, the states and Union Territories are facing the challenge of finalizing technology and applying it in the field.” (p. 289)
> **Gloss**: The state itself acknowledges the complexity of the programme.

> “The sole objective is, to refer to James C. Scott, to improve the legibility of the property system and to ultimately switch over from the presumptive land records system to a guaranteed title to land which would improve the functioning of land market in India.” (p. 291)
> **Gloss**: The reform is framed as a legibility project with market aims.

> “The discourse of land titling policy is quintessentially a political project of creating market-oriented land institutions but to be attained through apolitical and techno-managerial strategies.” (p. 292)
> **Gloss**: Technocratic framing masks a political project.

> “No conclusive evidence has so far come out against the working of the existing presumptive nature of land records and ownership document that warrants a radical change.” (p. 294)
> **Gloss**: The push for conclusive title is not empirically compelled.

---

### 6. Internal Tensions and Silences

The book is forceful in criticising the technocratic framing of land titling, yet its own reliance on policy documents sometimes mirrors the same documentary rationality it critiques, leaving less space for micro-level evidence on how records work in practice. It repeatedly stresses that title security is not guaranteed, but it still engages with a legalistic conception of security that may underplay informal and customary legitimacy. The critique of World Bank advocacy is strong, while the internal political coalitions inside Indian states that advance land liberalisation receive less sustained empirical treatment. There is also a tension between the argument that presumptive records are historically suited to complexity and the acknowledgement that records are outdated and often corrupted, which could be read as a case for reform that is not necessarily conclusive titling.

### 8. Ideas and Questions Sparked

The reform by stealth framing suggests a thesis subsection that tracks how policy continuity narratives are used to depoliticise institutional change. This advances Ch. 3.4 because it reframes DILRMP as political strategy rather than administrative reform.

If presumptive records are historically resilient because they accommodate complexity, then the proper reform question is not “how to make records conclusive” but “how to build verified dispute-resolution pathways that preserve plural rights.” This opens an argument about alternative institutional design for Ch. 3.2.3.

The book’s evidence that title cannot guarantee security without broader institutional capacity suggests a comparative test case for Odisha: does digitisation without resurvey worsen the RoR-ground reality gap? This is a question for fieldwork for Ch. 6.2.

The emphasis on gendered personal laws raises a methodological implication: land record surveys must track inheritance practices and marital status as ownership determinants. This advances Ch. 5.5 because it operationalises gendered disjunctures between RoR and possession.

The critique of land markets without reliable collateral infrastructure suggests a policy appendix that evaluates CERSAI and registry integration for Odisha. This advances Ch. 4.3.2 because it grounds the finance-link argument.

The World Bank’s role as agenda-setter implies a tracing exercise of how its policy prescriptions surface in Odisha’s land policy documents. This opens an argument about agenda diffusion for Ch. 3.1.2.
