## Core Argument

Byamugisha (1999) develops an economy-wide theoretical and conceptual framework linking land registration to financial development and, ultimately, economic growth. Building on Feder et al. (1988) and new institutional economics, the paper articulates five transmission linkages—(i) tenure security and investment incentives; (ii) land title, collateral and credit; (iii) land liquidity and deposit mobilization; (iv) land markets and allocative efficiency; and (v) labour mobility—that together explain how formalized land rights reduce information and transaction costs and thereby expand credit, investment and aggregate productivity.

## Key Concepts

- Information asymmetry and transaction costs as the central frictions.
- Collateralization of land: land as a credible, divisible form of collateral.
- Land liquidity: registration as a mechanism to unlock wealth embedded in land.
- Economy-wide spillovers: cross-sectoral credit channels, labour mobility, and reallocative land markets.
- Institutional quality: registry performance, foreclosure rules, and market depth as conditioning variables.

## Evidence & Reasoning

- The paper synthesises cross-country and case-study evidence (Feder et al., 1988; Feder & Nishio, 1998) and presents a country case (Thailand) as an application of the framework.
- By formalising the multiple channels, it clarifies why empirical results are heterogeneous: benefits depend on complementary institutions (banks, foreclosure regimes, active land markets) and on the quality of registration services.
- Theoretical emphasis on net effects: while collateral lowers monitoring costs, it introduces collateral-specific transaction costs (valuation, title search, foreclosure) which must be netted when assessing gains.

## Policy Implications

- Donors and governments should prioritise not just issuance of titles but investments in registry quality, legal foreclosure procedures, and market infrastructure to realize economy-wide benefits.
- Sequencing matters: titling alone is unlikely to raise productivity absent credit market capacity and enforceable collateral recovery.
- Complementary reforms (reducing transaction costs, improving banking outreach, supporting land market liquidity) amplify titling benefits and reduce regressivity.

## Critique & Limitations

- Analytical clarity but limited causal identification: the framework is normative and conceptual; empirical validation depends on careful country studies and credible identification strategies.
- Possible omitted dynamics: distributional effects, capture by elites, and macro-financial risks from rapid mortgaging or land-price booms are acknowledged but not modelled in depth.
- Assumes functioning markets for foreclosure and resale; problematic in contexts with restricted transferability or weak legal enforcement.

## Thesis Relevance

- Useful theoretical scaffold for chapters on land titling and financialisation: maps mechanisms linking registration to credit, investment, and urban–rural reallocations.
- Use to justify empirical tests of conditional effects (interaction terms): registry quality × bank depth; titling × foreclosure efficiency.
