# Chapter 4 Reading Architecture

**Status:** Finalisation architecture, fully refreshed and cross-checked June 2026  
**Purpose:** Reading, note-making and drafting plan, not a bibliography to cite exhaustively

## Evolution of Land Rights and Land Records in Odisha

This architecture follows Chapter 4 in `Chapters_Sections.pdf` and the research problem in
`Proposal_Dinesh_Mahapatra.pdf`. Chapter 2 establishes the historical production of property,
territory and records in India. Chapter 3 reconstructs the national political economy of land
records modernisation. Chapter 4 must bring those arguments to Odisha by showing how a
historically heterogeneous tenure order was unified in law, maintained through revenue
administration, converted into digital infrastructure, and increasingly used to determine
access to farmer-welfare transfers.

This is not merely a state-level descriptive chapter. Its intellectual task is to identify the
institutional mechanisms that Chapters 5 and 6 will investigate at village, household and
parcel level.

## The Argument Chapter 4 Should Build

The chapter can advance the following argument:

1. Odisha inherited not one land system but a territorially uneven assemblage of Bengal
   zamindari, Madras ryotwari, Central Provinces revenue institutions and princely-state or
   Gadjat arrangements. The province created in 1936, and enlarged after the merger of the
   princely states, therefore inherited unequal record quality, administrative capacity and
   categories of right.
2. Post-independence legislation abolished intermediaries, regulated tenancy, imposed
   ceilings, consolidated holdings and created a formally uniform survey-and-settlement
   framework. Legal unification did not erase inherited regional differences or the social
   relations embedded in earlier records.
3. Land reform and land-record administration were mutually constitutive. Redistribution,
   tenancy protection and government-land settlement became effective only when claims were
   surveyed, entered, mutated and defended in the RoR. Conversely, failures or reversals in
   record maintenance could nullify substantive reform.
4. Odisha's RoR is a presumptive administrative record rather than state-guaranteed title.
   Registration, mutation, possession, inheritance, partition, cadastral boundaries and civil
   title can consequently diverge. This creates a chain of institutional dependence in which
   citizens must move between the Sub-Registrar, Tahasil, Revenue Inspector, Amin, revenue
   courts and civil courts.
5. Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, e-Registration, e-Mutation and RCCMS have changed the speed,
   visibility and circulation of land information. They have not, by themselves, resolved
   legacy errors, oral partitions, concealed tenancy, unrecorded succession, contested
   commons, unsurveyed land or textual-spatial mismatch.
6. DILRMP progress must therefore be disaggregated into computerisation, digitisation,
   textual-spatial linkage, georeferencing, survey or resurvey, registration integration,
   continuous updating, record-room modernisation, court computerisation, ULPIN generation
   and effective correction. A high score on one component does not prove completion of the
   others.
7. Farmer-welfare schemes turn the RoR from evidence used in land administration into an
   eligibility infrastructure. PM-KISAN is explicitly landholder-based. Odisha's KALIA and
   CM-KISAN have attempted broader inclusion, including landless households and, in parts of
   their design, sharecroppers or actual cultivators. Yet verification increasingly depends on
   Bhulekh-authenticated land data, Aadhaar/e-KYC, NFSA family data, bank seeding and
   interoperable databases.
8. Integration creates both administrative capacity and cumulative exclusion. A stale or
   gender-biased RoR can now affect mutation, welfare eligibility, compensation and other
   benefits across multiple systems. The decisive question is therefore not whether Odisha
   has digitised records, but whether the modernised system can recognise and correct the
   socially produced gap between record, possession, cultivation and legitimate right.

## Direct Relationship to the Thesis Questions

Chapter 4 should provide Odisha-level answers and testable propositions concerning:

1. The historical origins of present ownership and tenancy patterns.
2. The relationship between post-independence land reform and record-making.
3. The institutions through which RoRs are created, maintained and contested.
4. The processes that generate gaps between registered transactions, mutation, map, record
   and possession.
5. The design and progress of Bhulekh, Bhunaksha and DILRMP in Odisha.
6. Whether integration and high-technology survey correct or reproduce inherited errors.
7. How land records affect CM-KISAN and PM-KISAN inclusion, exclusion and correction.
8. Why aggregate programme indicators require village- and parcel-level validation.

## Core Analytical Distinctions

Do not collapse the following:

| Distinction | Analytical importance |
|---|---|
| Tenure system / record system | Social rights and obligations are not identical to their documentary representation. |
| Legal reform / administrative implementation | An Act can create a right that remains unrecorded, unpossessed or unenforced. |
| Registration / mutation | Registration records a document or transaction; mutation updates the revenue record. |
| RoR / title | An RoR carries evidentiary weight but does not by itself finally adjudicate complicated title. |
| Textual record / cadastral map | A correct name in the khatian does not prove an accurate parcel boundary, and vice versa. |
| Digitisation / updating | Scanning or data entry can reproduce an obsolete analogue record. |
| Linkage / reconciliation | Connecting databases does not establish that their underlying facts agree. |
| Administrative coverage / substantive accuracy | A village counted as computerised may still contain stale succession, partition or possession entries. |
| Recorded owner / actual cultivator | This distinction is central to tenancy and farmer-welfare eligibility. |
| Error / exclusion | Not every exclusion is caused by an erroneous record; some are produced by scheme design. |

## Institutional Sequence to Trace

Use the same chain throughout the chapter:

`historical tenure -> survey and settlement -> creation of RoR and map -> transaction or
succession -> registration/application/report -> field verification -> mutation or correction
-> database update -> use by another programme -> grievance, appeal or adjudication`

At each stage ask:

- Which institution has jurisdiction?
- Which document or database is treated as authoritative?
- Who must initiate the process?
- What notice, hearing and field verification occur?
- What are the direct and indirect costs?
- Can the official decide title, or only maintain the record?
- Does correction update both Bhulekh and Bhunaksha?
- Does the corrected record propagate to welfare databases?
- Which claims remain outside the workflow?

## Evidence Discipline

| Evidence | What it can establish | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Act, rule or regulation | Formal rights, powers, procedure and jurisdiction | Routine implementation |
| Departmental manual or circular | Administrative workflow and official responsibility | Compliance in every Tahasil |
| Judgment | Legal interpretation in a dispute | General prevalence of the underlying problem |
| Bhulekh/Bhunaksha portal | Public availability of a digital representation | Accuracy, currency, possession or conclusive title |
| DILRMP MIS | State-reported component completion | Ground accuracy or citizen experience |
| Scheme guideline | Formal eligibility and verification design | Actual inclusion or exclusion |
| CAG audit | Documented systemic and database failures in the audited period | Present performance after later reforms |
| Household or parcel evidence | Correspondence between record, map, possession and benefit in study sites | Statewide prevalence without a representative design |

## Priority Key

- **A - Essential:** read closely and use in the chapter's principal argument.
- **B - Important:** read selectively for a subsection, comparison or counterargument.
- **C - Reference:** use for factual, legal or contextual verification.
- **Primary:** legislation, judgment, policy, manual, audit or official administrative data.

## 4.1 Odisha's Land Tenure History Through Selective and Thematic Lenses

### Purpose and scope

This section should not repeat Chapter 2's general history. It should explain only those
historical features necessary to understand Odisha's present RoRs, regional variation,
administrative categories and record-possession gaps.

Organise the historical narrative around five themes:

1. Territorial heterogeneity before and after the formation of Odisha.
2. Revenue collection as the original purpose of systematic records.
3. Conversion of cultivators, intermediaries and customary claimants into legal categories.
4. Uneven survey, settlement and village-accounting capacity.
5. Persistence of historical differences after statutory unification.

### Essential readings

1. **A** Nayak, Sashadhara. 2021. "Land Records in Odisha: A Historical
   Perspective." *Odisha Review*, May: 1-6.
   - The most efficient Odisha-specific chronology of precolonial record practices, Bengal,
     Madras, Central Provinces and princely-state legacies, the Odisha Survey and Settlement
     Act, and the movement to Bhulekh and DILRMP.
   - Read critically: it is an informed administrative history, not an independent evaluation.
   - Crucial admission: computerisation and textual-spatial linkage coexist with obsolete
     settlements, oral partitions, inaccurate maps and records that omit important burdens or
     proceedings.

2. **A** Mearns, Robin, and Saurabh Sinha. 1999. *Social Exclusion and Land
   Administration in Orissa, India*. World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 2124.
   - Foundational for the relationship among inherited tenure regions, land administration,
     transaction costs, gender, tenancy, commons, registration, mutation, survey and
     settlement.
   - Particularly valuable because it links law and institutions to village evidence.
   - Use its 1990s findings as a baseline to test change, not as a description of current
     administration.

3. **A** Patnaik, Sanjoy. 2008. *Status Report: Land Rights and Ownership in
   Orissa*. Bhubaneswar: Regional Centre for Development Cooperation and UNDP.
   - State-level synthesis on landlessness, government land, ceiling redistribution,
     Bhoodan, tribal and forest rights, women and alienation.
   - Useful bridge from historical reform to contemporary exclusion.

4. **A** Nayak, Pradeep. 2022. *The State and the Politics of Knowledge:
   E-Governance in India's Land Records Modernisation Programmes*. Cambridge
   University Press, especially chapter 4, "The Computerisation of Land Records
   Programme in Odisha," and chapter 6.
   - The strongest book-length institutional account of Odisha's land-record
     computerisation and its comparison with Karnataka.
   - Use for bureaucracy, informal actors, organisational networks and the limits of
     techno-managerial governance.

5. **A** Pati, Biswamoy. 1993. *Resisting Domination: Peasants, Tribals and the
   National Movement in Orissa, 1920-50*. Manohar.
   - Use selectively to recover agrarian hierarchy, tribal-peasant politics and the transition
     into the postcolonial order.

6. **B** Pati, Biswamoy. 2012. *South Asia from the Margins: Echoes of Orissa,
   1800-2000*. Manchester University Press.
   - Places Odisha's agrarian history within caste, tribe, region, colonialism and
     marginality.

7. **B** Pati, Biswamoy. 2014. *Tribals and Dalits in Orissa: Towards a Social
   History of Exclusion, c. 1800-1950*. Oxford University Press.
   - Essential corrective to an administrative history that treats record-making as socially
     neutral.

8. **B** Bailey, F. G. 1960. *Tribe, Caste and Nation: A Study of Political Activity
   and Political Change in Highland Orissa*. Manchester University Press.
   - Use for political mediation, status and land in highland Odisha.

9. **B** Bailey, F. G. 1963. *Politics and Social Change: Orissa in 1959*. University
   of California Press.
   - Early post-independence account of the social and political setting in which reform was
     administered.

10. **C** Government of Odisha. Relevant district gazetteers and settlement reports.
    - Use only for district-specific tenure systems, estate histories, settlement dates,
      terminology and administrative boundaries.
    - Prioritise the gazetteers for the eventual field-study districts.

### Historical source map

| Region incorporated into Odisha | Earlier institutional inheritance | Record implication to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| Coastal Odisha division | Bengal Permanent Settlement and zamindari institutions | Intermediary records, raiyati status, tenancy layers and zamindar-maintained records |
| Ganjam and Koraput regions | Madras Presidency, including ryotwari and zamindari areas | Village accountant traditions, patta practice and different mutation histories |
| Sambalpur and western areas | Central Provinces land-revenue and tenancy laws | Khewat/khatian forms, malguzari or village-head institutions and settlement chronology |
| Gadjat/princely states | State-specific regulations and headman-mediated revenue | Uneven survey quality, customary or collective claims and late preparation of individual RoRs |
| Scheduled and forested areas | Customary tenure overlaid by revenue and forest law | Unsurveyed cultivation, hill slopes, forest rights and limits of raiyati categories |

### Drafting proposition

The relevant path dependence is not simply that old records survive. It is that historically
different systems produced different administrative capacities, evidentiary conventions and
degrees of fit between local tenure and the categories later absorbed into a uniform RoR.

## 4.1.1 Post-Independence Land Reforms and Tenancy Legislations

### Argument to establish

Post-independence reform should be analysed as a sequence of right creation, right
restriction, record inscription and implementation. The central question is not only what the
law promised, but how a tenant, ceiling-surplus allottee, landless household, Scheduled Tribe
claimant, woman heir or forest dweller became legible in a revenue record and secured
possession.

### Core primary legislation

1. **A Primary** *Orissa Estates Abolition Act, 1951*.
   - Examine vesting, abolition of intermediary interests, continuity of tenants, retention of
     land by intermediaries and subsequent amendments.
   - Ask how abolition changed the state's informational and administrative responsibilities.

2. **A Primary** *Orissa Survey and Settlement Act, 1958* and *Orissa Survey and
   Settlement Rules, 1962*.
   - The central statutory framework for preparing and maintaining maps and RoRs.
   - Read sections and rules concerning the constitution of the RoR, presumptions, final
     publication, maintenance, correction, appeals and civil suits.

3. **A Primary** *Orissa Land Reforms Act, 1960* and *Orissa Land Reforms
   (General) Rules, 1965*, with amendments.
   - Read tenancy, rent, resumption, acquisition of raiyati rights, ceiling and restrictions on
     transfer as connected components.
   - Pay particular attention to how legal protection generated incentives to conceal leasing
     and rotate tenants.

4. **A Primary** *Orissa Government Land Settlement Act, 1962* and *Orissa
   Government Land Settlement Rules, 1983*, with current amendments.
   - Necessary for homestead settlement, wasteland distribution, categories of government
     land and the relationship between allotment, possession and RoR.

5. **A Primary** *Orissa Scheduled Areas Transfer of Immovable Property (by
   Scheduled Tribes) Regulation, 1956*, with amendments.
   - Examine restrictions, restoration, burden of proof and the administrative route for
     addressing tribal land alienation.

6. **B Primary** *Orissa Consolidation of Holdings and Prevention of Fragmentation
   of Land Act, 1972* and Rules.
   - Consolidation did more than rearrange parcels: it generated or republished records and
     shifted jurisdiction over rights disputes.

7. **B Primary** *Orissa Prevention of Land Encroachment Act, 1972* and Rules.
   - Use for the unstable boundary between government land, commons, encroachment and
     settlement with eligible landless households.

8. **B Primary** *Orissa Bhoodan and Gramdan Act, 1970*, associated rules and
   administrative reports.
   - Investigate the chain from donation to identification, settlement, possession and
     recording.

9. **B Primary** *Forest Rights Act, 2006* and Rules, read with Odisha implementation
   orders and progress reports.
   - Individual and community forest rights do not fit neatly into the conventional raiyati
     record. Trace whether and how recognised rights enter maps and revenue records.

10. **B Primary** *Hindu Succession Act, 1956*, especially the 2005 amendment,
    alongside applicable personal and customary law.
    - Use to analyse the difference between formal inheritance equality and mutation in
      women's names.

### Essential analytical readings

11. **A** Appu, P. S. 1996. *Land Reforms in India: A Survey of Policy, Legislation
    and Implementation*. Vikas.
    - National benchmark for implementation failure and state variation.

12. **A** Appu, P. S. 1975. "Tenancy Reform in India." *Economic and Political
    Weekly* 10 (33/35): 1339-1375.
    - Canonical analysis of eviction, concealment and the weak implementation of tenancy
      reform.

13. **A** Mearns and Sinha 1999.
    - Read the historical legal framework, land distribution, women, survey and settlement,
      registration, mutation, tenancy and commons as one institutional system.

14. **A** Sinha, B. K. 2014. "Land Reforms: Evidences of Reversal in Orissa."
    *Journal of Land and Rural Studies* 2 (2): 171-190.
    DOI: 10.1177/2321024914534048.
    - Action research in Puri showing how deletion or non-recognition in RoRs can reverse the
      material effects of redistribution.
    - An indispensable mechanism study: a beneficiary can possess an allotment document yet
      lose effective rights through record administration.

15. **A** Patnaik 2008.
    - Use for the state-level status of ceiling land, homesteads, Bhoodan, tribal rights,
      forest rights and women's ownership.

16. **A** Gaurav, Shailesh, and Srijit Mishra. 2016. "Land Ownership and Tenancy in
    Odisha." In Pulin B. Nayak et al., eds., *The Economy of Odisha: A Profile*.
    Oxford University Press.
    - Quantitative and institutional account of ownership, operational holdings and tenancy.

17. **B** Deo, Fanindam. 2011. "Land Distribution Programmes in Odisha: A Social
    Audit in Nuapada District." Consult the locally archived paper.
    - District-level evidence on allotment, possession and the implementation chain.

18. **B** Choudhury, P. R., and R. Mohapatra. 2021. "Enhancing Tribal Land Tenure
    Security in Odisha: OTELP-Landesa Partnership." SSRN.
    DOI: 10.2139/ssrn.3812978.
    - Institutional innovation addressing homesteadlessness, unsurveyed cultivation and
      tribal households through government-civil society collaboration.

19. **B** Ambagudia, Jagannath. 2010. "Tribal Rights, Dispossession and the State in
    Orissa." *Economic and Political Weekly*.
    - Use to situate protective law against continuing dispossession and state development
      projects.

20. **B** Pradhan, R. 2013. "Securing Land Rights for Women through Institutional
    and Policy Reforms." Landesa paper.
    - Useful for joint titling, identification of single women and the institutional barriers
      between legal entitlement and recorded ownership.

21. **B** World Bank. 2016. *Gender Equitable Land Governance in Odisha, India: An
    Analysis through VGGT-Gender Lens*.
    - Good-practice cases on joint homestead titles, Women Support Centres, women Revenue
      Inspectors, forest-right title holders and group leasing.
    - Treat it as a programme-oriented assessment and triangulate claims of success.

22. **B** World Bank. 2021. *Agricultural Land Leasing Reform in India: Strategies
    to Increase Poor Farmers' Access to Land and Related Benefits*.
    - Strong statement of the contemporary policy case for recording land-use rights and the
      welfare consequences of ownership-only RoRs.

### Land-reform implementation matrix to construct

| Reform | Intended beneficiary/right | Required record action | Typical failure mechanism | Present relevance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Estates abolition | Direct state-raiyat relationship and continuity of tenants | Vesting, recognition and new RoR | Eviction before recording; intermediary retention; disputed status | Origin of many raiyati and government-land claims |
| Tenancy reform | Rent regulation, security and possible raiyati rights | Identification and recording of tenant | Concealed oral tenancy; rotation; landlord resistance | Actual cultivator absent from Bhulekh and welfare lists |
| Ceiling redistribution | Land to landless beneficiaries | Declaration, allotment, demarcation, possession and mutation | Benami fragmentation; poor land; missing demarcation; deleted entry | Gap between patta, possession and current RoR |
| Government-land settlement | Homestead or cultivable land | Eligibility inquiry, settlement, patta and RoR | Encroachment, unsuitable parcel, delay, elite capture | Housing, welfare and women's titles |
| Tribal protection | Restriction and restoration of alienated ST land | Verification of status, transfer and possession; restoration entry | Informal transfer, coercion, prolonged litigation | Scheduled-area land security |
| Consolidation | Viable parcel structure and updated rights | Chaka allotment, map and republished RoR | Boundary and title objections; jurisdictional complexity | Source of present maps and revision litigation |
| FRA recognition | Individual/community forest rights | Claim map, title and record integration | Rejection, poor mapping, revenue-forest mismatch | Rights invisible in conventional RoR systems |
| Succession equality | Women's inheritance | Partition/relinquishment/succession mutation | Social pressure, non-mutation, family-level recording | Gendered exclusion from ownership-linked benefits |

### Required caution

Do not describe Odisha as having "banned tenancy" without statutory precision. Identify:

- which persons may lease out;
- the legal consequences of unauthorised leasing;
- the distinction between lease, sharecropping and labour arrangements;
- how later amendments changed the position;
- whether a proposed reform was actually enacted; and
- how officials and households describe practices that may not match legal terminology.

## 4.1.2 Landmark Court Cases and Their Influence on the Land Reform Trajectory

### Purpose

This subsection should not become a detached case digest. Select cases that altered or
clarified one of four institutional questions:

1. The constitutional legitimacy and reach of intermediary abolition.
2. The boundary between revenue records and adjudication of title.
3. Protection and restoration of tribal land.
4. Recognition of customary, forest and community claims.

### Constitutional and abolition cases

1. **A Primary** *K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa*, AIR 1953 SC 375.
   - Foundational challenge to the Orissa Estates Abolition Act and canonical statement on
     colourable legislation.
   - Read the actual judgment for which provisions survived or failed; do not cite the case
     merely for a constitutional slogan.

2. **A Primary** *Biswambhar Singh v. State of Orissa*, AIR 1954 SC 139.
   - Important for the construction and implementation of the Estates Abolition framework.

3. **B Primary** Later Supreme Court and Orissa High Court cases on trust estates,
   intermediaries, leases and settlement under the Estates Abolition Act.
   - Select only cases that illuminate the route by which pre-vesting claims entered
     post-abolition records.

### RoR, mutation and title

4. **A Primary** *Panu Behera v. Jitendra Das*, 54 (1982) CLT 393.
   - Cited in the Odisha Mutation Manual on limitation under section 42 after final
     publication of the RoR.

5. **A Primary** *Bhagabat Jena v. Gobardhan Patnaik*, 54 (1982) CLT 30.
   - Read with *Panu Behera* on correction suits and limitation.

6. **A Primary** *State of Orissa v. Pana Mali*, 1978 (1) CWR 209.
   - Cited in the Mutation Manual for the distinction between a correction claim and a suit
     based on title.

7. **A Primary** *Harihar Mohapatra v. Commissioner of Land Records and
   Settlement*.
   - Use for the grounds and limits of correction under Rule 34. Verify the report citation
     from the authoritative judgment before final drafting.

8. **A Primary** *State of Orissa v. Brundaban Sharma*, 1995 Supp (3) SCC 249.
   - Use for administrative power, fraudulent or invalid settlement and the proposition that
     fraud cannot become the foundation of a protected land claim.
   - Verify the precise factual and statutory context before generalising.

9. **B Primary** *Suraj Bhan v. Financial Commissioner*, (2007) 6 SCC 186, and
   later Supreme Court authorities reiterating that mutation entries do not create title.
   - National doctrinal comparator. The Odisha-specific procedure must still be derived from
     the Odisha Act, Rules and Manual.

### Tribal and community rights

10. **A Primary** *Amrendra Pratap Singh v. Tej Bahadur Prajapati*, (2004)
    10 SCC 65.
    - Central Odisha case on tribal land, adverse possession and the protective purpose of
      Scheduled Area regulation.

11. **A Primary** *Orissa Mining Corporation Ltd. v. Ministry of Environment and
    Forest*, (2013) 6 SCC 476.
    - Niyamgiri judgment recognising the institutional role of the Gram Sabha in determining
      community, habitat and religious rights under the FRA.
    - Use to show that authoritative land claims can arise through institutions outside the
      conventional revenue-record chain.

12. **B Primary** Odisha High Court decisions on Regulation 2 of 1956, restoration,
    limitation and evidentiary burdens.
    - Construct a small purposive sample rather than collecting dozens of repetitive cases.

### Case-analysis template

For every case retained in the final chapter, record:

| Field | Required note |
|---|---|
| Citation and court | Verify from official or recognised law report |
| Date and legal regime | Act, rule, regulation or constitutional provision |
| Material facts | What happened to the land and record |
| Claimant categories | Intermediary, raiyat, tenant, allottee, ST claimant, state, deity, company etc. |
| Record involved | Sabik/Hal RoR, map, lease, patta, mutation, consolidation record |
| Holding | Narrow legal proposition actually decided |
| Institutional consequence | Which authority gained or lost jurisdiction or discretion |
| Distributional consequence | Whose claim became easier or harder to establish |
| Later influence | Subsequent legislation, administrative practice or citation |

### Drafting discipline

The subsection should end by showing that courts did not merely resolve private disputes.
They defined the authority of the land record, the boundary between administrative and civil
adjudication, and the evidentiary conditions under which reform beneficiaries could defend
their claims.

## 4.1.3 Key Administrative Changes Shaping Land Tenure Patterns

### Argument to establish

Administrative change is part of agrarian change. Creation of new Tahasils, settlement
operations, consolidation, changes in village-level personnel, record classification,
registration-mutation integration and special survey alter the cost and probability of having a
claim officially recognised.

### Changes to trace

1. Formation of Odisha in 1936 and integration of differently administered territories.
2. Merger of princely states in 1948-49 and extension of state legislation.
3. Estates abolition and transfer of record responsibility from intermediaries to the state.
4. Statewide survey and settlement under the 1958 Act.
5. Consolidation operations and creation of Hal records and maps.
6. Expansion and reorganisation of districts, subdivisions, Tahasils and RI circles.
7. Decline or transformation of village-level offices such as Pradhan, Gountia, Sarbarakar,
   Karanam and Patwari, and emergence of the RI/ARI/Amin chain.
8. Computerisation under CLR and SRA&ULR, followed by NLRMP and DILRMP.
9. e-Registration, e-Mutation, RCCMS, online rent payment and digital service delivery.
10. Special or high-technology survey under the Odisha Special Survey and Settlement Act,
    2012.
11. Recent rationalisation or alteration of land classifications and record-correction rules.

### Essential readings and sources

1. **A** Nayak 2021.
2. **A** Mearns and Sinha 1999.
3. **A** Nayak 2022.
4. **A Primary** Odisha Revenue and Disaster Management Department annual reports,
   administrative reports, circular compilations and Tahasil manuals.
5. **A Primary** Odisha Board of Revenue reports and Director, Land Records and Surveys
   documents.
6. **B Primary** Odisha district gazetteers for field districts.
7. **B** Historical settlement reports cited by Nayak 2021, especially N. C. Behuria and
   R. K. Ramadhayani.
8. **B** Pati's agrarian histories and Bailey's political anthropology, used to prevent an
   institution-only account.

### Administrative-change table to build

| Change | Formal objective | Information effect | Likely distributive effect | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| State territorial integration | Uniform governance | Harmonise categories and records | Older regional inequalities may persist | Settlement history and district comparison |
| Estates abolition | Remove intermediaries | State assumes direct records | Tenants recognised or evicted depending on inscription | Vesting files, RoRs and cases |
| Major/revisional settlement | Update rights and rent | New map and RoR | Opportunity for correction and dispossession | Draft/final records, objections, field testimony |
| Consolidation | Reduce fragmentation | Replace plots and republish rights | Revaluation of access and boundaries | Sabik-Hal correspondence |
| New Tahasil/RI circle | Administrative proximity | Changes record custody and workload | May improve access or fragment archives | Government notifications and interviews |
| Computerisation | Faster access and maintenance | Converts textual records into databases | Reduces search costs but freezes legacy entries | System design and record comparison |
| Special survey | Faster georeferenced resurvey | Produces new parcel geometry and RoR | Boundary disputes and treatment of commons | Notifications, technical manual, objections |
| Classification rationalisation | Simplify kisam/sattwa | Standardises categories | May alter conversion, valuation or use implications | Gazette notification and implementation files |

## 4.2 Institutional and Revenue Administration in Odisha

### Purpose

This section should provide the institutional anatomy necessary to understand where records
are made, changed and contested. It should be concrete enough to guide interviews and case
file sampling in Chapters 5 and 6.

### Institutional map

At minimum, trace the roles of:

- Revenue and Disaster Management Department;
- Board of Revenue, Odisha;
- Revenue Divisional Commissioner;
- Collector and District Magistrate;
- Sub-Collector;
- Tahasildar and Additional Tahasildar;
- Revenue Supervisor, Revenue Inspector and Assistant Revenue Inspector;
- Amin and other survey personnel;
- Inspector General of Registration, District Sub-Registrar and Sub-Registrar;
- Director of Land Records and Surveys / settlement and consolidation authorities;
- Odisha Space Applications Centre and NIC;
- revenue courts, consolidation authorities and civil courts;
- Common Service Centres, Mo Seva Kendras or other front-end service providers;
- village institutions, applicants, deed writers, lawyers, intermediaries and informal brokers.

### Essential sources

1. **A Primary** *Orissa Survey and Settlement Act, 1958* and Rules.
2. **A Primary** *Orissa Mutation Manual, 1962*, with amendments and current circulars.
3. **A Primary** Odisha Revenue and Disaster Management Department's compilation of
   circulars on e-Registration, e-Mutation, RCCMS, Bhulekh and Bhunaksha.
4. **A Primary** Registration Act, 1908; Odisha Registration Rules; Odisha amendments;
   Stamp legislation and IGR Odisha process material.
5. **A** Mearns and Sinha 1999, especially land administration and transaction-cost
   analysis.
6. **A** Nayak 2022.
7. **B Primary** Odisha Right to Public Services framework and notified timelines for
   revenue services.
8. **B Primary** Departmental training material for Tahasildars, RIs, ARIs and Amins.

### Analytical focus

Treat the administration as a polycentric chain, not a single office. The central empirical
problem is how responsibility is distributed when:

- a deed is registered but mutation is not completed;
- the textual RoR changes but the map does not;
- possession differs from both;
- an inheritance is socially accepted but not recorded;
- the applicant lacks a link document;
- a transaction predates the current final RoR;
- land is recorded as government, forest, deity, Bebandobasta or another special status;
- the welfare database cannot authenticate Bhulekh details; or
- a citizen must choose between mutation, revision and a civil suit.

## 4.2.2 Process of Mutation, Partition and Record Correction

### Central argument

### Process maps to create

#### A. Registered sale

`deed preparation -> registration and stamp compliance -> electronic transmission/intimation
-> mutation case -> notice -> field or record verification -> Tahasildar order -> correction
of RoR/map -> issue of corrected patta -> propagation to Bhulekh/Bhunaksha`

#### B. Inheritance

`death -> application or suo motu report -> proof of death and genealogy -> notice to heirs
-> objection/no objection -> order -> joint or partitioned mutation -> map correction if needed
-> welfare database update`

#### C. Partition

`family arrangement or registered partition -> application -> determination of shares ->

#### D. Record correction

`alleged wrong entry -> identify statutory ground -> jurisdiction screening -> notice and

### Mutation case-file extraction sheet

For a purposive sample, record:

- case type and source of initiation;
- dates of event, registration, application, institution and disposal;
- applicant's caste, gender and relation to recorded tenant, where ethically available;
- plot, khata, area, kisam and sattwa;
- documents demanded and documents unavailable;
- notice and objectors;
- RI/Amin report and whether a field visit occurred;
- whether title was disputed;
- reason for approval, rejection or pendency;
- whether both textual and spatial records changed;
- time and visits required;
- informal payments or intermediary use, based on interview evidence;
- effect on PM-KISAN, CM-KISAN, compensation, credit or other entitlement.

### Key proposition for Chapters 5-6

The meaningful unit of administrative performance is not disposal of a mutation case. It is
successful reconciliation of lawful claim, possession, textual record, parcel map and
downstream databases at tolerable cost.

## 4.4 Land Records Modernisation and Cash Transfer Schemes

### Central argument

The welfare significance of land records lies in a change of function. A record originally
maintained for revenue, property administration and evidence now becomes an input into
automated eligibility determination. This increases the material consequences of record
quality and changes the political constituency for mutation and correction.

### Analytical model

`RoR attributes + identity + family definition + residence + bank status + exclusion
databases + scheme rule = eligibility decision`

An exclusion can therefore arise from:

1. absence from the RoR;
2. stale succession or partition;
3. tenancy not recognised in the RoR;
4. incorrect land size or classification;
5. identity or spelling mismatch;
6. family-deduplication rule;
7. rural/urban classification;
8. Aadhaar/e-KYC or NPCI failure;
9. ration-card/NFSA mismatch;
10. scheme-specific exclusion despite a correct land record.

## 4.4.1 CM-KISAN / PM-KISAN

### Scheme chronology to establish

1. Odisha introduced KALIA in December 2018.
2. PM-KISAN took effect from 1 December 2018 and was launched nationally in February
   2019.
3. KALIA's original design included cultivators, sharecroppers and landless agricultural
   households through multiple components and database sources.
4. Odisha introduced CM-KISAN for financial year 2024-25.
5. CM-KISAN and PM-KISAN are now designed for portal and API integration, including
   sharing of land details, e-KYC and NPCI-seeding status.

### CM-KISAN design

The official 2024-25 guideline/SOP states:

- support of Rs 2,000 per crop season, Rs 4,000 annually, for eligible small and marginal
  cultivators;
- Rs 12,500 in livelihood support to eligible landless agricultural households through
  specified activities and tranches;
- a Krushi Vidya Nidhi scholarship component;
- Odisha domicile;
- exclusion parameters involving age, identity mismatch, medium/large holdings, duplicate
  family membership, tax or government-employment status, death, specified public offices
  and professions, urban residence and invalid ration-card status;
- registration through Krushak Odisha and Bhulekh-authenticated land details for farmers;
- a transition toward PM-KISAN-style mandatory e-KYC, land seeding and NPCI seeding.

One version of the guideline expressly lists sharecroppers/actual cultivators among eligible
categories, while the SOP's abbreviated eligibility list foregrounds small/marginal farmers
and landless agricultural households. This internal documentary variation must be resolved
through the latest operative order and implementation practice before making a definitive
claim.

### PM-KISAN design

The official operational guidelines establish:

- eligibility based on a landholding farmer family with cultivable land in its name, subject
  to exclusion categories;
- reliance on state land records and state administrative machinery;
- a cut-off and special rules for ownership change, especially succession;
- responsibility for mutation and updating eligibility when land rights change;
- Aadhaar/e-KYC and bank/payment requirements introduced or strengthened over time.

1. **A Primary** Government of Odisha. 2024. *Guideline of CM-KISAN*.
2. **A Primary** Government of Odisha. 2024. *Standard Operating Procedure /
   Guidelines for Registration of Farmers under CM-KISAN and PM-KISAN*.
3. **A Primary** CM-KISAN portal FAQs, registration and grievance documentation.
4. **A Primary** Government of India. Revised PM-KISAN Operational Guidelines.
5. **A Primary** PM-KISAN clarifications on transfer, succession and land-record
   updating.
6. **A Primary** PM-KISAN FAQs and dashboard, with retrieval date.
7. **A Primary** CAG 2024, KALIA compliance audit.
8. **B Primary** Odisha Agriculture and Farmers' Empowerment Department KALIA
   resolutions, implementation-committee minutes and database protocols.
9. **B** Samagra and other implementation-partner accounts of KALIA.
   - Useful for system design and insider reconstruction, but triangulate with government

### What the CAG audit contributes

For 2018-21, the audit documents:

- incomplete implementation of KALIA components;
- use of multiple farmer and welfare databases;
- inability initially to use Bhulekh because the database lacked a common key such as
  Aadhaar and farmer lists lacked khata and plot fields;

## 4.4.2 Dynamism Between RoRs from Bhulekh and Cash Transfer Eligibility

### Central research problem

"Dynamism" should mean two-way institutional interaction:

1. Changes in the RoR alter eligibility, landholding category or household recognition.
2. Welfare verification creates pressure to mutate, partition, correct or seed the RoR.
3. Welfare databases may reveal inconsistencies but cannot legally adjudicate land rights.
4. API integration can speed propagation of correct data and also propagate an error.

### Mechanisms to examine

#### Succession

A deceased person remains in Bhulekh; heirs cultivate but have not mutated. Determine:

- whether the benefit stops;
- whether the account continues temporarily;
- which heir applies;
- whether all heirs must be recorded;
- how family duplication is handled; and
- how long the correction takes to reach each portal.

#### Oral partition

Brothers cultivate separate parcels but remain jointly recorded. Determine:

- whether each household qualifies independently;
- whether acreage is allocated by recorded share or actual possession;
- whether NFSA family data and RoR family structure conflict; and
- whether welfare incentives encourage formal partition.

#### Concealed tenancy/sharecropping

The owner appears in Bhulekh while another household cultivates. Determine:

- which scheme recognises the cultivator;
- what proof is accepted;
- whether claiming cultivation threatens the tenancy arrangement;
- whether the owner receives an ownership-based benefit; and
- whether the system risks double support or complete exclusion.

#### Women's claims

Land remains in a deceased father-in-law's or husband's name. Determine:

- whether a widow or daughter can register without completed mutation;
- how joint ownership and family deduplication work;
- whether a woman cultivator is treated as farmer or dependent; and
- whether welfare access motivates or discourages assertion of inheritance.

#### Tribal, forest and common-property rights

Determine whether:

- FRA titles are recognised in Bhulekh;
- community titles can generate individual farmer eligibility;
- land recorded as forest/government but cultivated under a recognised claim passes
  land-seeding checks; and
- non-individual rights remain invisible.

#### Text-map mismatch

If textual ownership passes but parcel geometry or area is wrong:

- which value determines small/marginal status;
- whether benefit calculation uses area;
- whether map correction is necessary; and
- whether ULPIN introduces a new failure point.

### Empirical data architecture

Create a linked, anonymised case table:

| Case ID | Bhulekh tenant | Possessor | Cultivator | Mutation status | Map match | Scheme applied | Portal status | Failure code | Resolution path |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Add event dates for:

- underlying transfer/death/partition;
- mutation application and order;
- Bhulekh update;
- scheme registration;
- land-seeding validation;
- grievance;
- payment or rejection.

This permits process tracing of whether administrative latency causes welfare exclusion.

### Hypotheses for village-level testing

1. Households with recent unmutated succession are more likely to fail land seeding or rely
   on the recorded deceased person's scheme status.
2. Women cultivators are more likely than men to be absent from both the RoR and
   ownership-linked farmer lists.
3. Tenants and sharecroppers depend on non-RoR routes, making recognition more
   discretionary.
4. Joint holdings and oral partitions create mismatch between revenue and welfare definitions
   of family.
5. Portal interoperability reduces processing time for standard registered transfers but does
   less for inheritance, oral partition and disputed possession.
6. Welfare stakes increase demand for mutation but may also increase strategic or contested
   claims to recorded ownership.

| D6 | Kisam or land use is obsolete |
| D7 | Government/forest/common status conflicts with household or community claim |
| D8 | Registered transaction not reflected in RoR |
| D9 | Mutation order not reflected in map or portal |
| D10 | Name, identity or share mismatch blocks welfare authentication |
| D11 | Woman's legal share absent or relinquished without meaningful choice |
| D12 | Multiple databases assign inconsistent family or beneficiary status |

### Chapter 4 concluding propositions

The section should end with a disciplined set of propositions:

1. Odisha has largely achieved digital conversion and online availability of legacy records.
2. The unfinished task is substantive reconciliation of record, map, possession, cultivation
   and lawful claim.
3. Historical tenure regions and post-independence reform implementation are likely to
   explain part of present spatial variation.
4. Routine mutation is the principal mechanism for continuous updating, but it is least suited
   to socially complex events such as oral partition, concealed tenancy and disputed
   succession.
5. Special survey and ULPIN can improve spatial identification but cannot by technology
   alone decide competing rights.
6. Welfare integration raises the cost of record error and makes correction a question of
   distributive citizenship.
7. Village- and parcel-level comparison is therefore not a supplement to DILRMP evaluation;
   it is required to determine whether administrative completion corresponds to lived tenure
   security.

## Final Drafting Architecture

A coherent final Chapter 4 can follow this narrative:

1. **Inherited diversity:** Odisha began with multiple tenure and record regimes.
2. **Postcolonial unification:** reform laws and statewide survey created a common legal
   framework but uneven implementation.
3. **Record as reform infrastructure:** rights depended on inscription, maintenance and
   possession.
4. **Administrative chain:** registration, mutation, partition, correction and adjudication
   distributed authority across institutions.
5. **Digital transformation:** Bhulekh, Bhunaksha and DILRMP changed access, speed and
   interoperability while retaining legacy content.
6. **Welfare turn:** RoRs became inputs to cash-transfer eligibility and database
   authentication.
7. **Unresolved empirical question:** whether high administrative coverage corresponds to
   accurate, current and socially inclusive records.
8. **Research transition:** Chapters 5 and 6 test that correspondence through village,
   household, case-file and parcel evidence.

The chapter's contribution is therefore to show how Odisha's historical agrarian order is
carried into contemporary governance through the land record, and how digitisation magnifies
both the administrative utility and the distributive consequences of that record.
