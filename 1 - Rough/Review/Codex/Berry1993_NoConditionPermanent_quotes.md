## A. By Thesis Chapter

### Chapter 1.1 — Conceptual and Analytical Framework

#### QB-01 — Access, Not Ownership, as the Analytical Starting Point
- Book location: Introduction, pp. 3-4.
- Relevant concept: [[Access to Land as Social Process]].
- Passage substance: Berry frames agrarian change around changing conditions of access to productive resources, not around ownership as a settled legal fact.
- How to use: Introduce a critique of narrow property-rights formalisation. Land records matter, but they sit inside wider social processes of access.
- Thesis sentence: The thesis should treat land records as one institutional layer in a broader field of negotiated access.

#### QB-02 — Property Rules Are Insufficient
- Book location: Introduction, pp. 3-4.
- Relevant concept: [[Property as a Social Relation]].
- Passage substance: Berry argues that resource use cannot be explained only by factor prices, property-right rules, or contract enforcement; power, obligation, authority, and meanings of exchange also matter.
- How to use: Use against an overly economistic reading of DILRMP as a transaction-cost solution.
- Thesis sentence: DILRMP may reduce some information costs while leaving the social production of land claims unresolved.

#### QB-03 — Fluid Institutions
- Book location: Introduction, pp. 6-7.
- Relevant concept: [[Informal Constraints]].
- Passage substance: Berry treats institutions and social boundaries as fluid, dynamic, and ambiguous, rather than fixed containers of behaviour.
- How to use: The Odisha parallel is methodological: land tenure categories should be examined as active social arrangements, not presumed stable because the record is stable.
- Thesis sentence: The apparent fixity of the RoR may conceal ongoing institutional fluidity on the ground.

#### QB-04 — Ambiguity and Keeping Claims Open
- Book location: Introduction, pp. 17-18.
- Relevant concept: [[Investment in Networks]].
- Short anchor: "keeping options open"
- Passage substance: When rules and transactions are negotiable, actors may prefer to preserve future room for manoeuvre rather than seek exclusive control immediately.
- How to use: Explain why families may delay partition, avoid mutation, tolerate joint records, or keep informal claims unresolved.
- Thesis sentence: Non-mutation may be a strategy, not only neglect.

### Chapter 1.2 — Review of Literature

#### QB-05 — Corrective to NIE Property Rights Theory
- Book location: Introduction, pp. 14-17; Chapter 5, pp. 101-104.
- Relevant concept: [[New Institutional Economics]]; [[Transaction Costs]].
- Passage substance: Berry criticises models that treat clearer property rights as sufficient to secure efficient resource allocation.
- How to use: Place Berry after Demsetz/Feder/Deininger as an agrarian political economy correction.
- Thesis sentence: Formalisation theory assumes that rights can be clarified; Berry asks what happens when clarification itself becomes a social struggle.

#### QB-06 — Link to Lund and Recognition
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 101-104.
- Relevant concept: [[Contracts of Recognition]]; [[Property as a Social Relation]].
- Passage substance: Berry shows that property claims depend on participation in adjudication and interpretation. Lund explains recognition by authority; Berry explains the social struggle behind that recognition.
- How to use: Pair Berry with Lund in the theory chapter.
- Thesis sentence: RoR recognition is institutionally produced, but the claims seeking recognition remain socially negotiated.

### Chapter 2.2.2 / 2.3.2 — Colonial Approaches to Land Records and Settlement

#### QB-07 — Colonial Rule Intensified Customary Debate
- Book location: Introduction, pp. 9-10; Chapter 2, pp. 22-42.
- Relevant concept: [[Colonial Legal Reconstitution of Property]].
- Passage substance: Berry argues that colonial rule did not simply freeze tradition; it intensified debate over custom, power, and property.
- How to use: Use carefully as a comparative support to Cohn. The point is not that African and Indian custom are equivalent, but that colonial recording can become a site of contestation.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial land administration often converted living custom into administrative categories while simultaneously creating new incentives to dispute those categories.

#### QB-08 — Written Records Do Not End Custom
- Book location: Introduction, pp. 9-10.
- Relevant concept: settlement records; customary claims.
- Passage substance: Berry rejects the idea that once traditions were written down, they stopped changing.
- How to use: Use for Survey and Settlement history: writing down a claim may make it durable, but also gives future disputants a new object to contest.
- Thesis sentence: The settlement record is not the end of social negotiation; it may become its archive and instrument.

#### QB-09 — Identity Determines Applicable Rules
- Book location: Chapter 2, pp. 34-39.
- Relevant concept: caste/community membership; jurisdiction.
- Passage substance: Colonial administrators often needed to classify a person's social identity before deciding which customary rule applied.
- How to use: Link to caste, tribe, tenancy category, raiyat status, and welfare eligibility in Odisha.
- Thesis sentence: Land administration often decides claims by first deciding who the claimant is.

### Chapter 3.4 — Technocratic Modernisation vs Political Economy of Reform

#### QB-10 — Formalisation Cannot Eliminate Social Claims
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 101-104.
- Relevant concept: [[Technocracy in Land Administration]].
- Passage substance: Berry shows that land rights are not made socially final merely by legal definition or formal policy.
- How to use: Use directly against a DILRMP-as-data-cleaning view.
- Thesis sentence: Digitisation can standardise the state record without resolving the social relationships that produce land access.

#### QB-11 — Registration May Become Another Arena
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 125-129.
- Relevant concept: [[Record-Reality Gap]].
- Passage substance: In Kenya, adjudication and registration did not simply settle ownership; they reopened group, family, and customary claims around the question of who should be registered.
- How to use: Strong comparative warning for conclusive titling and ULPIN: the pre-registration adjudication process matters as much as the digital end state.
- Thesis sentence: The struggle over who enters the register may be more politically consequential than the register's later technical cleanliness.

### Chapter 5.4 — Historical Comparison of Land Ownership and Access

#### QB-12 — Land Claims Carry Social Histories
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 104-107.
- Relevant concept: historical ownership; settlement; migration.
- Passage substance: Access to land was shaped by settlement histories, allegiance, migration, descent, and local authority, not just current use.
- How to use: Guide village history interviews: ask how families came to hold, possess, cultivate, or claim parcels.
- Thesis sentence: Current possession patterns may encode older histories of settlement, caste location, marriage, labour, and political patronage.

#### QB-13 — Locals, Strangers, Tenants
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 110-112.
- Relevant concept: tenancy; insider-outsider distinction.
- Passage substance: Tenant or stranger status was political as well as economic; payments could acknowledge jurisdiction, subordination, or belonging.
- How to use: Use as a conceptual comparator for informal tenants/sharecroppers or caste-differentiated village settlement in Odisha.
- Thesis sentence: Tenancy is not only an economic contract; it may also encode local status and recognition.

### Chapter 5.5 — Ownership vs Possession

#### QB-14 — Registered or Sold Land Can Remain Multi-Claimed
- Book location: Chapter 5, p. 104.
- Relevant concept: [[Record-Reality Gap]]; [[Access to Land as Social Process]].
- Passage substance: Berry notes that land formally sold or registered to an individual may still remain subject to multiple claims in practice.
- How to use: Core quote target for the ownership-possession chapter.
- Thesis sentence: A formal owner in the RoR may be only one claimant in a wider social field of possession and entitlement.

#### QB-15 — Ambiguity Is Not Just Insecurity
- Book location: Chapter 5, p. 104.
- Relevant concept: ambiguous tenure; adjudication.
- Passage substance: The significance of ambiguous rights lies in the fact that access depends on participation in interpretation and adjudication, not only on money or title.
- How to use: Distinguish administrative error from socially unresolved ownership.
- Thesis sentence: The record-reality gap may reflect the unfinished adjudication of social claims, not just faulty data.

#### QB-16 — Family Claims and Labour Contributions
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 115-117.
- Relevant concept: inheritance; gender; family property.
- Passage substance: Claims to land within families could be based on descent, inheritance, labour invested in land improvements, conjugal claims, and seniority.
- How to use: Use for inheritance and partition sections in village studies.
- Thesis sentence: Partition disputes often combine legal descent with claims based on labour, residence, marriage, and recognition by kin.

#### QB-17 — Many Courts, Many Verdicts
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 117-120.
- Relevant concept: jurisdictional plurality.
- Passage substance: Multiple judicial arenas, unclear jurisdiction, appeals, informal settlement, and unenforced decisions kept land rights open to renegotiation.
- How to use: Use for the split between revenue offices, registration offices, civil courts, panchayat mediation, local elites, and informal settlement.
- Thesis sentence: Land disputes move across institutions; each institution may recognise a different version of the claim.

### Chapter 5.6.2 — Mutation, Partition, and Correction

#### QB-18 — Registers Can Cease to Reflect Ground Affairs
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 126-129.
- Relevant concept: [[Mutation Lag]]; [[Record-Reality Gap]].
- Passage substance: After registration, many transactions were unrecorded, holdings were refragmented, and officials observed that registers were drifting away from ground realities.
- How to use: Directly relevant to Bhulekh/RoR updates and mutation lag in Odisha.
- Thesis sentence: A record can be accurate at the point of registration and still become socially obsolete as transactions, inheritance, and possession continue outside it.

#### QB-19 — Adjudication Reopens Group Claims
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 125-127.
- Relevant concept: adjudication; group rights.
- Passage substance: Before registration, adjudication required identifying legitimate owners; because customary claims presumed group rights, the process reopened dormant or disputed group claims.
- How to use: Useful for any argument that resurvey or conclusive titling will require politically difficult adjudication, not just technical mapping.
- Thesis sentence: The transition to cleaner records requires adjudicating social claims, not merely digitising plots.

### Chapter 5.7 — Governance, Discrepancies, and Gaps

#### QB-20 — Litigation as Accumulation Strategy
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 109-110; Chapter 7, pp. 162-164.
- Relevant concept: [[Investment in Networks]].
- Passage substance: Litigation could become a means of accumulating land, status, or influence, especially where courts and witnesses mattered.
- How to use: Use for revenue-office intermediaries, document writers, bribes, repeated petitions, and litigation costs.
- Thesis sentence: The land-record system creates not only records but a political economy of claim-making.

#### QB-21 — Social Recognition and Administrative Recognition Diverge
- Book location: Chapter 5, pp. 120-129.
- Relevant concept: recognition gap.
- Passage substance: Decisions and registrations could remain socially incomplete when families, elders, courts, or local authorities continued to recognise other claims.
- How to use: Use in analysis of disputes where RoR, possession, and local acceptance differ.
- Thesis sentence: A recorded claim may be administratively valid but socially incomplete.

### Chapter 5.8 — Welfare, Eligibility, and State Intervention

#### QB-22 — Access to the State Through Social Identity
- Book location: Introduction, pp. 19-20; Chapter 3, pp. 43-66.
- Relevant concept: welfare eligibility; recognition.
- Passage substance: Social identity and status become channels of access to state resources and opportunities.
- How to use: Extend carefully to PM-Kisan/CM-Kisan/KALIA: land-record identity can become a gate to welfare.
- Thesis sentence: When welfare is tied to land records, access to state benefits depends on whether social claims become administratively recognisable.

## D. Fieldwork Prompts Derived from Berry

1. Who is named in the RoR, and who actually possesses or cultivates the parcel?
2. Who else can make a claim on this parcel, and on what basis?
3. Has the family avoided mutation or partition? If yes, why?
4. Is ambiguity useful to anyone in the household or village?
5. Which social relationships help someone correct a record: kin, caste, party, broker, RI, Amin, Tehsildar, lawyer?
6. What costs are involved in making a land claim: money, time, witnesses, gifts, documents, litigation, travel?
7. Does local recognition differ from administrative recognition?
8. Are women, tenants, sharecroppers, or absent heirs recognised locally but absent from the record?
9. Did digitisation change the claim itself, or only the document through which the claim is seen?
10. When there are multiple claims, who benefits from keeping the record as it is?
