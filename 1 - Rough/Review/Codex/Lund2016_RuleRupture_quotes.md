### A. By Thesis Chapter

#### Ch. 1.1 — Conceptual and analytical framework

> "The state is always in the making." (p. 1199)  
**Supports**: Frames the state as an ongoing institutional process, not a finished container that simply holds land records.

> "Claims to rights prompt the exercise of authority." (p. 1201)  
**Supports**: Gives the thesis a concise mechanism for reading mutation, RoR correction, and welfare claims as authority-producing acts.

> "Rights and political authority are contemporaneous" (p. 1202)  
**Supports**: Challenges any linear model where the state exists first and only later distributes property rights.

#### Ch. 2.2.2 — Colonial approaches to land records

> "Colonization describes the most dramatic and violent rupture" (p. 1203)  
**Supports**: Places colonial land administration in a rupture framework rather than a neutral institutional-transfer framework.

> "New identities and new categories of property were produced" (p. 1203)  
**Supports**: Anchors the claim that colonial administration created property categories, not merely recorded pre-existing ones.

> "The Domesday Book established the sovereign" (p. 1203)  
**Supports**: Comparative evidence that recording property can also establish authority.

#### Ch. 2.3.2 — Colonial settlements and land administration in India

> "categorical distinctions as principles for recognizing or dismissing claims" (p. 1203)  
**Supports**: Useful for explaining how colonial settlement categories decide which claims are administratively valid.

> "post-colonial governments have... preserved... colonial patterns" (p. 1204)  
**Supports**: Supports the colonial continuity argument behind presumptive land records and postcolonial record formats.

#### Ch. 3.2.2 — Integration of RoR, cadastral maps, and registration

> "classification, registration and mapping" (p. 1205)  
**Supports**: Names the administrative triad that connects Lund to Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, cadastral maps, and ULPIN.

> "organize the social and political perception" (p. 1205)  
**Supports**: Shows that mapping and registration change how land is understood, not just how land is stored.

#### Ch. 3.3 — State welfare and land-record eligibility

> "the right to have rights" (p. 1205)  
**Supports**: Conceptual anchor for RoR as a welfare gate: recognition as landholder becomes recognition as eligible subject.

> "not being seen" (p. 1205)  
**Supports**: Useful for Aadhaar/RoR/Bhulekh visibility arguments: invisibility can mean exclusion from schemes.

> "not to be seen as a rights-bearing subject" (p. 1205)  
**Supports**: Strong phrase for describing tenants, women heirs, sharecroppers, and informal possessors outside the record.

#### Ch. 3.4 — Technocratic modernisation vs political economy of reform

> "the act of authorizing recursively authorizes the authorizer" (p. 1205)  
**Supports**: Explains why DILRMP strengthens state authority when citizens rely on digital records for proof.

> "effective recognition of rights by an institution" (p. 1206)  
**Supports**: Helps distinguish mere data entry from enforceable recognition.

#### Ch. 4.2 — Revenue and settlement administration in Odisha

> "identify the actual authorities" (p. 1202)  
**Supports**: Fieldwork instruction: map who villagers actually treat as authoritative in land disputes.

> "An individual is beholden to different institutions" (p. 1208)  
**Supports**: Fits Odisha's split between revenue, survey, registration, courts, panchayat, and local intermediaries.

#### Ch. 4.3 — Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, DILRMP

> "Territorialization and the spatial ordering of people" (p. 1205)  
**Supports**: Connects cadastral mapping to political ordering, not just technical survey.

> "regulate certain forms of mobility and transactions" (p. 1205)  
**Supports**: Useful for land-use conversion, parcel transfers, and restrictions tied to recorded status.

#### Ch. 5 — Village Studies: ground-level realities

> "Who can acquire, hold and transact property?" (p. 1211)  
**Supports**: Fieldwork question for caste, gender, insiders/outsiders, tenants, heirs, and possessors.

> "categories such as men/women, old/young, insiders/outsiders" (p. 1211)  
**Supports**: Direct coding vocabulary for village-level land-claim differences.

> "multiple relationships are therefore established" (p. 1208)  
**Supports**: Helps read one parcel as carrying multiple recognitions: RoR, deed, possession, family partition, local acknowledgement.

> "payment of property tax, registration in a census" (p. 1210)  
**Supports**: Suggests evidence villagers may use to prove residence, possession, or entitlement beyond formal title.

### B. By Active Debate

#### Debate 3 — Technocratic modernisation vs political economy of reform

> "Claims to rights prompt the exercise of authority." (p. 1201)  
**Position**: Supports the political-economy view that record modernisation is not neutral; it restructures claim-authority relations.

> "the act of authorizing recursively authorizes the authorizer" (p. 1205)  
**Position**: Complicates the technocratic view by showing that digital recognition also reproduces state authority.

#### Debate 4 — Colonial revenue record to postcolonial title document

> "New identities and new categories of property were produced" (p. 1203)  
**Position**: Supports the argument that colonial records constituted property categories rather than neutrally recording them.

> "post-colonial governments have... preserved... colonial patterns" (p. 1204)  
**Position**: Supports the continuity claim central to the thesis.

#### Debate 5 — Spatial-textual disjuncture

> "classification, registration and mapping" (p. 1205)  
**Position**: Supports treating spatial and textual records as linked recognition technologies.

> "organize the social and political perception" (p. 1205)  
**Position**: Complicates the assumption that map digitisation is merely technical.

#### Debate 6 — RoR as welfare inclusion vs exclusion mechanism

> "the right to have rights" (p. 1205)  
**Position**: Supports reading land-record recognition as a gateway to welfare citizenship.

> "not to be seen as a rights-bearing subject" (p. 1205)  
**Position**: Supports the exclusion half of the debate: unrecorded claimants become weak or invisible.

#### Debate 8 — Ownership vs possession on the ground

> "Property is... a legitimized claim" (p. 1204)  
**Position**: Complicates ownership by treating property as recognition, not just title or possession.

> "Who can acquire, hold and transact property?" (p. 1211)  
**Position**: Supports investigating the gap between legal status and actual social capacity to transact.

### C. By Concept

#### Concept: Contracts of Recognition

> "Authority and rights are conceptually tied together by recognition." (p. 1199)  
**Function**: Defines the article's core conceptual bridge.

> "effective recognition of rights by an institution" (p. 1206)  
**Function**: Clarifies that recognition must be institutionally enforceable, not merely symbolic.

> "recognition... is reciprocal" (p. 1206)  
**Function**: Shows why claimants also validate the recognising institution.

#### Concept: Property-Citizenship Nexus

> "Struggles over property and citizenship" (p. 1199)  
**Function**: Introduces the joint analytical object.

> "Property and citizenship are thus intimately related" (p. 1205)  
**Function**: Directly states the nexus.

> "local citizenship and status are often just as important" (p. 1205)  
**Function**: Extends citizenship beyond formal nationality into local land access.

#### Concept: Territorialization

> "legal control over spatial property" (p. 1205)  
**Function**: Connects land as property to land as territory.

> "political control over territory" (p. 1205)  
**Function**: Shows why cadastral and territorial questions cannot be separated.

> "classification, registration and mapping" (p. 1205)  
**Function**: Names the operational tools of territorialization.

#### Concept: Jurisdictional Competition

> "no single institution defines and enforces rights" (p. 1201)  
**Function**: Establishes institutional plurality.

> "Governance is not reserved for statutory institutions alone" (p. 1201)  
**Function**: Opens analysis to customary, informal, and practical authorities.

> "institutions compete to establish functional jurisdiction" (p. 1213)  
**Function**: Defines competition over which body can settle land claims.

#### Concept: Rupture Analysis

> "Ruptures are open moments" (p. 1202)  
**Function**: Defines rupture as an analytical window.

> "the choice of rupture is epistemological" (p. 1203)  
**Function**: Clarifies that rupture is used to see authority formation, not simply to privilege dramatic events.

> "new structural scaffolding is erected" (p. 1202)  
**Function**: Useful for thinking about digital land infrastructure as a softer administrative rupture.

#### Concept: Recognition Gap

> "not being seen" (p. 1205)  
**Function**: Captures why administrative invisibility matters.

> "not to be seen as a rights-bearing subject" (p. 1205)  
**Function**: Directly names the exclusion mechanism.

> "Claims are recognized to a certain degree" (p. 1208)  
**Function**: Supports a graded model of recognition rather than binary ownership/no ownership.
