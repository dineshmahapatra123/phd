## Core Argument

James C. Scott’s *Seeing Like a State* is one of the most useful books for understanding why technically sophisticated state reforms often produce worse outcomes than the systems they seek to replace. Scott’s central claim is that modern states, especially high-modernist states, depend on **legibility**: they simplify complex social and ecological worlds into formats that can be measured, counted, taxed, regulated, and controlled from above. That simplification makes rule possible, but it also erases the practical knowledge and local variation on which real social life depends.

Scott’s argument has four linked parts. First, state power grows by imposing simplified grids on land, people, language, and production. Second, high-modernist ideology turns this simplification into a moral and technical ambition: order should be visible, geometric, and planned. Third, when such visions are backed by authoritarian power, they can become destructive. Fourth, the formal scheme always depends on informal practices, metis, and local improvisation that the scheme cannot itself generate.

For this thesis, Scott is load-bearing because he explains why records, maps, and administrative databases do not merely describe reality. They reshape it. A cadastral map, a standardised land register, or a digital parcel identifier is never neutral. It is a simplification that privileges what the state can see over what land users actually need. That is why DILRMP and related land-record reforms can improve administrative reach while worsening tenure insecurity, exclusion, and conflict.

## Legibility as State Power

Scott begins with a simple but powerful idea: states need to make society legible. That means converting messy, local, overlapping, and often unwritten arrangements into standard categories that can be governed from the centre. Permanent surnames, cadastral surveys, population registers, standard weights and measures, and land titles are all part of this same project.

His forestry example shows the logic clearly. Scientific forestry did not just manage existing forests better. It changed the forest into something the forester could read: same-age trees, same-species stands, straight rows, uniform outputs, calculable yields. In the short run this was efficient. Over time it destroyed biodiversity, soil health, and resilience. The apparent administrative gain concealed ecological loss.

That is exactly the pattern Scott wants the reader to notice elsewhere. The state does not simply observe reality. It creates a simplified version of reality that fits its own administrative needs, and then acts as if that version were the whole truth.

For land governance, this matters directly. A parcel map, a registration system, or a digital record set does not merely record land relations. It imposes a new order on them. It privileges clarity, singular ownership, fixed boundaries, and state-recognised claims, even where land is actually governed through layered, seasonal, collective, or customary rights.

## High Modernism and Its Risks

Scott’s second major contribution is his concept of high modernism. High modernism is not science itself; it is a faith in the power of science, planning, and technical expertise to redesign society from above. It is animated by the conviction that visible order is inherently good and that disorder is a sign of backwardness.

This matters because the reformer’s desire for a clean, rational system is not enough. It has to be joined to authoritarian capacity and a weakened civil society. Only then does simplification become dangerous. Scott’s model explains why some of the worst modern disasters occurred where the state was strong enough to impose schemes and where citizens lacked the power to resist.

That diagnosis is useful for DILRMP because digitalisation is often framed in precisely high-modernist language: transparent, efficient, interoperable, tamper-proof, scalable. Those are not false claims in themselves, but Scott helps show what they hide. They assume that social complexity is a problem to be flattened, that local knowledge can be subordinated to the database, and that visible order is equivalent to justice.

Scott’s warning is not simply that planning fails. It is that planning that refuses to recognise local practice and practical knowledge will systematically produce blind spots, and those blind spots will fall hardest on the vulnerable.

## Metis and the Missing Link

The deepest part of Scott’s book is his defence of metis, or practical knowledge. Metis is the kind of know-how that comes from experience, adaptation, and close engagement with a specific setting. It is not easily codified. It is exactly the kind of knowledge that makes real systems work: farmers knowing soils, tenants knowing kinship obligations, village actors knowing boundaries, and local officials knowing how disputes actually move.

Scott’s point is not that metis is pure or benign. It can coexist with domination, exclusion, and hierarchy. His point is narrower and more important: formal schemes fail when they try to work without it. A registry that ignores informal practice, an agrarian reform that ignores local bargaining, or a map that erases multiple use-rights will not function as intended.

This is one of the clearest conceptual bridges to the land-record literature. DILRMP assumes that if the state improves formal records, rights become secure. Scott shows why that is too simple. The database may become more accurate as a state tool while becoming less useful as a social tool. The formal system can become cleaner at the same time as lived rights become more fragile.

## What Scott Reveals About Land Records

Scott’s analysis gives a direct vocabulary for the dangers of land-record modernisation.

### 1. Maps create the territory they claim to record
A cadastral map is not a neutral mirror. It creates a bounded territory, fixes property, and makes claims administratively visible. Once the map acquires legal force, the mapped version of land begins to override the ground version.

### 2. Simplification is selective
The state does not see all land relations. It sees taxable property, saleable units, standard parcels, and measurable boundaries. It does not easily see commons, seasonal access, grazing rights, burial spaces, or informal use.

### 3. Standardisation can destroy resilience
Uniformity improves administration, but it also makes the system less adaptable. A single property format can erase plural rights and social safety nets that matter in times of stress.

### 4. Formal schemes depend on informal supports
Even a digital land system still depends on surveyors, local intermediaries, dispute resolution, and on-the-ground knowledge. If those are weak or captured, the scheme performs badly.

### 5. High-modernist language masks power
Words like efficiency, transparency, and modernisation can hide the political fact that the system is being redesigned to serve the state’s own legibility, not necessarily the land user’s security.

## Critical Tensions

Scott is compelling, but his framework has limits.

First, he can sometimes make the state appear more singular than it is. In practice, states contain conflict, negotiation, and partial failure. Not every simplification is equally destructive.

Second, his defence of local knowledge is useful but incomplete. Local systems can also exclude women, tenants, and outsiders. So the choice is not between state rationality and local virtue.

Third, Scott is strongest when explaining failure, but less precise about the conditions under which simplification can be made accountable and useful.

Those limits do not weaken the book’s value for this thesis. They just remind us that land-record reform has to be evaluated institution by institution, not by slogan.

---

## Ideas Sparked

Scott helps sharpen three key thesis moves.

First, DILRMP should be treated as a legibility project, not merely a digitisation project. The question is not whether records are digital, but what kinds of land relations become visible and which disappear.

Second, the real issue is not only accuracy but social adequacy. A record can be technically correct and socially misleading if it erases commons, customary rights, or multiple claims.

Third, the reform problem is not solved by better software alone. It is solved, if at all, by the difficult task of fitting formal systems to local practice without destroying the latter.
