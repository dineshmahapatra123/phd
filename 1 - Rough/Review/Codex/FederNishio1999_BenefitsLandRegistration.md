## Core Argument

Feder & Nishio (1999) synthesise conceptual and empirical evidence that land registration and titling—when implemented with supporting institutions—raise tenure security, expand access to formal credit, increase land values, and incentivise investment, thereby boosting productivity and income. However, benefits depend on legal enforcement, administrative capacity, and protection of customary rights.

## Key Concepts

- Tenure security → investment → higher productivity
- Titles as collateral → formal credit expansion
- Preconditions: functioning courts, registries, surveys, and dispute resolution
- Risks: elite capture, dispossession of commons, privacy concerns

## Evidence Summary

- Cross-country and within-country micro-studies (Costa Rica, Brazil, Jamaica, Thailand) show positive correlations between titles and investment/output.
- Historical and theoretical models (Feder et al. 1988) link tenure security to expected returns on long-lived investments; empirical studies often report sizable effects but are sensitive to context and selection.

## Policy Implications

- Prioritise data-cleaning, dispute-resolution mechanisms, and institutional complements before irreversible digitisation or immutability steps.
- Design titling programs that recognise overlapping and customary claims; include grievance windows and transitional protections.

## Critique & Limitations

- Observational studies risk selection bias: titled plots or households may differ systematically.
- Magnitude of effects varies; context (credit markets, enforcement) conditions outcomes.

## Thesis Relevance

- Use as authoritative synthesis on why titling can increase investment and credit; cite for empirical ranges and policy caveats.

## Keywords

land titling, tenure security, collateral, investment, credit, selection bias
