# Chapter 5 Fieldwork and Writing Guide

**Status:** Comprehensive architecture, refreshed and cross-checked June 2026  
**Purpose:** Fieldwork design, data-analysis plan, writing guide and prioritised reading
architecture for Chapter 5  
**Provisional chapter title:** *Village Studies: Ground-Level Realities of Land Governance*

## Why Chapter 5 Requires a Different Strategy

Chapters 2-4 are principally literature-, law- and policy-led. Chapter 5 must be
evidence-led. The literature should provide concepts, comparisons and interpretive leverage,
but it should not overwhelm the voices, histories, records and institutional encounters
observed in the two villages.

The chapter should be designed as a **comparative, qualitatively led mixed-methods village
study**. Its evidence will come from:

- household-level land and livelihood data;
- semi-structured interviews and household land histories;
- village social and settlement histories;
- Bhulekh RoRs and Bhunaksha maps;
- mutation, partition and correction experiences;
- CM-KISAN/PM-KISAN application and payment histories;
- records of commons, encroachment and land-use conversion;
- interviews with Revenue Inspectors, Amins, Tahasil staff and intermediaries;
- observations of village space, caste sahis, boundaries and land use; and
- selected parcel evidence, with detailed parcel measurement reserved for Chapter 6.

The chapter is not intended to estimate causal effects or produce statistically representative
findings for Odisha. Its purpose is to identify and explain mechanisms, patterned
relationships and contrasting institutional configurations.

## The Argument Chapter 5 Should Build

The final argument must emerge from the field evidence. It should not be predetermined.
Nevertheless, the chapter can investigate the following provisional argument:

1. Land records in the two villages are not external descriptions of agrarian society. They
   are historically accumulated institutional artefacts that shape who can sell, inherit,
   partition, mortgage, receive compensation and establish welfare eligibility.
2. Ownership, possession, cultivation and the recorded right frequently overlap, but they are not
   identical. Their divergence is generated through identifiable social processes: death,
   inheritance, oral partition, concealed tenancy, informal sale, encroachment, land-use
   change, boundary adjustment and delayed administrative updating.
3. Record discrepancies are socially differentiated. Households do not have equal capacity
   to detect, tolerate, exploit or correct them. Caste location, gender, wealth, education,
   migration, political connections and familiarity with officials affect the cost and outcome
   of formalisation.
4. Multi-owner RoRs can provide evidence of inherited rights while simultaneously obscuring
   actual household shares and possession. They transfer the cost of clarification from the
   state to families and become increasingly difficult to correct over generations.
5. Village knowledge often supplies a workable account of possession and boundaries, but
   it does not automatically protect weaker claimants. Local recognition is structured by
   power, kinship, caste and gender.
6. Bhulekh and Bhunaksha increase visibility and reduce some search costs. Digital access
   does not remove dependence on RIs, Amins, kiosks, deed writers, brokers and Tahasil
   officials when a record is incomplete, contested or spatially inaccurate.
7. Welfare integration increases the material value of an updated RoR. It can motivate
   mutation and correction, but it also converts historical record omissions into present
   exclusions from CM-KISAN and PM-KISAN.
8. Differences between the villages should be explained through settlement history,
   landholding structure, irrigation and markets, development interventions, social
   composition, administrative proximity and political mediation rather than being presented
   as two isolated descriptions.

## What Chapter 5 Must Contribute

Chapter 5 should answer four kinds of questions.

### Descriptive

- Who owns, possesses and cultivates land?
- How unequal and fragmented are holdings?
- What economic activities depend on land and commons?
- How widespread are joint records, oral partitions, tenancy and unmutated inheritance?
- Who accesses Bhulekh, Bhunaksha and farmer transfers?

### Explanatory

- Through which processes did current landholding patterns emerge?
- Why do records and possession diverge?
- Why do some households correct records while others do not?
- Why are some record discrepancies tolerated for long periods?
- Why do similar record problems produce different outcomes across households?

### Institutional

- How do villagers interact with the RI, Amin, Tahasil, Sub-Registrar, kiosks and brokers?
- Which problems enter formal mutation or correction proceedings?
- Which remain outside the state system?
- What documents, costs, delays and discretionary decisions shape outcomes?

### Political-economic

- Who benefits from record clarity, ambiguity and delay?
- How do caste, gender, class, kinship and political connections structure access?
- Does digitisation reduce transaction costs or reorganise intermediation?
- How does welfare eligibility change the value and meaning of the RoR?

This distinction is essential.

- perceptions and uses of RoRs;
- administrative encounters;
- welfare inclusion and exclusion;

## 5.1 Introduction and Methodology

### Function of the introduction

The introduction should do six things in a disciplined sequence:

1. Restate the problem carried forward from Chapter 4.
2. Explain why aggregate DILRMP indicators require village-level investigation.
3. Introduce the two villages and the logic of their selection.
4. State the fieldwork period, sources and methods.
5. Clarify the chapter's analytical limits.
6. Preview the comparative argument and section structure.

### Recommended opening movement

Begin with a concrete institutional problem, not a general statement about Indian villages.
For example:

- a family cultivates separately while its RoR remains in the names of deceased ancestors;
- a sharecropper cultivates but the owner is legible to PM-KISAN;
- a digitally available map reproduces a boundary no longer recognised on the ground; or
- a welfare rejection prompts a household's first attempt to mutate inherited land.

Then widen the lens: the episode is not an isolated error but a point where family, caste,
property, bureaucracy and digital welfare infrastructure meet.

### Research design

Describe the study as:

> A comparative, qualitatively led mixed-methods study of two purposively selected villages
> in Nayagarh district, combining household-level descriptive data, semi-structured
> interviews, archival and administrative records, digital RoRs and maps, observation and
> parcel-level verification.

Do not claim statistical representativeness unless a probability sample was actually used.
Do not call the household component a census unless every resident household under a
clearly defined village boundary was enumerated.

### Village selection

Explain selection using explicit dimensions:

- settlement and tenure history;
- caste and tribal composition;
- agrarian and non-agrarian livelihood structure;
- irrigation and land productivity;
- proximity to road, town, Tahasil and market;
- exposure to development projects or acquisition;
- welfare-policy relevance;
- land-record modernisation or survey experience;
- known presence of commons, encroachment or conversion;
- feasibility and access.

Distinguish:

- **selection criterion:** why the village was chosen;
- **background characteristic:** what was learned after selection; and
- **analytical contrast:** why a difference matters for comparison.

### Unit of analysis

The study contains several units. Name them separately:

| Unit | What is analysed |
|---|---|
| Village | Agrarian structure, settlement history, institutions and social geography |
| Household | Ownership, possession, cultivation, livelihood and welfare |
| Individual | Gendered rights, knowledge, experience and authority |
| RoR/khata | Recorded tenants, shares, plots and status |
| Parcel | Boundary, area, use, possession and map correspondence |
| Administrative case | Mutation, partition, correction, demarcation or appeal |
| Scheme episode | Application, verification, rejection, grievance and payment |

Avoid shifting silently between household, family, holding, khata and parcel.

### Definitions to fix before analysis

Define and use consistently:

- resident household;
- family;
- ownership holding;
- operational holding;
- land possessed;
- land cultivated;
- land leased in and leased out;
- sharecropping;
- agricultural labour household;
- landless household;
- homestead-only household;
- marginal and small holder;
- parcel and plot;
- recorded owner/tenant;
- multi-owner RoR;
- oral partition;
- encroachment;
- common property resource;
- record discrepancy;
- welfare inclusion and exclusion.

Use MoSPI/NSS definitions as a benchmark, but modify them transparently where local
institutions require it.

### Data sources

The methodology should list:

1. Village-level administrative and Census data.
2. Household schedule or listing.
3. Semi-structured household interviews.
4. Key-informant interviews.
5. Women's individual interviews or group discussions.
6. Caste-sahi or neighbourhood walks.
7. Genealogies and intergenerational land histories.
8. Bhulekh RoRs and Bhunaksha maps.
9. Mutation, partition and correction documents.
10. CM-KISAN/PM-KISAN documents or portal status.
11. Observation in the RI office, Tahasil or service kiosk, where permitted.
12. Parcel survey evidence developed in Chapter 6.

### Sampling and coverage statement

Report:

- total households listed in each village;
- total households interviewed;
- selection method;
- refusal and non-contact count;
- interviews by gender, caste and tenure status;
- number of key informants and officials;
- number of administrative cases traced;
- number of scheme episodes traced;
- number of parcels selected and the selection logic;
- fieldwork dates and repeat visits.

If the household sample was purposive or convenience-based, percentages must be labelled
as percentages **among interviewed households**, not village prevalence.

### Stronger sampling strategy

For interviews, use purposive maximum-variation sampling across:

- caste and tribe;
- women-headed households and women claimants;
- ownership class;
- landless and homestead-only households;
- owner-cultivators;
- tenants/sharecroppers;
- lessors;
- households with recent inheritance or partition;
- multi-owner records;
- encroachers or government-land occupants;
- households with clean records;
- households with unsuccessful and successful mutation;
- CM-KISAN/PM-KISAN recipients, rejected applicants and non-applicants;
- migrants and non-resident co-sharers.

Include successful cases. A sample composed only of disputes will exaggerate dysfunction and
cannot explain when the system works.

### Comparative logic

Use **structured, focused comparison**. Ask the same core questions in each village:

1. What is the inherited landholding structure?
2. How are ownership, possession and cultivation distributed?
3. Which processes generate record discrepancies?
4. How are disputes and ambiguities managed?
5. How do villagers access record services?
6. How do records affect welfare?
7. Which features of the village alter these processes?

### Mixed-method integration

The chapter should not place a table in one section and interviews in another without
connection. Integrate evidence through joint displays:

| Quantitative pattern | Qualitative explanation | Record evidence | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| High share of joint RoRs | Families describe costly partition and absent co-sharers | Old khata and mutation status | Administrative updating lags social partition |
| Low female recorded ownership | Women describe relinquishment and non-mutation | RoR names and genealogy | Legal entitlement does not become recorded control |
| Tenant exclusion from PM-KISAN | Oral tenancy concealed from owner and state | Owner RoR and scheme status | Ownership-based welfare misrecognises cultivation |

### Analytical status of the evidence

State clearly:

- descriptive tables identify patterns in the studied households;
- interviews identify meanings, processes and mechanisms;
- records establish what the state has inscribed;
- observation establishes current possession or use only within practical limits;
- village testimony may corroborate possession but is not equivalent to legal title;
- the design does not establish causal effects or statewide prevalence.

### Positionality and reflexivity

Briefly discuss:

- how entry to the villages was negotiated;
- the role of gatekeepers;
- whether officials introduced respondents;
- caste, class, gender, linguistic and insider/outsider relations;
- whether interviews were conducted privately;
- how respondents may have understood the research;
- whether discussion of land created expectations of correction or benefits;
- how contradictory accounts were handled.

### Ethics and data protection

Land data can expose wealth, family conflict, informal tenancy and allegedly unlawful
possession. The methodology should specify:

- informed consent for interviews and parcel visits;
- separate consent for photographs, GPS points and document copies;
- pseudonymisation of households and disputed parcels;
- removal of khata, plot, Aadhaar, bank and mobile identifiers from published material;
- secure separation of identity keys from analytical data;
- no public map that exposes a vulnerable claimant without consent;
- no promise that research will secure mutation, title or welfare;
- a protocol for allegations of corruption or criminal conduct;
- treatment of intra-family disagreement over participation;
- whether ethics approval was obtained and under what reference.

### Methodological limitations

Include real limitations:

- two villages cannot represent Odisha;
- purposive samples do not produce population estimates;
- concealed tenancy is likely underreported;
- land area reported by households may use local measures and approximations;
- women's claims may be obscured in household interviews led by men;
- portal status can change after fieldwork;
- record and map copies may represent different update dates;
- disputed possession cannot be legally adjudicated by the researcher;
- parcel measurement has technical error and access constraints;
- welfare rejection reasons may be incomplete or misunderstood;
- respondent recall of inheritance and transfer dates may be uncertain.

## 5.4 Historical Comparison of Land Ownership and Access

### Central task

This section should reconstruct change, not merely compare current land-size classes.

Build household and village land histories around:

- original settlement or estate structure;
- abolition and post-independence land reform;
- government-land and ceiling allotment;
- inheritance;
- dowry, gift and women's inheritance;
- purchase and distress sale;
- partition and fragmentation;
- tenancy and sharecropping;
- migration;
- acquisition and compensation;
- conversion and rising land values;
- encroachment and legalisation;
- decline or reclassification of commons.

### Sources and triangulation

Use:

- earlier and current RoRs;
- Sabik and Hal records where applicable;
- genealogies;
- registered deeds and pattas;
- mutation histories;
- tax/rent receipts;
- oral histories from more than one lineage and caste group;
- settlement reports and village maps;
- field observation.

Oral history should not be treated as inferior to the record, or the record as automatically
true. Analyse disagreements as evidence about authority, memory and power.

### Household land-history timeline

| Period/event | Recorded holder | Socially recognised holder | Possessor/cultivator | Mode of change | Record updated? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### Cohort strategy

Where dates permit, group transformations into:

1. land reform and early post-independence period;
2. settlement/consolidation period;
3. post-1991 market and infrastructure period;
4. digital record and welfare-integration period.

Do not force a periodisation if field evidence does not support it.

### Measures of distribution

Report:

- households by ownership class;
- households by operational class;
- total area owned by each class;
- mean and median holding;
- landless and homestead-only households;
- number of parcels per household;
- women recorded owners;
- land leased in/out.

If coverage is sufficiently complete, compute:

- Lorenz curve;
- Gini coefficient for land owned;
- concentration share of the top 10 or 20 percent;
- caste-group distribution of land.

Always report the underlying coverage and treatment of joint holdings. A precise Gini based
on uncertain household shares is misleading.

## 5.4.1 Gendered and Caste-Based Landholding Patterns

### Required disaggregation

Analyse:

- recorded ownership by sex;
- effective control by sex;
- inheritance claims and relinquishment;
- joint and individual titles;
- ownership area by caste/social category;
- operational land by caste;
- tenancy by caste and gender;
- landlessness;
- homestead ownership;
- location and quality of land;
- access to irrigation and roads;
- welfare recognition.

Do not equate a woman's name in an RoR with effective control. Do not infer that absence
from the RoR means absence of every claim.

### Gendered claim categories

Classify women as:

- sole recorded owner;
- joint recorded owner with spouse;
- co-sharer in natal property;
- widow awaiting mutation;
- legally entitled but not recorded;
- cultivating family land without recognised control;
- tenant/sharecropper;
- landless agricultural worker;
- recipient of government homestead or FRA title, where applicable.

### Caste-spatial analysis

Investigate:

- whether caste sahis correspond to homestead ownership patterns;
- proximity to cultivable land, roads, water and commons;
- whether dominant groups hold larger, better irrigated or more accessible parcels;
- whether SC/ST households depend disproportionately on government land or commons;
- whether administrative correction outcomes vary by caste and political connection.

Avoid publishing a caste map that could expose sensitive household-level information.

## 5.7 Governance, Discrepancies and Gaps

### Synthesis function

This section should draw the evidence together. It should not repeat every previous finding.

Organise discrepancies by source:

1. **historical:** old settlement, abolition, consolidation or map quality;
2. **demographic:** death, marriage, migration and succession;
3. **familial:** oral partition and unasserted shares;
4. **market:** informal sale, mortgage, conversion and speculation;
5. **tenurial:** concealed lease and sharecropping;
6. **spatial:** boundary, area and parcel subdivision;
7. **administrative:** delay, missing file, jurisdiction and non-propagation;
8. **technological:** data entry, identity mismatch, interface and database integration;
9. **political:** influence, brokerage, selective enforcement and strategic ambiguity.

### Explanatory matrix

| Discrepancy | Immediate event | Why not corrected | Who benefits | Who bears cost | Trigger for correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### Key analytical insight

An outdated record may persist because:

- correction is costly;
- the ambiguity is mutually convenient;
- the family fears conflict;
- tenancy must remain concealed;
- the land has low present value;
- an official route is unclear;
- a powerful actor benefits;
- the record becomes consequential only upon sale, acquisition or welfare verification.

This moves the analysis beyond the language of clerical "errors."

## 5.8.2 Exclusion and Contestation in CM-KISAN and PM-KISAN Cash Transfers: A People's Perspective

### Required comparison

Separate:

- eligible and receiving;
- eligible but not receiving;
- rejected with known reason;
- rejected with unclear reason;
- not applied due to expected ineligibility or lack of knowledge;
- owner receiving while tenant cultivates;
- landless household using CM-KISAN livelihood route;
- deceased or duplicate beneficiary;
- payment stopped after verification;
- grievance resolved or unresolved.

### Exclusion typology

1. **Design exclusion:** tenant/sharecropper outside ownership-based rule.
2. **Record exclusion:** claimant absent from or mismatched in Bhulekh.
3. **Identity exclusion:** Aadhaar, e-KYC, name, age or family mismatch.
4. **Bank exclusion:** NPCI, account or payment failure.
5. **Classification exclusion:** medium/large, urban, tax or employment category.
6. **Administrative exclusion:** application not processed, rejected or not corrected.
7. **Informational exclusion:** claimant unaware or unable to navigate application.
8. **Household exclusion:** one-beneficiary/family rule conflicts with actual household split.

### People's perspective

Analyse:

- what respondents believe the scheme recognises;
- whether they see themselves as farmers, landowners or labourers;
- whether rejection is attributed to record, official, technology or politics;
- whether the benefit justifies mutation costs;
- whether tenants seek recognition or avoid disclosure;
- whether welfare changes family disputes over ownership.

## Writing Method

### Write claims, not data dumps

Each substantive subsection should follow:

1. **Claim**
2. **Pattern in household/record data**
3. **Case or interview evidence explaining the mechanism**
4. **Contrary or deviant case**
5. **Comparison between villages**
6. **Interpretation through the literature**
7. **Implication for the thesis question**

### Case vignette structure

- anonymised actor and setting;
- land/record configuration;
- precipitating event;
- administrative or welfare journey;
- outcome;
- why the case reveals a wider mechanism.

Vignettes are evidence, not decoration. Follow them with analysis.

### Within-case before cross-case

For each theme:

1. establish the Village 1 pattern;
2. establish the Village 2 pattern;
3. compare;
4. explain the contrast;
5. identify limits and exceptions.

### Negative and deviant cases

Actively analyse:

- poor household with successful correction;
- wealthy household with unresolved joint record;
- woman with effective control despite absent name;
- recorded woman without effective control;
- tenant receiving support through a non-RoR route;
- accurate old map despite wider claims of obsolescence.

These cases refine rather than weaken the argument.

### Language discipline

Prefer:

- "among interviewed households";
- "respondents reported";
- "the RoR records";
- "field observation indicated";
- "the accounts converge/diverge";
- "this case suggests a mechanism";
- "the two villages differ in..."

Avoid:

- "the villagers believe" when views differ;
- "ground truth" for contested ownership;
- "clear title" without legal qualification;
- "illegal encroacher" unless legally determined;
- "beneficiary" for an applicant who never received payment;
- "impact" where the design shows association or process.

## Recommended Work Sequence

### Stage 1: Freeze definitions and IDs

1. Define household, ownership, possession, operation and discrepancy.
2. Assign anonymous household, interview, khata and parcel IDs.
3. Create a data dictionary and source register.

### Stage 2: Build village chronologies

4. Establish settlement, consolidation and major development dates.
5. Record major land-reform, commons and conversion events.
6. Identify administrative boundary changes.

### Stage 3: Clean household land data

7. Standardise acres/hectares and local units.
8. Separate owned, possessed, operated, leased and homestead land.
9. Resolve duplicate household and khata links.
10. Flag uncertain values rather than imputing silently.

### Stage 4: Code interviews and episodes

11. Code interviews.
12. Create land-history, administrative-case and scheme-episode tables.
13. Write analytic memos for each main mechanism.

### Stage 5: Conduct within-village analysis

14. Produce Village 1 profile and theme matrices.
15. Produce Village 2 profile using identical definitions.
16. Identify deviant and successful cases.

### Stage 6: Compare villages

17. Create the comparative profile and mechanism matrix.
18. Explain differences through history, social structure and institutions.
19. Separate observed contrast from speculation.

### Stage 7: Write

20. Draft 5.4-5.8 first, where the analysis lies.
21. Draft village profiles only after knowing which context matters.
22. Draft methodology and introduction after the evidence architecture is stable.
23. Write section conclusions that answer thesis questions.
24. Move technical parcel detail into Chapter 6.

## Claims That Must Wait for Field Evidence

Do not write as established findings, before analysis, that:

- one village has more accurate records than the other;
- caste or gender independently causes an administrative outcome;
- most tenants or women are excluded from a scheme;
- digitisation increased or reduced corruption;
- a record discrepancy caused a dispute;
- welfare integration increased mutation;
- commons declined because of modernisation;
- an official or intermediary took a bribe;
- high-technology survey improved parcel accuracy.

These are hypotheses or questions until supported by the field evidence. Where fieldwork
cannot establish them, retain them as bounded interpretations or future research questions.

## Quality-Control Checklist

### Evidence

- Every numerical table has a denominator and source.
- Counts and percentages match.
- Ownership, possession and operation are not conflated.
- Joint land is not double counted.
- Women's claims are not inferred from household-head data.
- Every major mechanism has more than one source or an explicit evidence limitation.
- Contrary cases are included.

### Comparison

- Both villages use the same core definitions.
- Differences in sample coverage are disclosed.
- Historical boundary changes are considered.
- Comparisons are not driven by one exceptional household.
- Context is used to explain, not excuse, differences.

### Legal and institutional accuracy

- RoR is not described as conclusive title.
- Official procedure is distinguished from experienced procedure.
- Alleged encroachment and corruption are attributed and anonymised.
- Scheme eligibility is checked against the rule operative during fieldwork.
- Portal screenshots and values have dates.

### Ethics

- Personal identifiers are removed.
- Disputed maps and genealogies cannot identify vulnerable parties.
- Consent covers document and spatial-data use.
- Sensitive caste and welfare cells are suppressed where necessary.
- No respondent is presented as legally culpable without adjudicated evidence.

### Writing

- Profiles are concise.
- Themes drive the chapter.
- Tables are interpreted.
- Vignettes demonstrate mechanisms.
- Literature follows evidence rather than replacing it.
- Chapter 6 material is not duplicated.
- Conclusions remain within the design's inferential limits.

## Suggested Chapter Conclusion

The conclusion should not merely summarise. It should state:

1. which discrepancies are most important in each village;
2. which historical and social processes generated them;
3. who can correct records and who cannot;
4. whether digitisation changed access, intermediation or outcomes;
5. how RoRs shape welfare inclusion and exclusion;
6. what the comparison reveals that a one-village study would miss;
7. which claims require parcel-level testing in Chapter 6.

The final transition can be:

> Chapter 5 establishes that discrepancies among record, possession and cultivation are
> socially patterned and institutionally produced. Chapter 6 turns from household histories
> and administrative encounters to the parcel itself, testing how these discrepancies appear
> in the relationship among the textual RoR, cadastral map and measured field situation.

## Final Standard

A strong Chapter 5 will not claim that the two villages represent rural Odisha. It will show,
with a transparent chain of evidence, how statewide laws and digital systems are translated
through village history, caste, gender, kinship, agrarian class and bureaucratic practice. Its
contribution lies in explaining the mechanisms through which a formally modernised land
record becomes accurate, obsolete, useful, contested or exclusionary in everyday life.
