## Core Argument

Cohn's central claim is that British colonial rule in India was not only a military, fiscal, or legal project. It was also a knowledge project. The British made India governable by converting complex social, legal, linguistic, spatial, and material worlds into state-readable forms: grammars, surveys, settlement reports, legal digests, maps, censuses, museum catalogues, ethnographies, and official proceedings.

For this PhD, the most important version of the argument is narrower: colonial land administration did not merely record land relations. It reorganised them. Through survey, settlement, revenue assessment, and legal codification, colonial rule translated layered agrarian relations into administrative facts. This helps explain why postcolonial land records are simultaneously powerful and unreliable: they inherit a state form of knowledge that was designed around revenue, classification, and control rather than around perfect correspondence with lived possession.

## Conceptual Architecture

### Colonial Knowledge-Power

Cohn treats colonial knowledge as constitutive of rule. The state did not simply collect neutral descriptions of India; it created usable categories through which India could be administered. This is most visible in the relationship between reports, statistical returns, legal codes, histories, surveys, and institutions of rule.

PhD use: this gives Chapter 1 a way to frame land records as state knowledge rather than mere administrative paperwork.

### Investigative Modalities

This is the book's master concept. An investigative modality is a state-oriented method for collecting, ordering, classifying, and converting information into usable administrative knowledge. Cohn lists historiographic, observational/travel, survey, enumerative, museological, and surveillance modalities.

PhD use: the survey and enumerative modalities are the only ones that should be heavily used. The others are useful as background but not central.

### Survey Modality

The survey modality is the strongest concept for this PhD. Cohn shows that survey in colonial India meant more than measuring land. It included inspecting, supervising, valuing, mapping, bounding, classifying, and documenting both natural and social features of territory. The survey produced a government-readable territory.

PhD use: direct relevance to cadastral maps, Bhunaksha, Survey and Settlement, ULPIN, parcel-level verification, and the spatial-textual mismatch.

### Enumerative Modality

Enumeration converts people and society into countable categories. Cohn's main example is the census, especially caste, religion, occupation, age, literacy, residence, and domestic organisation. The key point is that enumeration does not merely reflect categories; it hardens them.

PhD use: useful for caste and class patterns in land ownership, welfare eligibility, and RoR as an entitlement gate.

### Documentation Project

Cohn's colonial state works through documentation: reports, enquiries, commissions, records, maps, histories, and statistical returns. This produces a totalising and individualising form of rule: the state classifies the whole population while also locating individual subjects within administrative categories.

PhD use: DILRMP can be framed as a digital continuation of this documentation logic, but the analogy must remain precise.

### Colonial Legal Reconstitution of Property

Chapter 3 shows how the colonial state converted plural, locally mediated, and textually complex legal traditions into court-administered legal forms. Hastings, Jones, Halhed, Colebrooke, pandits, maulavis, and British judges all appear in a process that claims to preserve Indian law while converting it into something usable by British courts.

PhD use: this is central for inheritance, succession, joint khata, partition, presumptive title, and the legal basis of record-ground mismatch.

### Intermediaries and Translation

Cohn repeatedly shows that British official knowledge depended on Indian intermediaries: munshis, pandits, maulavis, scribes, translators, vakils, and local informants. The British relied on these intermediaries while also trying to subordinate or bypass their interpretive authority.

PhD use: this is useful for analysing RIs, Amins, data-entry operators, document writers, local brokers, and the mutation/correction economy around land records.

## Empirical Terrain or Evidence and Examples Used

### Foreword and Introduction

The Foreword by Nicholas Dirks situates Cohn as a major figure in historical anthropology and colonial knowledge studies. It frames colonialism as a cultural and documentary project, not just a political economy of conquest.

Chapter 1 introduces the conceptual frame. Cohn contrasts older forms of visible power with modern state procedures: records, registration, classification, standardisation, histories, censuses, and surveys. He then identifies the major investigative modalities.

PhD relevance: very strong for the conceptual introduction and Chapter 2 historical framing.

### Chapter 2 - Language

Cohn reconstructs British dependence on Persian, Sanskrit, Bengali, Hindustani, and other languages. He shows how grammars, dictionaries, translations, training colleges, and language manuals converted Indian knowledge into administrative tools. Fort William College is the major institutional example.

PhD relevance: conditional. Use only for the general idea that rule depends on translation and intermediaries. Do not make language policy central to the thesis.

### Chapter 3 - Law

This is the most directly relevant chapter. Cohn explains how the East India Company became a territorial state after Plassey and the Dewani grant; how revenue assessment demanded knowledge of property, possession, and obligation; how Hastings created the Collector and district machinery; how British legal models divided India into despotic and classical/theocratic interpretations; and how Hindu/Muslim law was codified and transformed through British courts.

PhD relevance: very strong. This chapter supports the genealogy of revenue records, legal codification, presumptive property claims, inheritance, and the institutional architecture behind RoR disputes.

### Chapter 4 - Objects

Cohn discusses the transformation of Indian objects into artifacts, antiquities, and art. Most of the chapter is museological and archaeological. The PhD-relevant portion is the section on Wellesley's surveys, Colin Mackenzie, Mysore, local tracts, inscriptions, oral histories, and the collection of information about tenures.

PhD relevance: narrow. Use pp. 80-85 only, mainly as evidence that colonial survey was a broad archival and documentary practice.

### Chapter 5 - Cloth

This chapter analyses cloth, dress, authority, military uniforms, khilat, Gandhi, caste/gender dress disputes, and British efforts to define custom. It is strong cultural history, but not central to land records.

PhD relevance: weak. Use only the final idea that once the British defined a practice as custom, deviation could be treated as disorder. Otherwise skip.

## Theoretical Lineage

### Foucault

Cohn's work fits the knowledge-power frame often associated with Foucault, but Dirks stresses that Cohn was developing these insights in South Asian historical anthropology before many later theoretical formulations became standard.

Use: Ch. 1 theoretical framework, but keep the argument grounded in land administration rather than abstract discourse theory.

### Said and Orientalism

Cohn overlaps with Said's concern that Orientalist knowledge produced its objects. But Cohn's strength for this PhD is institutional specificity: surveys, revenue, legal digests, language colleges, census, and state archives.

Use: Mention only if positioning Cohn in literature review. Do not turn the land-records thesis into a general Orientalism thesis.

### Ranajit Guha

The land revenue discussion is adjacent to Guha's work on property in Bengal. Cohn's chapter on law and revenue can be paired with Guha for the Permanent Settlement and the construction of property categories.

Use: Ch. 2.3.2 and Ch. 4.1.

### Historical Institutionalism

Cohn is not writing NIE or path dependence in a formal way, but his analysis is highly compatible with historical institutionalism: colonial knowledge practices create institutional categories that persist and structure later governance.

Use: Ch. 1.1, Ch. 2, Ch. 4, and conclusion.

### State Legibility

Cohn gives an India-specific colonial history of what later state-legibility literature theorises more generally. He is especially useful alongside James C. Scott because Cohn supplies the archival colonial mechanism: survey, settlement, legal codification, and enumeration.

Use: Ch. 1 and Ch. 3.4.

## Internal Tensions and Silences

### 1. Cohn is not a land-records author

The book is foundational for colonial knowledge and useful for land administration history, but it is not directly about RoR, cadastral record maintenance, mutation, Odisha, DILRMP, or village-level record-ground mismatch. Its value is conceptual and genealogical.

### 2. Odisha is absent

Cohn's strongest empirical terrain is Bengal, upper India, Banaras, Madras, Mysore, and imperial institutions. Odisha must be connected through Bengal Presidency, Madras Presidency, Orissa Survey and Settlement Act, and later Odisha-specific revenue administration. The extension must be done by the thesis, not assumed from Cohn.

### 3. The postcolonial transition is asserted more than traced

Cohn and Dirks indicate that colonial knowledge persists after colonialism, but the book does not analyse postcolonial land administration. This PhD's originality lies partly in tracing that afterlife through DILRMP and Odisha fieldwork.

### 4. The village subject is underdeveloped

Although Cohn is historically anthropological, this book mostly works through official archives, texts, elites, institutions, and representational practices. The peasant whose land is measured or whose possession is misrecorded remains largely off-stage.

### 5. The state is sometimes too coherent

Cohn shows contradictions among Orientalists, utilitarians, administrators, scholars, and Indian intermediaries. Still, the broader frame can make "the British" appear more unified than colonial administration often was.

### 6. Chapter 5 is theoretically rich but thesis-weak

The cloth chapter is excellent cultural history, but the link to land records is too indirect. It should not be allowed to distract from Chapters 1 and 3.

### Active Debates Engaged

1. Presumptive vs. conclusive title: Chapter 3 supports the argument that revenue/property categories were built for enforceability and administration, not necessarily conclusive title.

3. Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy of reform: Cohn helps read digitisation as a continuation of documentation logic rather than a neutral fix.

4. Colonial revenue record to postcolonial title document: strongest fit. The book gives conceptual and historical backing to the claim that the record apparatus began as a revenue-administrative system.

5. Spatial-textual disjuncture: survey modality helps explain why spatial grids and textual records may diverge from lived ground possession.

6. RoR as welfare inclusion vs. exclusion: enumerative modality helps explain how official categories become gates to state recognition.

7. NIE efficiency logic vs. agrarian social relations: Cohn helps critique the assumption that cleaner records simply produce better outcomes.

8. Ownership vs. possession on the ground: Chapter 3 directly supports the idea that ownership categories simplify layered possession and obligation.

## Ideas and Questions Sparked

1. Is DILRMP best understood as a digital land-record project or as the latest phase of a much older documentation project?

2. Does digitisation resolve the colonial revenue record/title mismatch, or does it stabilise and reproduce it in a more authoritative form?

3. Are Bhulekh and Bhunaksha modern survey modalities: one textual, one spatial?

4. Who are the contemporary equivalents of Cohn's intermediaries: RI, Amin, data-entry operator, local computer centre, document writer, broker?

5. Does e-mutation attempt to bypass local intermediaries while actually depending on their translations of possession and inheritance?

6. How does the state decide which version of land reality counts: oral possession, field boundary, cadastral map, RoR, registered deed, or welfare database?

7. Are joint khata and partition disputes evidence that living family property relations exceed the categories of official records?

8. Can a conclusive-title project succeed without redoing the basic survey-settlement work that originally produced the record?

9. Does the Odisha case show that the state has digitised old presumptive records faster than it has reconciled them with ground realities?

10. What does Cohn miss that fieldwork can supply? Answer: the voices and strategies of record-holders, possessors, sharecroppers, women, landless households, and intermediaries.

## Final Use Verdict

Cohn should be used as a selective theoretical and historical source, not as a core empirical source. The book is load-bearing for:

1. colonial knowledge-power framing;
2. survey and settlement as documentation practices;
3. colonial legal reconstitution of property;
4. the genealogy of record-ground disjuncture;
5. DILRMP as a digital extension of land legibility.

The book is not load-bearing for:

1. Odisha-specific evidence;
2. DILRMP implementation details;
3. RoR procedures;
4. mutation process;
5. welfare scheme evidence;
6. village-level realities.

The thesis should cite Cohn in a disciplined way: use Chapter 1 and Chapter 3 heavily, use Chapter 4 only for the Mackenzie/survey passage, and skip Chapter 5 except for rare support on officialised custom.
