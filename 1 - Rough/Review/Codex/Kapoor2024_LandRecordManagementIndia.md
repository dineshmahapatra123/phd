## Core Argument

This working paper is valuable because it provides a clear contemporary map of the Indian land-records system at the exact point where digitisation, ULPIN, and DLT are being sold as governance fixes. Its overall tone is reformist and largely supportive of digital modernisation, but the paper is still useful for this thesis because it unintentionally shows why the land-record system remains fragmented, historically layered, and politically vulnerable.

The paper’s main argument is straightforward: India’s land administration is fragmented across revenue, registration, and survey departments; records are inconsistent, titles are presumptive rather than conclusive; mutation is slow and error-prone; and digitisation, integration, and eventually DLT can improve transparency, efficiency, and service delivery. In the language of the paper, the problem is a system that is still not fully interoperable, not fully updated, and not yet capable of delivering secure, conclusive land titles.

For this thesis, that makes the paper useful in two ways. First, it provides a contemporary institutional description of how the modern land-records architecture actually works: who holds what, where the bottlenecks are, and why the system keeps breaking down. Second, it gives the most policy-friendly version of the modernisation case, which is helpful to critique. The paper believes digitisation can fix fragmentation, but its own evidence shows that digitisation mostly replays old contradictions: stale survey baselines, silos across departments, privacy risks, data backlog, tenancy exclusion, and the persistence of presumptive title.

The paper therefore sits at the boundary between reform and critique. It wants a DLT-enabled future, but it also shows that DILRMP cannot work as a purely technical project because land rights in India are governed by law, administration, and political economy at the same time. That is the opening this thesis needs.

## The Land-Records System as a Fragmented State Apparatus

The working paper’s clearest contribution is its institutional map of land administration. It shows that land rights, survey and settlement, and registration are all split across different state departments, with different documents, officers, and workflows. The Record of Rights, cadastral maps, mutation entries, sale deeds, property tax receipts, and encumbrance certificates all circulate separately.

This is not just an administrative inconvenience. It is a structural condition. The paper shows that India does not have a single, unified land information system. Instead, it has overlapping and only partially synchronised records systems that are still shaped by colonial inheritance, state-level variation, and local bureaucratic practice. That is why land ownership is still presumptive, why disputes remain unresolved, and why digital records are often no more reliable than the paper files they replace.

For the thesis, this matters because it gives concrete form to the argument that the record system is not a neutral archive but a fragmented power structure. The state does not merely keep land records. It manages multiple partial records whose inconsistencies create room for discretion, delay, and capture.

## From Presumptive Title to Conclusive Title

A central theme in the paper is the move from presumptive to conclusive titling. The authors explain the Torrens model, the mirror principle, the curtain principle, and indemnity. The ideal is clear: the state should provide a title system that accurately reflects reality, eliminates the need for exhaustive historical searches, and indemnifies errors.

But the paper also shows why India is far from that model. Current land title is still presumptive, meaning that ownership is inferred from documents and possession rather than guaranteed by the state. Registration records the transaction, not the title. Mutation updates the revenue record, but it does not automatically settle the underlying legal status. In practice, this means that a person can have a registered deed and still face title uncertainty.

That is an important point for this thesis because it clarifies why DILRMP is so politically attractive. It promises to convert a weak, presumptive system into a stronger, more reliable one. Yet the paper’s own discussion shows that this conversion depends on far more than digitisation. It requires clear records, completed surveys, integrated departments, low backlog, trustworthy dispute resolution, and a governance framework strong enough to keep the system updated.

The paper treats these requirements as implementation challenges. The thesis reads them as evidence that title security cannot be produced by software alone.

## DILRMP as Administrative Modernisation

The paper’s discussion of DILRMP is especially useful because it shows how the state imagines the programme. The project includes computerisation of land records, survey and re-survey, computerisation of registration, modern record rooms, capacity building, multilingual interfaces, NGDRS, and linkage with Aadhaar and revenue courts. The vision is one of unified digital governance.

The paper also tracks newer tools such as Bhu-Aadhaar and ULPIN, which seek to create unique parcel identifiers tied to geospatial coordinates. The implied promise is that each parcel can be made fully traceable, interoperable, and administratively fixed.

This is where the thesis can sharpen the critique. The paper assumes that if the system becomes digital enough, state legibility will become social legibility. But digitisation does not automatically resolve the underlying questions of possession, customary rights, disputed boundaries, tenancy, or unequal access to state processes. A cleaner database can still be a database of injustice.

The paper does not say that outright, but it provides enough evidence to make the point. It notes the persistent backlog, siloed departments, unclear titles, privacy risks, and the difficulty of updating data in real time. Those are not transitional bugs; they are signs that the system is deeply political.

## The Limits of Digitisation

The most useful part of the paper, from this thesis’s perspective, is its own list of persistent problems.

First, the system is fragmented. Different departments hold different parts of the truth, and those datasets are not reliably harmonised.

Second, the system is outdated. Land records in many states still rely on colonial-era survey baselines or poor-quality maps.

Third, the system is legally incomplete. India still depends on registered sale deeds and mutation entries rather than conclusive titles.

Fourth, the system is administratively weak. Backlogs, delays, and poor interdepartmental coordination mean that even computerised records do not reliably reflect current ownership or occupation.

Fifth, the system is socially exclusionary. The paper itself notes that community ownership regimes, customary tenure, and non-landowning users are not easily captured by the dominant record format.

For the thesis, this is important because it shows that digitisation mainly addresses format, not power. It changes how records are stored and circulated, but not necessarily who controls them or whose rights they recognise.

---

## DLT as a Governance Fix

The paper’s most ambitious move is to propose Distributed Ledger Technology as a future architecture for land administration. It treats DLT as a way to create tamper-proof, distributed, interoperable, and transparent records. The appeal is obvious: if one source of truth is corrupted or outdated, a distributed system may reduce manipulation and improve recoverability.

But the paper also inadvertently reveals why this is not a clean solution. It notes that DLT still depends on legal and administrative frameworks, that it may hamper customary tenure, that private vendors dominate the ecosystem, and that governments must still handle governance, quality control, privacy, and legal enforceability.

In other words, the paper’s own argument shows that DLT does not remove the state. It reorganises the state’s informational capacity. That means the core issue remains political: who defines the records, who can update them, who can contest them, and whose version of land is considered authoritative.

That is precisely where the thesis can intervene. DLT may reduce some forms of tampering, but it does not resolve the structural gap between formal record and lived land relation. A distributed ledger can still encode exclusion, tenancy invisibility, and bureaucratic bias if those are what the state chooses to input.

## What the Paper Reveals About Exclusion

The working paper is especially useful where it discusses the exclusion of customary and non-landowning users. It notes that DILRMP and adjacent formalisation projects can turn communal rights into individual rights, erase common property resource regimes, and exclude tenants, pastoralists, and other users whose claims are not easily expressed as individual ownership.

This is one of the strongest overlaps with the thesis argument. The digitisation of land records does not just make records more visible. It can also narrow what counts as a valid land claim. That is a huge political change. It shifts the basis of recognition from social use and customary access to individually legible ownership.

The paper’s examples from Meghalaya, Karnataka, and Jharkhand show that this is not a theoretical concern. Digitisation can become a mechanism for reinterpretation, enclosure, and administrative reclassification. Errors made during data entry or resurvey can become durable because they are then propagated through digital systems and linked databases.

That is why the thesis should treat digitisation not as a cure for legibility failure, but as a possible amplifier of pre-existing classification mistakes.

---

## The Technical-Political Trap

The paper repeatedly frames the problem as technical: outdated data, incomplete maps, disconnected systems, lack of interoperability, privacy issues, and the need for capacity building. All of that is true, but the paper’s own examples show that the problem is also political.

The Tamil Nadu audit discussed in the paper makes this clear. Even where land records are heavily computerised, discrepancies remain between manual and digital records, many parcels are misclassified, and service delivery still fails because the underlying survey and integration problems are unresolved. That is a crucial lesson: technology can improve access and speed, but it cannot by itself guarantee correctness, legitimacy, or distributive justice.

This matters because the paper’s policy logic leans toward a technical fix for a political problem. The thesis can use that tension directly. If the system’s core defects are historical, legal, and political, then more digital infrastructure may simply make the defects more efficient.

---

## Critical Assessment

The paper is useful, but it has limits.

First, it is normatively committed to digitisation and DLT, so it tends to treat political problems as solvable through better architecture. That makes it more diagnostic than critical.

Second, it underplays tenancy and common-property conflict in favour of a state administration view of land.

Third, it gives less attention to the way digitisation can become a new layer of exclusion, especially for those without digital literacy, title documents, or access to grievance systems.

Fourth, the paper is strongest on system design, but weaker on how power shapes implementation. That is precisely the gap the thesis fills.
