# Chapter 6 Parcel-Level Fieldwork and Writing Guide

**Status:** Comprehensive architecture, refreshed and cross-checked June 2026  
**Purpose:** Parcel-survey protocol, data-analysis plan, writing guide and prioritised
reference architecture  
**Provisional chapter title:** *Parcel-Level Findings: Record, Map and Possession*

## Why Chapter 6 Requires a Distinct Strategy

Chapter 5 studies villages, households, social relations, administrative encounters and
welfare outcomes. Chapter 6 must change scale. Its unit of analysis is the **parcel-record
configuration**: the relationship among a plot in the textual RoR, its representation in the
cadastral map, the boundary or occupation observed in the field, and the rights and uses
claimed by relevant actors.

The proposal commits to selecting 15 parcels in each of two villages and comparing:

- Bhulekh RoRs;
- Bhunaksha cadastral maps;
- physical survey evidence;
- ownership and possession;
- textual and spatial discrepancies.

The chapter should therefore be a **forensic, comparative and mixed-evidence audit of 30
parcel cases**, not a conventional land survey and not a second village-profile chapter.

It cannot legally adjudicate ownership or fix a boundary. It can identify, document and
explain correspondence, mismatch, ambiguity and the limits of the modernised record.

## The Central Methodological Principle

Do not compare one official record with an undifferentiated category called "ground
reality." Compare separate objects:

1. **Textual record:** what Bhulekh and the certified RoR state.
2. **Cadastral representation:** what Bhunaksha or the source cadastral map depicts.
3. **Field geometry:** what the research survey can measure.
4. **Physical occupation/use:** what is visible or observed at the time of fieldwork.
5. **Possession claim:** who says they possess the parcel.
6. **Cultivation/use claim:** who actually cultivates or uses it.
7. **Local recognition:** what neighbours, co-sharers and village actors recognise.
8. **Administrative/legal status:** mutation orders, deeds, pattas, court orders,
   acquisition or settlement proceedings.

These layers may agree, partially agree or conflict. No single layer automatically supplies
the definitive legal truth.

## The Argument Chapter 6 Should Investigate

The final argument must follow the evidence, but the chapter can test the following:

1. Digital availability is not equivalent to spatial updating. A Bhunaksha parcel can be a
   precise digital reproduction of an obsolete or locally displaced analogue map.
2. Textual and spatial records can diverge independently. Names and shares may be current
   while parcel geometry remains old; a map may reflect a subdivision while the RoR does
   not, or vice versa.
3. Parcel discrepancies have different sources and consequences. Area mismatch, positional
   shift, boundary displacement, split/merge, obsolete use classification and owner-possessor
   divergence should not be combined into one error rate.
4. Some discrepancies are products of measurement and map transformation; others are
   consequences of inheritance, oral partition, tenancy, informal exchange, acquisition,
   encroachment, erosion, road construction or administrative non-updating.
5. A record can be technically consistent yet socially inaccurate. The RoR and map may
   agree with one another while both fail to represent current possession or use.
6. A parcel can be socially uncontested but administratively ambiguous. Local boundary
   recognition may permit cultivation despite an outdated multi-owner RoR.
7. Discrepancies are likely to be concentrated in particular parcel types: government or
   common land, river/nala edges, village boundaries, converted land, subdivided holdings,
   inherited multi-owner land and parcels affected by infrastructure.
8. Continuous mutation can update ordinary transactions but cannot substitute for resurvey
   where the underlying cadastral geometry, village boundary or network of parcel relations
   has changed.
9. High-technology survey can improve coordinates and geometry, but adjudication,
   participation, objection and correction remain necessary to convert measured space into an
   authoritative rights record.

## What Chapter 6 Can and Cannot Establish

### It can establish

- whether sampled documentary sources agree;
- whether measured dimensions and areas correspond within stated tolerances;
- whether parcel shape, topology and location can be matched;
- whether visible occupation/use corresponds to the record;
- whether respondents and adjacent claimants agree on boundaries;
- the mechanisms that produced selected discrepancies;
- the practical limits of Bhulekh-Bhunaksha integration in the sample;
- differences between the two villages and parcel categories.

### It cannot establish by itself

- conclusive legal title;
- the lawful boundary in a disputed case;
- statewide or district-wide prevalence;
- causal effects of DILRMP;
- exact positional error from chain measurements alone;
- that a visible bund is necessarily the legal cadastral boundary;
- that possession establishes ownership;
- that every mismatch is an administrative error;
- that newer technology produces socially legitimate outcomes.

## 6.1 Introduction and Methodology

### Function of the introduction

The introduction should:

1. Carry forward Chapter 5's finding that record-possession divergence is socially produced.
2. Explain why parcel-level verification is needed.
3. Define the objects being compared.
4. State the 30-parcel design and its limits.
5. Preview the principal discrepancy dimensions.
6. Explain the transition from lived tenure to measured and mapped land.

### Recommended opening

Open with one parcel whose classification changes depending on the evidence consulted:

- Bhulekh names an ancestor;
- Bhunaksha shows one undivided polygon;
- the field contains two separately cultivated plots;
- family members recognise an oral partition;
- no map correction has occurred.

The point is not that one source is simply false. The case demonstrates that a parcel is
simultaneously a geometry, an administrative identifier, a bundle of claims, a material field
and a history.

## Research Questions for Chapter 6

1. Can each sampled Bhulekh plot be unambiguously linked to a Bhunaksha polygon and a
   field parcel?
2. Do RoR area, mapped area and measured field area correspond?
3. Do parcel shape, boundary lengths, adjacency and location correspond?
4. Who owns, possesses, cultivates and controls each parcel?
5. Which social and administrative events produced mismatch?
6. Are discrepancies associated with particular land categories or parcel histories?
7. Does digital linkage represent substantive reconciliation or only database connection?
8. Which cases could be corrected through routine mutation, and which require demarcation,
   resurvey, objection or adjudication?

## Unit of Analysis

The preferred unit is a **parcel dossier**, not simply a plot number.

Each dossier should include:

- anonymous parcel ID;
- village;
- Bhulekh khata and plot linkage;
- Bhunaksha polygon linkage;
- recorded area and classification;
- map source, scale and date;
- field survey geometry;
- observed use;
- recorded right-holder(s);
- possessor(s);
- cultivator/user(s);
- relevant household history;
- mutation, partition, deed or court evidence;
- adjacent claimant confirmation or disagreement;
- discrepancy codes;
- confidence grade;
- photographs/sketches, where consented;
- analytical conclusion.

## Parcel Selection

### Existing proposal commitment

The proposal specifies 15 parcels per village, selected with the landholder's consent. This is
a small purposive sample. It can illuminate types and mechanisms but cannot estimate the
village-wide prevalence of map or record error.

### Recommended purposive stratification

If parcel selection is not complete, maximise variation across:

- small, medium and relatively large parcels;
- regular and irregular shapes;
- agricultural and homestead parcels;
- irrigated and unirrigated land;
- single-owner and multi-owner RoRs;
- recent mutation and old/unmutated record;
- inherited and purchased land;
- owner-cultivated and tenant-cultivated land;
- oral partition;
- village-boundary or road/water-edge location;
- government/common/deity land edge;
- converted or acquired land;
- apparently clear record and known discrepancy.

### Illustrative quota for each village

| Parcel type | Suggested minimum |
|---|---:|
| Uncontested, apparently updated parcel | 3 |
| Inheritance or multi-owner parcel | 3 |
| Oral partition/subdivision | 2 |
| Tenant- or non-owner-cultivated | 2 |
| Boundary, road, nala or common-land edge | 2 |
| Converted/acquired/changed-use | 1 |
| Government/common/deity-status issue | 1 |
| Other locally important case | 1 |

Categories can overlap. The objective is analytical range, not proportional allocation.

### Selection-bias disclosure

Consent-based selection may underrepresent:

- disputed parcels;
- absent owners;
- powerful encroachers;
- women without documentary control;
- tenants whose landlords oppose disclosure;
- inaccessible terrain;
- parcels whose boundaries cannot be safely identified.

Report refusals, substitutions and inaccessible cases. Never replace a difficult parcel
silently.

## Define the Parcel Before Measuring

Distinguish:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Recorded plot | Unit identified by plot number in the RoR |
| Cadastral polygon | Geometry associated with a plot number in the map |
| Possession parcel | Area physically controlled by a person/household |
| Cultivation unit | Contiguous area managed as one field |
| Survey polygon | Geometry measured by the research team |
| Legal parcel | Legally recognised spatial unit, which the research does not adjudicate |

One recorded plot may contain several cultivation units. Several recorded plots may be
cultivated as one unit. This is a substantive finding, not a data-cleaning inconvenience.

## Source Provenance Protocol

For every source, record:

- source institution or portal;
- retrieval/certification date;
- village, khata and plot identifier;
- map scale;
- settlement or consolidation series;
- Sabik or Hal status;
- whether the source is a scan, digitised vector, portal image or certified copy;
- coordinate reference system, if known;
- update date or mutation status;
- image resolution or export method;
- visible damage, distortion or missing labels;
- legal/informational status.

### Source hierarchy

Do not assume the public portal is the master record. Where feasible compare:

1. certified Tahasil RoR;
2. Bhulekh display;
3. Tahasil/source cadastral map;
4. Bhunaksha display/export;
5. mutation order and corrected map;
6. field and household evidence.

If sources differ, preserve the difference rather than choosing one without explanation.

## Preparing the Cadastral Geometry

The method used to derive a map polygon is itself part of the evidence.

### Preferred order

1. Obtain an official vector export with metadata, if available.
2. Otherwise obtain the highest-quality official raster or scanned map.
3. Record the original scale and sheet.
4. Georeference only with identifiable, stable control points and report residuals.
5. Digitise plot boundaries at a consistent display scale.
6. validate plot labels, adjacency, gaps, overlaps and village edges.
7. Preserve the untransformed source and every processing step.

### Portal-image caution

A browser screenshot or resized portal image is not a survey plan. It may be adequate for
plot identification or illustration but not for metric area or positional analysis unless its
scale, transformation and coordinate properties are independently established.

If only a non-georeferenced image is available:

- compare topology and relative shape;
- use dimensions only where the source scale is reliable;
- report map-derived area as approximate;
- do not calculate absolute displacement or IoU.

## Survey Equipment Strategy

The method and claims must match the instrument.

### Track A: Chain/tape and local traverse

Use when fieldwork employed a Gunter's chain, steel tape or measuring tape without
survey-grade GNSS control.

Can support:

- side-length comparison;
- diagonal comparison;
- local parcel shape reconstruction;
- approximate horizontal area;
- comparison with map dimensions after scale conversion;
- documentation of visible subdivisions or consolidation.

Cannot support:

- reliable absolute coordinates;
- georeferenced positional displacement;
- centroid shift in a common coordinate system;
- legal demarcation;
- precise overlay with Bhunaksha where the map itself is ungeoreferenced.

### Track B: Total station or survey-grade RTK/DGNSS

Can additionally support:

- georeferenced parcel vertices;
- coordinate-based polygon area and perimeter;
- parcel overlay;
- positional offset;
- centroid displacement;
- boundary-distance measures;
- reconstruction tied to control points.

Requires:

- known datum and projection;
- calibrated equipment;
- control or CORS reference;
- survey log;
- accuracy report/fix status;
- trained operator;
- clear distinction between measured occupation boundary and legal boundary.

### Track C: Handheld or smartphone GNSS

Use for:

- reconnaissance;
- locating parcels;
- field photos and waypoints;
- approximate village mapping.

Do not use ordinary smartphone tracks as evidence of decimetre cadastral accuracy. Typical
error can exceed the width of small parcels and varies with canopy, buildings, satellite
geometry and device.

### Recommended hierarchy

If repeat fieldwork is possible:

1. Use RTK-GNSS or total station for a subset or all parcels.
2. Use tape/chain for boundary lengths and independent checks.
3. Use smartphone GNSS only for navigation and metadata.
4. Retain the original chain method as part of the research history, but do not overstate it.

## Gunter's Chain Protocol

A Gunter's chain is 66 feet long and conventionally divided into 100 links. Ten square
chains equal one acre.

### Unit controls

Use one canonical analysis unit, preferably square metres, while retaining the original unit.

| Unit | Conversion |
|---|---:|
| 1 acre | 4,046.8564224 square metres |
| 1 acre | 100 decimals |
| 1 decimal | 40.468564224 square metres |
| 1 decimal | 435.6 square feet |
| 1 chain | 66 feet / 20.1168 metres |
| 1 link | 0.66 feet / 0.201168 metres |

Record whether an area stated as "decimal" means the Odisha/Bengal one-hundredth-acre
unit. Do not convert rounded local reports into artificially precise metric values.

### Before measurement

- inspect and calibrate chain length against a known baseline;
- record instrument, nominal length and correction;
- use consistent units;
- identify slope, vegetation, water and obstruction;
- identify boundary markers with relevant participants;
- create a field sketch and vertex labels;
- record weather and ground condition.

### During measurement

- measure horizontal rather than slope distance where possible;
- range the line accurately;
- maintain consistent tension;
- prevent kinks and sag;
- use arrows/pegs and count full chain lengths;
- measure each side at least twice;
- measure one or more diagonals;
- document offsets around obstacles;
- record who indicated each boundary;
- do not move boundary markers.

### Error sources to disclose

- incorrect chain length;
- poor ranging and alignment;
- slope;
- sag and inconsistent tension;
- temperature;
- counting or transcription error;
- inability to occupy a corner;
- curved or irregular boundary;
- vegetation or water;
- boundary marker uncertainty;
- deliberate or inadvertent respondent influence.

### Area computation

Do not calculate irregular parcel area from side lengths alone. Use:

- triangulation with measured diagonals;
- perpendicular offsets;
- local-coordinate traverse;
- coordinate geometry;
- GIS polygon only where vertices are validly reconstructed.

Record the computational method parcel by parcel.

## Boundary Identification Protocol

Measurement precision cannot cure boundary uncertainty.

For every boundary segment classify the evidence:

| Code | Boundary evidence |
|---|---|
| B1 | Permanent official monument/pillar |
| B2 | Stable physical feature: wall, road, canal, nala |
| B3 | Field bund/hedge mutually recognised |
| B4 | Corner indicated by possessor and adjacent claimant |
| B5 | Corner indicated only by possessor |
| B6 | Inferred from cultivation/use |
| B7 | Obscured or inaccessible |
| B8 | Actively disputed |
| B9 | Reconstructed from map dimensions only |

### Participation

Where safe and feasible, request presence of:

- possessor or recorded right-holder;
- adjacent possessor/claimant;
- co-sharer;
- RI/Amin or knowledgeable village functionary where officially permitted;
- women and other claimants whose rights may be hidden by household representation.

Consent from one "landholder" is insufficient to establish a contested shared boundary.

### Disputed boundaries

If participants disagree:

- do not choose a winner;
- record each claimed line separately if safe;
- identify the disputed segment;
- document documentary bases;
- assign low confidence;
- exclude from positional-accuracy statistics requiring a unique reference boundary;
- retain as a substantive rights/governance case.

## Survey Workflow

### Phase 1: Desk preparation

1. Create parcel ID.
2. Obtain RoR and map copies.
3. Confirm plot/khata link.
4. Record map scale and series.
5. Prepare field map and source checklist.
6. Identify household and adjacent claimants.
7. Note expected split, merge, access or land-use issue.

### Phase 2: Field identification

8. Obtain consent.
9. Walk the full parcel.
10. Identify and code vertices and boundary evidence.
11. Record possession, cultivation and use.
12. Photograph features with consent.
13. Draw field sketch.
14. Record disputes, inaccessible segments and environmental change.

### Phase 3: Measurement

15. Measure sides, diagonals, offsets or coordinates.
16. Repeat or close the survey.
17. Record equipment and quality indicators.
18. Conduct an independent check where possible.

### Phase 4: Post-field processing

19. Enter measurements without overwriting raw data.
20. Construct survey polygon.
21. Calculate closure and area.
22. Digitise/prepare cadastral polygon using documented method.
23. Link textual and spatial attributes.
24. Compute only metrics supported by the data.
25. Assign discrepancy codes and confidence.
26. Write parcel memo before cross-case analysis.

## Measurement Quality Control

### Raw-data integrity

- retain original field book images;
- never replace raw observations;
- record corrections with date and reason;
- use stable parcel and vertex IDs;
- double-enter critical measurements;
- preserve original map exports;
- keep a processing log and software version.

### Repeatability

For at least a subset:

- repeat measurement on a different day;
- use a second measurer;
- compare chain/tape with RTK/total station where available;
- re-identify vertices without viewing prior values;
- report repeat difference.

### Closure

For a closed traverse, report:

- linear misclosure;
- perimeter;
- relative closure ratio;
- any adjustment method.

Do not create apparent accuracy by mathematically forcing a poor traverse to close without
reporting the original misclosure.

### Tolerance

Do not adopt one universal tolerance after seeing the results.

Tolerance should consider:

- source-map scale;
- survey instrument;
- parcel size and shape;
- terrain and visibility;
- boundary evidence;
- official standards relevant to the source;
- purpose of comparison.

The Odisha Technical Manual specifies, for official high-technology survey, nominal
accuracies of approximately +/-5 cm for urban areas at 1:500, +/-10 cm for village areas at
1:1000 and +/-20 cm for agricultural areas at 1:4000. These standards are not automatically
applicable to a research survey using a chain or consumer GNSS. Use them as a benchmark
for official programme claims, not as a claim about achieved research accuracy.

## Comparison Dimensions

### 1. Identifier correspondence

Ask:

- Does Bhulekh plot number exist in Bhunaksha?
- Is the label unique and legible?
- Does one RoR plot correspond to one map polygon?
- Are there zero, duplicate or unmatched plot labels?
- Does mutation create a new plot absent from the map?

### 2. Attribute correspondence

Compare:

- khata;
- plot number;
- area;
- kisam/classification;
- irrigation;
- right-holder name and share;
- rent/cess where relevant;
- acquisition, restriction or mutation note.

### 3. Area correspondence

For parcel \(i\):

```text
Signed area difference_i = Field area_i - RoR area_i

Absolute area difference_i = |Field area_i - RoR area_i|

Percentage area difference_i =
    |Field area_i - RoR area_i| / RoR area_i * 100
```

Also compare:

- mapped area versus RoR area;
- field area versus mapped area;
- all three in one table.

Do not use percentage difference where the denominator is zero or unreliable.

### 4. Boundary-length correspondence

For matched segments:

```text
Length difference = Field length - Map-derived length
Percentage length difference = |difference| / Map-derived length * 100
```

Only compare segments confidently matched between sources.

### 5. Shape correspondence

Where both polygons are valid in a common coordinate system, consider:

- visual overlay;
- perimeter difference;
- compactness;
- intersection-over-union;
- symmetric difference area;
- segment-level offset.

```text
Intersection over Union (IoU) =
    Area(Field polygon intersect Map polygon) /
    Area(Field polygon union Map polygon)
```

IoU is invalid if polygons have not been reliably georeferenced to the same coordinate
system.

### 6. Positional correspondence

Only for georeferenced data:

- vertex displacement;
- centroid displacement;
- mean boundary offset;
- maximum boundary offset;
- RMSE for well-matched control/check points.

Do not compute RMSE using boundary points whose correspondence is uncertain.

### 7. Topological correspondence

Classify:

- one-to-one match;
- one recorded plot to multiple field parcels;
- multiple recorded plots to one field unit;
- overlap;
- gap;
- sliver;
- shifted adjacency;
- missing access/path;
- village-boundary mismatch.

Topology can matter more than small metric error because it affects which parcels touch,
which parcel has access and whether the same land is represented twice or omitted.

### 8. Rights correspondence

Compare:

- recorded holder;
- claimed owner;
- possessor;
- cultivator;
- person controlling transfer;
- person receiving welfare/compensation.

### 9. Use correspondence

Compare recorded kisam with:

- current crop;
- fallow;
- homestead;
- road;
- water;
- plantation;
- commercial/non-agricultural use;
- common use;
- physically transformed or eroded land.

Observed use at one date does not prove permanent legal conversion.

### 10. Temporal correspondence

Record the effective date of:

- the settlement/cadastral map;
- the RoR copy;
- the latest mutation;
- imagery used for mapping;
- the field survey;
- the possession/use account.

A difference between a 1960s map and a 2026 field observation is not automatically a
digitisation error. The chapter must identify whether the system was expected to update that
dimension and through which procedure.

## Discrepancy Typology

Use multiple codes where necessary.

### Documentary and identifier

- **P1:** Bhulekh plot absent from Bhunaksha.
- **P2:** Bhunaksha polygon absent from RoR.
- **P3:** duplicate, zero or ambiguous plot identifier.
- **P4:** portal and certified record differ.

### Area and geometry

- **P5:** RoR-map area mismatch.
- **P6:** RoR-field area mismatch.
- **P7:** map-field area mismatch.
- **P8:** boundary-length mismatch.
- **P9:** shape mismatch.
- **P10:** positional shift.

### Topology

- **P11:** recorded plot split in the field.
- **P12:** recorded plots merged in use/possession.
- **P13:** overlap or gap.
- **P14:** adjacency/access mismatch.
- **P15:** village-boundary mismatch.

### Rights and possession

- **P16:** deceased person remains recorded.
- **P17:** multi-owner record with separate possession.
- **P18:** recorded holder differs from possessor.
- **P19:** recorded holder differs from cultivator.
- **P20:** registered or mutated change not propagated.
- **P21:** competing possession/boundary claims.

### Use and public land

- **P22:** recorded classification differs from observed use.
- **P23:** government/common/deity/forest edge occupied or disputed.
- **P24:** road, canal, nala, erosion or infrastructure transformed parcel.
- **P25:** acquisition/conversion absent or incomplete in the record.

### Measurement/status limitation

- **P26:** boundary cannot be confidently identified.
- **P27:** source map cannot be reliably georeferenced.
- **P28:** parcel inaccessible or partially measured.
- **P29:** evidence conflict prevents classification.
- **P30:** sources describe materially different dates and cannot be compared as if
  contemporaneous.
- **P31:** source scale, resolution or transformation is insufficient for the proposed metric.

P26-P31 are not failures. They are necessary statements of evidentiary limitation.

## Confidence Framework

Assign separate confidence for geometry and rights.

### Geometry confidence

- **G-A High:** closed survey; reliable instrument/control; stable or mutually confirmed
  boundaries; repeat check.
- **G-B Moderate:** complete measurement; reasonable boundary evidence; local coordinate
  geometry; limited positional evidence.
- **G-C Low:** incomplete/obscured boundary, single claimant identification, poor closure or
  uncertain map transformation.
- **G-D Indeterminate:** active dispute, inaccessible parcel or unmatched source.

### Rights/possession confidence

- **R-A High:** record plus consistent documents, possession observation and agreement of
  relevant parties.
- **R-B Moderate:** plausible documentary and interview support with minor gaps.
- **R-C Low:** single assertion, absent co-sharers or conflicting testimony.
- **R-D Contested:** unresolved competing claims.

Do not merge these into one score. A parcel can have excellent geometry and contested
rights.

## Classification of Parcel Outcomes

Avoid a binary accurate/inaccurate conclusion.

| Outcome | Description |
|---|---|
| C1 Correspondent | Text, map and field correspond within justified tolerance |
| C2 Administratively current, spatially legacy | Text updated; map remains old |
| C3 Spatially identifiable, rights outdated | Polygon identifiable; recorded holders/shares stale |
| C4 Socially partitioned, administratively joint | Separate possession/use under one joint record |
| C5 Use transformed | Current material use differs from record |
| C6 Public/common boundary pressure | Mismatch associated with state/common/deity/forest land |
| C7 Technically uncertain | Source or instrument cannot support conclusion |
| C8 Socially/legal contested | Competing claims require adjudication |
| C9 Multi-dimensional mismatch | Several independent discrepancy dimensions |

## Ethics, Safety and Legal Cautions

### Consent

Obtain separate consent for:

- interview;
- document review/copy;
- field entry;
- measurement;
- GPS coordinates;
- photographs;
- publication of anonymised map.

### Neighbour and co-sharer rights

A possessor cannot ethically authorise entry onto a neighbour's land or representation of
another person's claim. Confirm access and participation separately where needed.

### Conflict risk

Stop or modify measurement if:

- a boundary dispute escalates;
- a participant feels threatened;
- measurement could be mistaken for official demarcation;
- a powerful actor attempts to exclude another claimant;
- police or administrative permission is required;
- the parcel concerns sacred, burial, forest or community land requiring collective consent.

### Researcher representation

State clearly:

- the survey is for academic research;
- it is not an official demarcation;
- it will not change Bhulekh/Bhunaksha;
- measurements do not confer title;
- participation does not guarantee correction or benefit.

### Spatial data security

- remove public khata/plot numbers where re-identification is likely;
- jitter or generalise map location for publication;
- store exact coordinates securely;
- never publish Aadhaar, mobile, bank or signature data;
- suppress sensitive disputed parcel maps;
- separate identity keys from spatial files.

## Methodological Limitations

The final chapter should disclose:

- purposive, consent-dependent parcel selection;
- small sample of 30;
- potential underrepresentation of disputes and absent owners;
- limitations of chain/tape or consumer GNSS;
- uncertain or lost boundary monuments;
- map age, scale and physical distortion;
- differences between portal and source map;
- seasonal visibility of bunds and water;
- survey access and terrain;
- respondents' strategic claims;
- inability to adjudicate legal title;
- measurement and digitisation error;
- possible date mismatch among RoR, map, imagery and field observation.

## Topology: Split, Merge, Adjacency and Access

This may be the chapter's most substantively important spatial analysis.

### Split

One recorded plot has become:

- inheritance shares;
- oral partition;
- sale fragments;
- homestead subdivision;
- separate cultivation units.

Ask whether:

- the RoR changed;
- plot suffix/new number exists;
- map changed;
- access was created;
- area totals reconcile;
- welfare or transfer is affected.

### Merge

Several recorded plots function as:

- one field;
- one homestead;
- one acquired or converted site;
- one water body or road.

The absence of a visible internal bund is not necessarily an error if legal plot identity
remains.

### Adjacency and access

Identify:

- missing path;
- blocked access;
- nala/road movement;
- parcel overlap/gap;
- changed village edge.

## Evaluating Integration

For every parcel ask:

1. Is the RoR linked to a cadastral polygon?
2. Is the linkage one-to-one?
3. Do area and identifier agree?
4. Does the polygon represent the current parcel?
5. Are current right-holders recorded?
6. Is possession/cultivation represented?
7. Can an error be corrected through routine mutation?
8. Has correction propagated across systems?

Integration is substantive only when these questions can be answered coherently.

## Evaluating Whether Resurvey Is Necessary

### Routine mutation may be adequate where

- a registered transfer concerns an identifiable unchanged parcel;
- succession changes names but not geometry;
- the source map is reliable;
- co-sharers agree;
- no boundary or public-land issue exists.

### Demarcation or map correction may be needed where

- a boundary is identifiable but not reflected;
- mutation altered parcel identity without spatial update;
- a subdivision requires new geometry;
- a local boundary marker can be reconciled with the cadastral network.

### Wider resurvey/settlement may be needed where

- source map cannot be reliably georeferenced;
- systematic village-wide shift or deformation exists;
- numerous splits/merges have accumulated;
- roads, rivers, acquisition or settlement have transformed the parcel fabric;
- public/common and village boundaries are broadly contested;
- record provenance is missing;
- isolated corrections would create new overlaps and gaps.

### Adjudication is needed where

- parties dispute ownership or boundary;
- documents conflict;
- adverse possession, inheritance or customary rights are contested;
- measurement cannot decide the applicable right.

## Implications for ULPIN

ULPIN can provide a stable identifier only if the underlying spatial unit is stable and
correctly resolved.

Ask:

- What happens when one parcel splits?
- What happens when several plots are cultivated as one?
- Is ULPIN attached to legal plot, cadastral polygon or observed occupation?
- Can a coordinate-based ID stabilise an obsolete geometry?
- How are disputed and overlapping parcels treated?
- Does identifier uniqueness imply rights certainty?

Do not equate unique identification with conclusive title.

## Implications for Conclusive Titling

The parcel evidence should test prerequisites:

- complete chain of transactions;
- current parties and shares;
- reliable parcel geometry;
- absence or resolution of competing claims;
- rights, restrictions and liabilities attached;
- accessible objection and compensation mechanisms;
- state responsibility for error.

A technically precise polygon with unresolved rights is not a conclusive title. An agreed
right attached to unreliable geometry is also insufficient.

## Quality-Control Checklist

### Identification

- Every parcel has a stable anonymous ID.
- Plot, khata, village and map series are verified.
- One-to-many and many-to-one links are preserved.
- Source dates are recorded.

### Measurement

- Instrument and calibration are documented.
- Units are standardised.
- Raw observations are preserved.
- Closure/repeat checks are reported.
- Slope and obstacles are addressed.
- Georeferenced claims use survey-grade control.

### Boundary

- Boundary evidence is coded.
- Adjacent claimants are considered.
- Disputed segments are not averaged away.
- Visible bund is not automatically called legal boundary.
- Academic survey is not represented as demarcation.

### Analysis

- Area, shape, location, topology, attribution and use are separate.
- Tolerances are justified in advance.
- Low-confidence parcels are retained but flagged.
- Sensitivity analysis is shown.
- Small parcel percentage effects are explained.
- Village comparison remains exploratory.

### Legal and social interpretation

- RoR is not treated as conclusive title.
- Possession is not treated as ownership.
- Technical precision is not treated as legitimacy.
- Local agreement is not treated as legal adjudication.
- Women's, tenants' and co-sharers' claims are not erased.
- Public/common land is handled cautiously.

### Ethics

- Exact coordinates and identifiers are secured.
- Consent covers spatial publication.
- No map creates new conflict.
- Allegations are anonymised and attributed.
- Refusals and field interruptions are documented.

## Suggested Chapter Conclusion

The conclusion should answer:

1. How many sampled parcels could be matched across RoR, map and field?
2. Which dimensions corresponded and which diverged?
3. Which discrepancies were technical and which reflected social or administrative change?
4. Which parcel types were most difficult to reconcile?
5. Did Bhulekh-Bhunaksha linkage amount to substantive integration?
6. Which problems could routine mutation address?
7. Which require demarcation, resurvey or adjudication?
8. What do the findings imply for ULPIN and conclusive titling?
9. How do parcel findings refine Chapter 5's account of village land governance?

The chapter's strongest possible conclusion is not that the official map is inaccurate and the
field is true. It is that modern land administration must reconcile several forms of evidence
and authority. Technical measurement can reveal geometric inconsistency, but only an
institutional process combining survey, participation, notice, objection and adjudication can
produce an authoritative and socially legitimate parcel record.
