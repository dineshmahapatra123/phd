# No Condition Is Permanent: The Social Dynamics of Agrarian Change in Sub-Saharan Africa

Year: 1993  
Type: Book  
Source: `9 - Knowledge_base/sources/No Condition Is Permanent_ The Social Dynamics of Agrarian Change in Sub-Saharan Africa.md`

## Core Argument

Berry's central argument is that agrarian change in sub-Saharan Africa cannot be explained by treating property rights, contracts, markets, or state rules as fixed institutional givens. Access to productive resources - land, labour, capital, political authority, and social recognition - is continuously negotiated through social relations. The title's implication is methodological as much as historical: no institutional condition, no customary arrangement, no title, no registration, and no state reform should be assumed permanent.

For this PhD, the book matters because it gives a strong theoretical language for the gap between formal land records and ground-level claims. Berry's argument suggests that the record-reality gap is not only a technical error or mutation lag. It can also be the predictable outcome of property functioning as a social process. Records may name owners, but access to land may continue to depend on possession, inheritance, kinship, caste/community membership, tenancy, gendered labour contributions, local authority, and negotiated recognition.

Berry's strongest thesis-use is not African agrarian history as such. It is the conceptual warning that formalisation does not necessarily settle claims. It may instead become another arena in which claims are made, contested, reopened, and socially mediated.

## Conceptual Architecture

### Access as the Core Category

Berry shifts the analytical unit from ownership to access. The question is not simply "who owns land?" but how people obtain, defend, and use claims to land, labour, income, and authority. This is directly useful for Odisha because RoR ownership and actual land access need not coincide.

PhD use: Chapter 5 should treat possession, mutation, inheritance, informal partition, sharecropping, encroachment, and welfare eligibility as access practices, not as deviations from a clean title model.

### Property Rights as Social Process

Chapter 5 is the book's most important chapter for this thesis. Berry rejects the assumption that ambiguous property rights are merely inefficient or pre-modern. Ambiguity may be actively maintained because it leaves room for negotiation, inclusion, and future claims.

PhD use: This gives theoretical backing to the field observation that multi-owner RoRs, family partitions, caste-lane possession, and unresolved inheritance disputes may not simply be administrative backlogs. They may be socially sustained arrangements.

### Custom as Contestation

Berry argues that colonial rule did not simply freeze custom, nor did written customary law settle local debates. Colonial officials often sought fixed rules, but local actors continued to reinterpret custom in relation to land, authority, and political belonging.

PhD use: This is a useful comparator for Indian Survey and Settlement records. Settlement records may claim to capture existing tenure, but the act of recording can enter into ongoing disputes rather than end them.

### Social Networks as Resource Infrastructure

Berry treats kinship, patronage, community membership, litigation networks, local authority, and social status as mechanisms of access. People invest in these networks because their claims to land and other resources depend on being able to mobilise recognition and support.

PhD use: This sharpens the analysis of revenue intermediaries, local patrons, kin negotiations, caste networks, and bureaucratic brokerage in mutation and record correction.

### Formalisation as Incomplete Settlement

Berry's discussion of Kenyan land registration is especially useful. Registration did not eliminate family, lineage, and customary claims. In some cases, adjudication before registration opened the door for older or dormant group claims to reappear.

PhD use: This directly supports the critique of DILRMP and conclusive-title optimism. Digitisation or registration can organise claims into state-readable form, but it may not dissolve the social life of those claims.

## Empirical Terrain or Evidence and Examples Used

### Comparative African Agrarian Systems

The book compares rural economies in Ghana, Nigeria, Kenya, and Zambia, especially through colonial and postcolonial change. These cases should not be imported directly into Odisha as empirical analogues. Their value is theoretical: they show how commercialisation, state intervention, land scarcity, and social authority interact in agrarian settings.

### Chapter 1 - Introduction, pp. 3-21

This chapter sets up the argument against both neoclassical and deterministic Marxist explanations. Berry insists that resource access is shaped by power, culture, social relations, and material resources together.

For the thesis, Chapter 1 is useful because it gives a vocabulary for why land records cannot be interpreted only through institutional economics. NIE explains why secure rights might lower transaction costs, but Berry explains why actors may keep claims open, invest in social networks, and avoid definitive exclusion.

### Chapter 2 - Indirect Rule and Farmers' Access to Resources, pp. 22-42

This chapter shows how colonial indirect rule made access to resources depend on contested interpretations of custom, social identity, and local authority. Colonial attempts to codify native law did not settle disputes; they often created new incentives to argue over tradition and belonging.

For the thesis, this is useful for Chapter 2's historical land administration framing. It supports the argument that the colonial state did not merely discover tenure categories; it helped create arenas where those categories became politically valuable.

### Chapter 5 - Access to Land, pp. 101-134

This is the key chapter. Berry directly engages property-rights theory and land policy. She argues that land access remained tied to social networks and political processes even where land had been formally sold or registered.

The chapter's most important empirical mechanisms are:
- competing claims based on purchase, first clearance, family membership, labour investment, status, and customary authority;
- the distinction between "locals" and "strangers" as a basis of land claims;
- courts and customary tribunals as arenas where land rights could be reopened;
- registration and reform as processes that often created new opportunities for contestation.

For the thesis, this is the best source for theorising ownership versus possession and the limits of formal land-record correction.

### Chapter 7 - Investing in Networks, pp. 159-180

This chapter explains why farmers invest in social relations rather than only in productive assets. Ceremonies, litigation, education, dependents, community projects, and patronage can strengthen a claimant's status and ability to negotiate access.

For the thesis, this chapter is useful for interpreting the everyday political economy around land records: document writers, revenue-office intermediaries, caste or kin mobilisation, informal payments, litigation, and local political brokerage.

## Theoretical Lineage

### Against Simple Property-Rights Formalisation

Berry directly challenges the policy assumption that clearer individual property rights will automatically improve investment and resource allocation. She does not deny that formal rights matter. Her point is that rights operate within social and political processes that formalisation may not control.

Use with: [[Land Titling]], [[Tenure Security]], [[Record-Reality Gap]], [[Presumptive vs Conclusive Title]].

### In Dialogue with New Institutional Economics

Berry takes transaction costs, contracts, and property rights seriously but argues that economic models often under-theorise power, culture, and social ambiguity. This makes her an important corrective to North/Feder/Deininger style arguments in the thesis.

Use with: [[Transaction Costs]], [[Informal Constraints]], [[New Institutional Economics]].

### Alongside Lund

Berry and [[Christian Lund]] are highly compatible. Lund explains property as a recognised claim sanctioned by authority. Berry explains how the claim remains negotiable through social networks, courts, kinship, custom, and political positioning.

Together, they allow the thesis to say: a land record is both a recognition instrument and a site of continuing negotiation.

### Alongside Scott and Cohn

Cohn explains the colonial knowledge apparatus that creates administrative categories. Scott explains how legibility simplifies complex local worlds. Berry explains why local claims continue to exceed those simplifications even after formal recording.

Use as a triad:
- Cohn: colonial knowledge and survey.
- Scott: state legibility and simplification.
- Berry: negotiated access and social claims beyond the record.

## Key Passages

### Access, Power, and Social Relations

Location: Introduction, p. 3 onward.  
Substance: Berry says understanding resource access is not only a matter of tracing rational responses to prices, property rules, or contracts. It also requires legal and political processes, social relations, authority, obligation, and meanings of exchange.  
Use: Frame Chapter 1's critique of narrow NIE land-record modernisation.

Short anchor: "conditions of access" and "social relations" (Introduction).

### Institutions Are Fluid

Location: Introduction, pp. 6-7.  
Substance: Berry treats African cultures and institutions as fluid, dynamic, and ambiguous. This is the foundation for her refusal to treat customary tenure as either timeless tradition or simple pre-capitalist residue.  
Use: The Odisha parallel is not "African custom equals Indian custom"; it is that land institutions may be internally dynamic even when the state tries to freeze them in records.

### Written Custom Does Not End Contestation

Location: Introduction, pp. 9-10.  
Substance: Berry disputes the view that once traditions were written down, they stopped changing. She argues the colonial period intensified contestation over custom, power, and property.  
Use: Important for Survey and Settlement records: the written record can become a reference point in future contestation, not the end of dispute.

### Registration Does Not End Flexibility

Location: Introduction, p. 12.  
Substance: In central Kenya, property rights and political spheres remained contested and flexible despite land-title registration and administrative boundary stabilisation.  
Use: Direct comparative support for the argument that DILRMP/Bhulekh modernisation cannot be judged only by whether a record exists.

### Keeping Options Open

Location: Introduction, pp. 17-18.  
Substance: Berry argues that when rules and claims are negotiable, people may prefer to keep options open rather than pursue exclusive control.  
Use: Useful for explaining why families may delay partition, avoid mutation, or tolerate multi-claim arrangements.

Short anchor: "keeping options open" (Introduction).

### Multiple Claims as Strategy

Location: Introduction, p. 18.  
Substance: Multiple claims on property are not always signs of institutional failure. They can reflect systematic efforts to maintain influence in ongoing struggles over access.  
Use: Strong for Chapter 5.5 on multi-owner RoRs and possession patterns.

### Property Rights as Social Process

Location: Chapter 5, pp. 101-104.  
Substance: Berry reviews the view that freehold or nationalisation will solve ambiguous land rights, then rejects the assumption that property rules determine resource allocation by themselves.  
Use: This is the main theoretical foundation for critiquing conclusive-title optimism.

### Ambiguous Rights and Participation in Adjudication

Location: Chapter 5, p. 104.  
Substance: The significance of ambiguous land rights is not simply insecurity or inefficiency. Access depends on participation in interpretation and adjudication, as well as ability to pay.  
Use: Highly relevant to mutation, partition, revenue-office visits, local mediation, and court disputes in Odisha.

### Formal Sale or Registration Still Leaves Claims

Location: Chapter 5, p. 104.  
Substance: Berry notes that even land formally registered or sold to an individual may remain subject to multiple claims in practice.  
Use: Directly supports the record-reality gap argument.

### Land Rights and Social Belonging

Location: Chapter 5, pp. 104-107.  
Substance: Access to land historically depended on group membership, allegiance, authority, migration, and settlement. The question "who owns the land?" was rarely simple because land could be claimed through multiple social histories.  
Use: Use with Odisha questions of caste settlement, village belonging, inheritance, and customary possession.

### Strangers, Tenants, and Rent

Location: Chapter 5, pp. 110-112.  
Substance: Migrant farmers' status as "strangers" or "tenants" was not merely economic. Payments could acknowledge jurisdiction and continued authority over land and occupants.  
Use: Conceptual parallel for informal tenants/sharecroppers in Odisha: tenancy may signal more than rent; it may encode subordination, belonging, and recognition.

### Many Courts, Many Verdicts

Location: Chapter 5, pp. 116-120.  
Substance: Multiplicity of legal venues and unclear jurisdiction kept land claims open. Written rulings and court judgments did not necessarily settle disputes permanently.  
Use: Useful for India's fragmented land governance across revenue, registration, civil courts, panchayat mediation, and local intermediaries.

Short anchor: "Many Courts, Many Verdicts" (Chapter 5 section heading).

### Registration and Refragmentation

Location: Chapter 5, pp. 125-129.  
Substance: Kenyan registration produced title deeds, but adjudication recognised social and customary claims; many transactions were not recorded; holdings were refragmented; the register ceased to reflect ground affairs.  
Use: This is the strongest comparative passage for DILRMP. It warns that registration can become stale when social transactions continue outside the record.

### Investing in Networks

Location: Chapter 7, pp. 159-162.  
Substance: Farmers' investments in ceremonies, dependents, education, community projects, and social status shaped their ability to claim resources.  
Use: Strong for interpreting the political economy of land-record correction: claim-making requires social and bureaucratic capital.

### Land as Medium of Social Renegotiation

Location: Chapter 7, pp. 162-164.  
Substance: Cocoa farms and land disputes became arenas for renegotiating kinship, authority, seniority, gender, and community relations.  
Use: Strong for Chapter 5's caste/gender/class reading of land records.

### Networks Are Not Closed Corporations

Location: Chapter 7, pp. 165-166 and 173-176.  
Substance: Social networks remained fluid arenas of mobility and negotiation rather than closed corporate property-holding units.  
Use: Prevents over-romanticising custom or community. Local social relations can sustain access, but they can also reproduce inequality and exclusion.

## Internal Tensions and Silences

### Geographic Transfer Is Limited

Berry is not writing about India or Odisha. The book passes the PhD lens only as a comparative theoretical source. Its concepts should be used to sharpen questions, not to claim African and Odisha land systems are equivalent.

### The Book Is Stronger on Social Process than State Technology

Berry predates DILRMP, digital land records, ULPIN, GIS, Aadhaar linkage, and e-governance. Any application to Bhulekh/Bhunaksha is a thesis extension. The extension is defensible, but should be named as such.

### Ambiguity Can Be Both Protective and Exclusionary

Berry shows that ambiguity can keep options open, but it can also let powerful actors manipulate claims. For Odisha, this matters because informal arrangements may protect tenants or women in some cases, but may also enable dispossession, rent-seeking, and caste domination.

### Formalisation Is Not Refuted Entirely

Berry does not prove that registration is useless. She shows that registration is insufficient when social claims continue outside the record. The thesis should avoid using Berry as a blanket anti-registration source. Use her to argue for social and institutional reconciliation before, during, and after digitisation.

### Gender Is Present but Not the Main Frame

The book includes gendered claims, especially wives' and daughters' contested rights in land and tree crops. But it is not primarily a gender-and-land text. For Odisha gender analysis, pair Berry with India-specific gendered land-rights literature.

## Ideas and Questions Sparked

1. In Odisha fieldwork, separate "record lag" from "social contestation." Is the record wrong because mutation did not happen, or because multiple actors still contest what the correct record should be?

2. Treat multi-owner RoRs as social documents, not only administrative errors. What social arrangement is the joint record preserving or obscuring?

3. Ask whether people avoid mutation or partition because the current ambiguity is useful. Who benefits from keeping claims open?

4. Track "investment in networks" around land records: payments to document writers, repeated visits to RIs/Tehsildars, kin mobilisation, caste mediation, political patronage, and litigation.

5. Use Berry with Lund: RoR is a recognition instrument, but recognition does not end negotiation.

6. Use Berry with Scott: digital legibility may simplify land claims, but the simplified record can remain socially incomplete.

7. Use Berry with Cohn: colonial and postcolonial records may create administrative facts, but those facts continue to be reinterpreted through local social relations.

8. In Chapter 5 interviews, ask respondents not only "who owns this land?" but "who can make a claim on this land, and through whom?"

9. For parcel survey, include a field for "claim types present": recorded owner, possessor, cultivator, tenant, heir, encroacher, absentee owner, customary claimant, welfare claimant.

10. For DILRMP critique, frame the central limitation as follows: modernisation improves the record's technical form but may not resolve the social process through which land access is produced.
