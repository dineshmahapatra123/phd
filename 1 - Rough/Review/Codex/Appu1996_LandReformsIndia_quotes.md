## Axis A: By Thesis Chapter

### Ch. 1.2 (Historical background: colonial institutions)
1. "Lord Cornwallis, in the memorandum in which he defended his policy made clear that he was explicitly conscious that he was creating a new class, and establishing rights which bore no relation to the previous rights of the Zamindars...the purpose of Permanent Settlement was a bulwark against revolution."
   - **Relevance**: Colonial land system was deliberately designed to create landed gentry allied to British power. This institutional structure persisted post-independence.
   - **Page**: xiii-xiv

2. "The Permanent Settlement recognized that the East India Company, in its capacity of sovereign authority, and the Zamindars or farmers through whom the state's share had been collected, had a joint interest in the share of the produce from every acre of land."
   - **Relevance**: Shows how colonial administration fused state extraction with landlord interests, creating symbiotic institutional form.
   - **Page**: 12

---

### Ch. 3.1 (Institutional reform design: tenancy reform policy)
3. "Three important guidelines were laid down in the Five Year Plans. First, rent should not exceed one-fifth to one-fourth of the gross produce; second, the tenants should be accorded permanent rights in the lands they cultivated subject to a limited right of resumption to be granted to the landowners; and third, in respect of non-resumable land the landlord-tenant relationship should be ended by conferring ownership rights on tenants."
   - **Relevance**: Sound policy design. Yet implementation collapsed due to elite capture and political will absence.
   - **Page**: xx

4. "While formulating the policy the over-riding consideration was that the measures adopted for the protection of tenants should not hurt the landowners seriously. Considering the character of the political power structure and the fact that the owners who had let out their lands were numerous and that they had considerable political clout, the adoption of such a pro-landlord policy was inevitable."
   - **Relevance**: Appu explicitly identifies that reform design was compromised to protect elite interests. Policy became self-defeating from inception.
   - **Page**: xx-xxi

---

### Ch. 3.4 (Technocratic critique: flaws in implementation)
5. "The enactment of the ceiling laws was preceded by a national debate attended with a great deal of publicity. Naturally, all alert landowners took steps in good time to distribute the surplus land among their relations, friends and dependents, and in some cases they made paper transactions showing transfers to fictitious trusts."
   - **Relevance**: Demonstrates anticipated evasion mechanism. Elites reorganized holdings BEFORE legislation took effect, rendering law moot.
   - **Page**: xxii

6. "In order to protect the interests of landowners every tenancy law contained a provision for the resumption of a part of the tenanted land by landowners for 'personal cultivation'. In all the laws the term 'personal cultivation' was loosely defined so as to include cultivation of land without putting in one's own labour or even personal supervision."
   - **Relevance**: Deliberate ambiguity in law created loopholes. Not accidental vagueness but intentional design to allow evasion.
   - **Page**: xxi

---

### Ch. 4.2 (Mutation defects: elite capture)
7. "The inevitable consequence was numerous surrenders of tenancies under duress, but on paper shown as 'Voluntary surrenders'. Most of the tenancies in the Raiyatwari areas and under-tenancies in the Zamindari areas were oral and informal crop-sharing arrangements."
   - **Relevance**: Ground reality (duress, oral claims) diverged from official records (voluntary). Records became tools of elite, not livelihood security.
   - **Page**: xxi

8. "Even the states where the law recognized sharecroppers as tenants, they could hardly avail of the benefit as it was extremely difficult to prove the existence of tenancy."
   - **Relevance**: Formal rights proved unexercisable. Poorest class (sharecroppers) could not access legal protection due to evidentiary burden.
   - **Page**: xxi

---

### Ch. 5.5 (Institutional capacity: bureaucratic implementation)
9. "The lack of political will is the single most important cause for the failure of Indian Land Reforms."
   - **Relevance**: Core diagnosis. Formal institutions failed because state lacked commitment to enforce against elites.
   - **Page**: xxiii

10. "As a result of the implementation of the old and the amended ceiling laws only less than 2 per cent of the agricultural land could be redistributed. In Chapter 4 we have drawn attention to the half-hearted approach to the formulation of policy, the built-in loopholes in the laws, the generally poor implementation."
    - **Relevance**: Quantitative failure. Only 2% redistribution despite decades of effort. Demonstrates systemic incapacity or unwillingness.
    - **Page**: xxiii

---

### Ch. 5.7 (Spatial-textual disjuncture: law vs. ground reality)
11. "Tenancy reform has not put an end to the leasing out of land. As long as a large mass of poor peasantry with an insatiable hunger for land and a class of unenterprising landowners co-exist, all attempts to prohibit tenancy are bound to fail."
    - **Relevance**: Law tried to eliminate tenancy (recorded forms) but drove it underground (unrecorded). Formal records and ground reality diverged.
    - **Page**: xxi-xxii

12. "As a result of tenancy reform tenants acquired ownership rights only in about 4 per cent of the operated area in the country. Seven States account for 97 per cent of the total number of tenants who became owners. Everywhere tenancy reform led to the largescale ejectment of tenants."
    - **Relevance**: Spatial-textual disjuncture: records showed tenants acquiring land; ground reality showed mass ejectment and pauperization.
    - **Page**: xxi

## Axis B: By Active Debate

### Debate 2: Formal Institutions Are Insufficient
13. "The main consequence of the new policy was to push tenancies underground...all attempts to prohibit tenancy are bound to fail...If tenancies are legally permitted, tenants will enjoy better rights in the lands that they now cultivate illegally."
    - **Relevance**: Formal prohibition failed; it only pushed tenancy off the record, worsening conditions for poor. Shows formal law requires complementary political institutions to be effective.
    - **Page**: xxi

14. "No effective step was taken to regulate rent and protect the rights of the tenants. As Baden Powell himself concedes, the measures contemplated by Government for the protection of tenants 'were theoretically inadequate and in the course of actual events they proved absolutely futile.'"
    - **Relevance**: Even theoretically sound measures failed due to lack of enforcement will. Shows formal law + political resistance = nullification.
    - **Page**: 16

---

### Debate 4: Colonial-to-Postcolonial Continuity
15. "Efforts at tenancy reform starting with the Bengal Tenancy Act of 1885, the other measures of land reform attempted since independence and the various programmes for rural development designed during the last 45 years have not yet completely neutralized the deadly impact of Permanent Settlement on rural society."
    - **Relevance**: Shows path dependence. Colonial institutional damage so deep that decades of reform couldn't reverse it. Institutions lock in extraction logics.
    - **Page**: 17

16. "In a vast sub-continent of such great diversity as India, exposed as she had been to different strands of political, economic, social and cultural influences in her long and chequered history, it was, indeed, natural to come across a bewildering variety of tenurial conditions."
    - **Relevance**: Colonial rule created heterogeneous land systems across regions; postcolonial reform had to navigate this inherited complexity.
    - **Page**: xiii

---

### Debate 6: Elite Capture of Implementation
17. "The result was the vast official documentation project that included the Survey of India, under the direction of George Lambton, which eventually covered India with an imaginary grid on which the government could locate any site in India."
    - **Relevance**: Colonial records created state legibility but in service of extraction. Postcolonial bureaucrats inherit same infrastructure and same biases.
    - **Page**: (Appu, referencing colonial records)

18. "The Zamindars won all along the line. The rights of underproprietors were recognized but the rights of occupancy and nonproprietary cultivators were ignored."
    - **Relevance**: Elite capture of reform outcomes. Despite reformist intent, institutional power favoured landlords.
    - **Page**: 24

---

### Debate 7: NIE vs. Social Relations
19. "All through recorded history the Indian agrarian structure was inegalitarian and a substantial share of the income from land accrued to the state and the big landowners...the land policies adopted by the British, particularly the elevation of the intermediaries to the status of landlords and the extinguishment of the rights of millions of cultivators rendered the agrarian structure even more inegalitarian."
    - **Relevance**: Institutions reflect and reproduce power relations; they don't determine outcomes independently. NIE must account for power dynamics.
    - **Page**: 40

20. "The separation between proprietorship and physical cultivation both draws sanction from and serves to reinforce the caste structure of rural society."
    - **Relevance**: Land institutions and social hierarchy are mutually reinforcing. Formal property rights alone cannot undo caste domination.
    - **Page**: 44

---

### Debate 8: Institutional Mutation
21. "When all efforts to mend tenancy failed, the Planning Commission advised that tenancies should be ended. The new policy was that leasing out of land should be prohibited and all tenants should be made owners of the land they had been cultivating, on payment of compensation to the landowners."
    - **Relevance**: Institutional design mutated when original reform failed. New design also failed. Shows mutation is predictable response to evasion.
    - **Page**: xxi

22. "Despite its inadequacies and defects the administrative structure that the British had built up for the assessment and collection of land revenue proved to be a very useful instrument after independence."
    - **Relevance**: Postcolonial state inherited colonial administrative form. Mutation occurred not through new design but through redeployment of old forms for old purposes (extraction).
    - **Page**: 33

---

### Debate 9: International Model-Dependence
23. "In Five Year Plans the objectives of the land policy were laid down as, firstly, to remove such motivational and other impediments to increase in agricultural production as arise from the agrarian structure inherited from the past."
    - **Relevance**: India adopted internationally-circulated development logic (institutional reform → incentives → productivity). But context mattered: elite power defeated reform.
    - **Page**: xix

## Axis C: By Core Concept

### Concept: Anticipated Evasion & Strategic Reorganisation
24. "A committee of circuit was constituted to tour the provinces and make settlements by auction to the highest bidders for a period of five years. The new system had disastrous consequences for the old Zamindars as well as the raiyats."
    - **Relevance**: When colonial policies threatened existing landlords, they either bid up rents (causing exhaustion) or evaded systemically. Predictable response to perceived threat.
    - **Page**: 10

---

### Concept: Definitional Ambiguity as Evasion Mechanism
25. "The pargana rates were by no means uniform. The same village often had several rates. And even in the same village there were different rates for the same type of land depending on who the tenant was. The pargana rates being vague and variable it was easy for the Zamindar to pitch the rent so high that no tenant would come forward to execute a contract and accept the patta."
    - **Relevance**: Vagueness in land categories and rates enabled elite manipulation. Shows how records become instruments of power rather than security when definitions are ambiguous.
    - **Page**: 15

---

### Concept: Built-in Depressor of Agricultural Development
26. "Through the operation of this multifaceted 'depressor', Indian agriculture continued to be characterized by low capital intensity and antiquated methods. Few of the actual tillers were left with an efficacious interest in modernization...The pattern of landholding, cultivation, and product-sharing operated to hold down agricultural production."
    - **Relevance**: Institutional structure (unequal land distribution, insecure tenure, elite extraction) disincentivizes poor cultivators from investing. Formal property rights won't overcome this without redistribution.
    - **Page**: 45
