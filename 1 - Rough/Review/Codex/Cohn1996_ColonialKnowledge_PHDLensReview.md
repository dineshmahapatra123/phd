# Cohn 1996 - Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge - PHD_LENS Review

## Core Argument

Cohn's book should be used in the thesis less as a general postcolonial text and more as a genealogy of the colonial state as a knowledge-making state. Its strongest contribution is the claim that British rule in India depended on making Indian society administratively knowable through officialising procedures: documentation, classification, translation, survey, settlement, census, legal codification, and museum-like objectification.

For the Odisha land-records thesis, the load-bearing point is precise: the colonial state did not merely record an already settled land order. It created official facts about land, revenue, rights, categories, and authority through procedures designed for rule. This is why Cohn matters for the record-ground gap. Land records emerge as part of a larger documentation project whose purpose was revenue extraction, administrative control, and legal ordering, not conclusive recognition of socially lived ownership.

The broad "knowledge/power" argument passes the lens only when tied to land administration, legal codification, revenue settlement, survey, cadastral mapping, or bureaucratic classification. Used loosely, it becomes metaphor. Used institutionally, it explains why a revenue record could later be mistaken for, or pressed into service as, a title-like document.

## Conceptual Architecture

### Investigative Modalities

Cohn's core concept is the "investigative modality": a state-linked procedure for defining what information is needed, collecting it, ordering it, and transforming it into usable official forms such as reports, statistical returns, gazetteers, legal codes, and encyclopedias. For this thesis, the important modalities are not all equal.

- **Survey modality**: strongest fit. It connects directly to cadastral mapping, boundary-making, settlement, Bhunaksha, resurvey, ULPIN, and the parcel as an administrable unit.
- **Historiographic modality**: strong fit where it concerns settlement reports, local histories, customs, and land tenure.
- **Legal-codificatory modality**: strong fit through Hindu law, property, inheritance, contracts, and the transformation of flexible legal reasoning into court-administered doctrine.
- **Enumerative modality**: useful by analogy only when tied to RoR categories, welfare eligibility, caste/class categorisation, and the administrative production of target populations.
- **Museological and cloth chapters**: mostly peripheral for the thesis. They show Cohn's wider argument but should not be overused unless the chapter is making a methodological point about objectification.

### Documentation Project

The documentation project is the institutional umbrella under which settlement reports, surveys, statistical tables, and legal codes become instruments of state capacity. This is the most important bridge to DILRMP: DILRMP can be read as a postcolonial and digital intensification of a longer documentation project, not simply as a break from colonial practice.

### Objectification

Cohn's objectification is the process by which lived, contested, and locally mediated realities become state-readable objects: "landlord", "tenant", "proprietor", "Hindu law", "custom", "village", "boundary", "record". For the thesis, this should be connected to how RoRs objectify ownership/possession relations and how Bhulekh/Bhunaksha objectify textual and spatial realities at different speeds.

### Language and Translation

The language chapters are relevant when they show that translation is administrative conversion, not neutral communication. The British sought language knowledge to issue commands, collect taxes, maintain law and order, and classify society. This helps explain why revenue terminology and legal categories are never innocent: they are instruments of rule.

## Empirical Terrain or Evidence and Examples Used

Cohn's evidence is historical and archival. For this thesis, the highest-value empirical terrain is:

- Bengal after 1765, where the Company acquired the Dewani and had to assess and collect revenue.
- Hastings's 1772 administrative reorganisation, especially the collector, district, Dewani court, and the attempt to govern through Indian law.
- The Permanent Settlement background: the search for an owner/proprietor who could be made responsible for revenue.
- Settlement reports as district-level products of enquiries into revenue, custom, local history, and land tenure.
- Survey as a modality that included mapping, bounding, measuring, and classifying natural and social landscapes.
- Legal codification through Halhed, Jones, and Colebrooke, especially the attempt to turn Hindu and Muslim legal traditions into fixed, public, court-usable bodies of law.
- The role and displacement of Indian intermediaries: conogos, pandits, maulavis, munshis, dewans, scribes, local officials, and revenue specialists.

For Odisha, Cohn is not a direct empirical source. His Bengal material is nevertheless institutionally relevant because coastal and northern Odisha passed through Bengal Presidency structures, and because later Odisha settlement and tenancy categories were built within the wider colonial legal-revenue architecture.

## Theoretical Lineage

Cohn sits at the junction of historical anthropology, colonial state formation, and knowledge-power analysis.

- **Foucault**: Cohn gives Indian colonial administrative substance to the claim that knowledge and power are co-produced.
- **Said / Orientalism**: Cohn is less literary and more bureaucratic than Said; this makes him more useful for land-record analysis.
- **Subaltern Studies / Guha**: Cohn shares the concern that colonial categories reshape what can be known about Indian society.
- **Weberian rationalisation**: the colonial state converts plural, flexible, and locally interpreted arrangements into formal rules, files, codes, and offices.
- **Historical institutionalism**: not Cohn's explicit vocabulary, but the thesis can use path dependence to connect colonial investigative modalities to postcolonial land-record persistence.

## Key Passages

### State Documentation and Capacity

> "The documentation that was involved created and normalized a vast amount of information that formed the basis of their capacity to govern." (p. 3)

This is the main thesis bridge. Land records should be treated as a capacity-producing documentary form, not as neutral mirrors of ownership.

### Administrative Facts

> "There was widespread agreement that this society, like others they were governing, could be known and represented as a series of facts." (p. 4)

This is useful for Ch. 2 and Ch. 3 because it exposes the assumption behind settlement and digitisation: that social relations can be converted into administratively usable facts.

### Epistemological Conquest

> "In coming to India, they unknowingly and unwittingly invaded and conquered not only a territory but an epistemological space as well." (p. 4)

Use this carefully. It is powerful, but it must be tied to land, revenue, legal, and survey procedures to pass the PHD_LENS.

### Language, Tax, and Classification

> "The knowledge of languages was necessary to issue commands, collect taxes, maintain law and order- and to create other forms of knowledge about the people they were ruling." (p. 5)

This quote links language to revenue and classification. It supports the argument that colonial knowledge was operational.

### Definition of Investigative Modality

> "An investigative modality includes the definition of a body of information that is needed, the procedures by which appropriate knowledge is gathered, its ordering and classification..." (p. 5)

Use this as the formal definition before applying Cohn to land records.

### Land-Settlement Process

> "Out of this grew the most extensive and continuous administrative activity of the British, which they termed the land-settlement process." (p. 5)

This is the most directly usable Cohn sentence for the thesis. It names land settlement as the central, continuous administrative activity.

### Settlement Reports

> "Entailed in this enterprise was the collection of 'customs and local histories,' which in the British discourse related to land tenure." (p. 5)

This helps frame settlement reports as knowledge-making documents, not just administrative outputs.

### Survey Modality

> "The survey as an investigative modality encompasses a wide range of practices, from the mapping of India... to the most minute measuring of a peasant's fields." (p. 7)

This connects Cohn directly to cadastral survey and parcel-level measurement.

### Grid and Settlement

> "The result was the vast official documentation project... an imaginary grid on which the government could locate any site in India." (p. 7)

This is relevant to Bhunaksha, GIS, ULPIN, and the spatial imagination of land modernisation.

### Fixing, Bounding, and Settling

> "...knowledge were transformed into textual forms such as encyclopedias and extensive archives that were deployed by the colonial state in fixing, bounding, and settling India." (p. 8)

This phrase is ideal for the thesis's argument about records and maps as technologies of fixation.

### Census and Administrative Categories

> "It is my hypothesis that what was entailed in the construction of the census operations was the creation of social categories by which India was ordered for administrative purposes." (p. 8)

Use this for RoR/welfare only with care: it is not about land records, but it gives a model for how administrative categories become social facts.

### The Company's Revenue Problem

> "If the British could not master the details of the revenue system, they would be dependent on those 'subtle natives,' who could 'perplex' them at every turn." (p. 59)

This is important for the thesis's fieldwork design because revenue administration depends on intermediaries and local knowledge.

### Property and Revenue

> "The courts established and protected property rights and were the instrument for enforcing payment of the 'king's share of the revenue'." (p. 59)

This supports the claim that colonial property rights and revenue extraction were institutionally linked.

### Search for Ownership

> "The British in India initially tried to find who 'owned' the land, so that person could be made responsible for payment of revenue." (p. 59)

This is central to the colonial revenue record -> postcolonial title document debate. Ownership identification was revenue-driven.

### Forms of Knowledge Had to Be Acquired or Created

> "Forms of knowledge that would enable the foreign rulers to frame regulations... had to be acquired or created." (p. 59)

This is a crucial formulation: the colonial state acquired some knowledge and created other knowledge. The thesis should not assume records simply discovered ownership.

### Collector and District

> "The collector would have mixed executive and judicial powers in a defined area, a 'district'..." (p. 60)

This is useful for Ch. 4's revenue hierarchy and the genealogy of the Tehsildar/RI/Amin administrative world.

### Administrative Efficiency as Revenue Collection

> "The best indicator of efficiency of the administration was its capacity to collect 100 percent of the assessed revenues." (p. 61)

This is valuable for contrasting colonial revenue efficiency with contemporary DILRMP efficiency metrics.

### Rent, Landlord, Tenant

> "If payment were made... by an agriculturalist to a superior... these payments were 'rent,' the receiver was a 'landlord,' and the payer was a 'tenant'." (pp. 61-62)

This is one of the strongest passages for the thesis. It shows category-making through British administrative logic.

### Hastings and Property Disputes

> "...disputes 'concerning property, whether real or personal, all cases of inheritance, marriage and caste... disputed accounts, contracts, partnerships and demands of rent.'" (p. 66)

This shows the institutional range of the Dewani courts and why property, inheritance, rent, and legal categories became bundled.

### Codifying Indian Law

> "A fixed body of knowledge that could be objectified into Hindu and Muslim law had to be found." (p. 69)

This is directly applicable to property and inheritance disputes: colonial courts needed fixed law, even where lived legal practice was interpretive and plural.

### Property as the Legal Core

> "The object was to find and fix a Hindu civil law... concerned with... the ownership and transmission of property." (p. 71)

This quote is highly relevant to inheritance, partition, mutation, and land ownership history.

### English Case Law as Hindu Law

> "...the chain of interpretations of precedents by the English judges... became enshrined as Hindu law..." (pp. 74-75)

This is useful for the argument that legal categories were not inherited unchanged; they were remade through colonial courts.

### English Law as the Outcome

> "What had started with Warren Hastings and Sir William Jones as a search for the 'ancient Indian constitution' ended up... with English law as the law of India." (p. 75)

This is a capstone quote for Ch. 2.4 and Ch. 4.1.

## Internal Tensions and Silences

### 1. Cohn's Land Evidence Is Foundational but Not Sufficient

Cohn names land settlement as the most extensive and continuous British administrative activity, but the book does not provide a full institutional history of RoRs, cadastral maps, mutation, registration, or Odisha-specific settlement law. The thesis must pair Cohn with land-administration historians and Odisha legal materials.

### 2. Epistemology Can Become Too General

Cohn's vocabulary can tempt overextension. "Knowledge/power" should not be used as a floating theoretical phrase. In this thesis it must be attached to specific documentary instruments: settlement report, RoR, cadastral map, register, court precedent, revenue demand, mutation order, Bhulekh entry, Bhunaksha parcel.

### 3. Indian Intermediaries Are Present but Under-Theorised

Cohn repeatedly shows dependence on Indian experts but does not fully theorise their incentives, agency, class location, caste position, or strategies. This is a gap the thesis can fill empirically through RI, Amin, Tehsildar, deed writer, broker, and village-level interviews.

### 4. Postcolonial Continuity Is Asserted More Than Demonstrated

Cohn gives the colonial genealogy, not the postcolonial mechanism. The thesis should supply that mechanism through historical institutionalism: path dependence, sunk administrative routines, legal inertia, fiscal incentives, staff training, software architecture, and citizen dependence on documents.

### 5. Odisha Is Absent

Cohn's Bengal and broader India material must be routed through Odisha's specific historical paths: Bengal Presidency/coastal Odisha, Madras Presidency/Ganjam and Koraput, Central Provinces/Sambalpur belt, and gadjat/princely-state trajectories.

## Thesis Relevance - PHD_LENS Filter

### Test 1: Research Question Link

**RQ1 - DILRMP progress, motivation, design, implementation: Pass, indirect but strong.** Cohn explains the older documentary apparatus that DILRMP modernises. DILRMP's design inherits a world in which land is already parcelled, named, classified, mapped, and recorded through colonial and postcolonial revenue forms.

**RQ2 - Objectives/design versus outcomes and use/misuse of RoRs: Pass, moderate.** Cohn's census and legal-codification arguments help explain how administrative categories travel beyond their original purpose. This supports the RoR-as-welfare-gate argument, but the contemporary evidence must come from Odisha and DILRMP sources.

**RQ3 - History of land ownership and tenancy: Pass, strong.** Cohn's discussion of rent, landlord, tenant, property, inheritance, and land settlement shows how colonial categories shaped the recorded history of ownership and tenancy.

**RQ4 - Source of disjuncture between records and ground reality: Pass, very strong.** Cohn's strongest use is here. The British needed administratively usable knowledge to assess revenue and govern; this generated records that fixed certain relations while simplifying or excluding local practices.

**RQ5 - Technocratic modernisation versus political economy: Pass, strong.** Cohn's modalities show that apparently technical procedures are political and epistemological. Digital updates can reproduce the limits of the inherited record if they do not resolve underlying rights, possession, inheritance, and boundary disputes.

**RQ6 - Alternative methods to Survey and Settlement: Pass, moderate.** Cohn provides the genealogy of survey and settlement as state modalities. His work helps assess whether integration, Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, ULPIN, and digital resurvey are new institutional forms or renewed versions of older documentation practices.

### Test 2: Geographic and Institutional Grounding

Pass. The book is about British India. Bengal is central, and Bengal is institutionally relevant to Odisha's colonial land administration. The argument also extends to Madras and Mysore through survey and objectification, which matters for the Ganjam/Koraput side of Odisha's mixed administrative inheritance.

### Active Debates Engaged

**Debate 4 - Colonial revenue record -> postcolonial title document: central.** Cohn shows that ownership identification was undertaken to secure revenue liability. This is the most important debate connection.

**Debate 3 - Technocratic modernisation versus political economy: central.** Survey, settlement, legal codification, and census look technical but are embedded in state power.

**Debate 5 - Spatial-textual disjuncture: strong.** Cohn's survey modality helps frame why textual and spatial records may diverge from lived reality, though he does not discuss Bhulekh/Bhunaksha.

**Debate 8 - Ownership versus possession: strong.** Cohn helps explain why official categories may not map cleanly onto possession, customary use, tenancy, or informal partitions.

**Debate 6 - RoR as welfare inclusion/exclusion: moderate.** Use Cohn's enumerative modality as the analogy: administrative categories created for one purpose can become gates for another.

**Debate 7 - NIE efficiency versus agrarian social relations: moderate to strong.** Cohn helps criticise overly abstract property categories by showing how colonial legal-economic categories converted social relations into rent, landlord, tenant, and owner.

## Ideas and Questions Sparked

1. **DILRMP as a digital investigative modality**: Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, ULPIN, e-mutation, and integration workflows should be analysed as a new modality that defines what land information is needed, how it is gathered, and how it becomes official.

2. **RoR as a category-making instrument**: Like census categories, RoR entries create administratively actionable identities: owner, recorded tenant, co-sharer, encroacher, beneficiary, non-beneficiary.

3. **Collector to Tehsildar/RI/Amin genealogy**: Cohn's collector is the ancestor of today's local revenue bureaucracy. Ch. 4.2 can trace how mixed executive/judicial/revenue power persists in altered forms.

4. **Information asymmetry as historical continuity**: The Company's dependence on Indian revenue specialists resembles the contemporary state's dependence on RIs, Amins, deed writers, local brokers, and village-level knowledge holders.

5. **Possession as what records cannot easily see**: The source of the record-ground gap may lie in the old documentary assumption that land can be fixed through measurable, textual, and revenue categories, while possession is relational, negotiated, seasonal, inherited, and locally remembered.

6. **Odisha archival task**: Use Cohn as the conceptual frame, then test it in Odisha through settlement reports, Orissa Tenancy Act materials, Survey and Settlement manuals, estate abolition files, and village cadastral histories.

7. **Path dependence mechanism**: Cohn gives origin. The thesis should add persistence: legal reliance, bureaucratic training, citizen dependence, software migration from old records, welfare linkages, and the fiscal-administrative convenience of not reopening settled rights.
