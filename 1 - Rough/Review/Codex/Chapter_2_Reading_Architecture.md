# Chapter 2 Reading Architecture

**Status:** Final architecture, cross-checked June 2026  
**Purpose:** Reading, note-making and drafting plan, not a bibliography to cite exhaustively

## History and Evolution of Land Records: Global and Indian Contexts

This bibliography is designed for the thesis proposed in `Proposal_Dinesh_Mahapatra.pdf`
and follows the structure in `Chapters_Sections.pdf`. It is not a neutral catalogue. It is a
reading architecture for explaining how land was made legible to states, how fiscal records
became evidence of rights, and how historically layered records became the infrastructure
upon which contemporary modernisation operates.

The chapter should be historical and institutional, but theoretically informed. It should not
become a second literature-review chapter or anticipate the full political-economy argument
of Chapter 3. Its distinctive task is to reconstruct the institutional genealogy of the record:
what was recorded, by whom, for what purpose, under which conception of rights, and with
what consequences for claims that did not fit the record.

## The Argument Chapter 2 Should Build

Chapter 2 should resist a teleological account in which informal or customary tenure is
gradually replaced by an inherently superior system of individual, surveyed and registered
titles. Its central historical argument can instead be:

1. Land records are political and administrative artefacts, not passive mirrors of pre-existing
   rights.
2. Their functions have changed over time: taxation, territorial control, adjudication,
   transfer, credit, planning, welfare targeting and market facilitation.
3. Colonial rule did not merely improve existing records. It translated plural and layered
   interests into administratively manageable categories, often producing new proprietors,
   villages, boundaries and hierarchies.
4. Independent India inherited a fragmented fiscal, cadastral and registration infrastructure.
   It redirected that infrastructure toward land reform and development without eliminating
   its institutional fragmentation or presumptive character.
5. DILRMP therefore operates upon historically produced records and institutions. It cannot
   be understood simply as the digitisation of accurate analogue information.
6. The record-reality gap is not merely an administrative failure. It is generated by the
   difference between socially embedded, overlapping and changing claims to land and the
   state's need to represent those claims in stable, parcel-based and legally actionable forms.

The detailed analysis of neoliberalism, international institutions, market-oriented reforms
and the political economy of conclusive titling should remain in Chapter 3. Chapter 2 should
establish their historical and institutional preconditions.

## Three Analytical Lenses

Use these lenses throughout without creating three disconnected theoretical essays:

1. **Institutional/NIE lens:** records reduce information and transaction costs, support
   exchange and enforcement, and shape incentives. Read North, Feder and Deininger as
   arguments whose assumptions require historical testing.
2. **Agrarian political-economy lens:** records formalise particular relations of property,
   class, caste and gender; they may facilitate commodification while leaving possession,
   tenancy and labour relations unrecorded.
3. **State legibility and legal-pluralism lens:** records simplify complex claims, but state
   recognition is neither complete nor unilateral. People also seek, negotiate and manipulate
   recognition through records.

The chapter's contribution comes from holding these lenses together rather than selecting a
single total explanation.

## Priority Key

- **A - Essential:** read closely and use in the chapter's main argument.
- **B - Important:** read selectively for a subsection, comparison or counterargument.
- **C - Reference:** consult for definitions, factual verification or historiographical depth.
- **Primary:** legislation, official report, manual, committee report or administrative text.

## 2.1 Land Records and Their Functions

### Essential readings

1. **A** ==Dale, Peter F., and John D. McLaughlin. 1999. *Land Administration*.==
   ==Oxford University Press==.
   - The standard conceptual account of land administration, including juridical, fiscal,
     regulatory and information functions.

2. **A** Larsson, Gerhard. 1991. *Land Registration and Cadastral Systems: Tools for
   Land Information and Management*. Longman.
   - Useful for distinguishing cadastre, registration, cadastral survey and land information
     systems, and for comparing European and British-derived systems.

3. **A** Zevenbergen, Jaap. 2002. *Systems of Land Registration: Aspects and Effects*.
   Netherlands Geodetic Commission.
   - Particularly valuable for treating parcels, boundaries and rights as legally constructed
     rather than naturally given objects.
   - Open text: https://repository.tudelft.nl/islandora/object/uuid:44e404e9-c1e9-4c20-b1e1-977ee9c11570

4. **A** Williamson, Ian, Stig Enemark, Jude Wallace, and Abbas Rajabifard. 2010.
   *Land Administration for Sustainable Development*. ESRI Press.
   - Defines the modern land-administration paradigm and its land-tenure, value, use and
     development functions.

5. **A, critical lens** Scott, James C. 1998. *Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes
   to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed*. Yale University Press.
   - Use the concepts of legibility, simplification and administrative ordering. Do not use
   Scott as a substitute for specific institutional history.

6. **A, social-relations lens** Lund, Christian. 2016. "Rule and Rupture: State Formation
   through the Production of Property and Citizenship." *Development and Change*
   47 (6): 1199-1228. DOI: 10.1111/dech.12274.
   - Recognition of property simultaneously authorises institutions and constitutes political
     subjects. This helps move beyond a one-directional "state sees society" account.

7. **A, access lens** Ribot, Jesse C., and Nancy Lee Peluso. 2003. "A Theory of Access."
   *Rural Sociology* 68 (2): 153-181.
   - Essential distinction between a right to benefit and the actual ability to benefit. This is
     foundational for later analysis of ownership versus possession, tenancy and welfare.

7. ==**A, negotiated claims** Berry, Sara. 1993. *No Condition Is Permanent: The Social==
   ==Dynamics of Agrarian Change in Sub-Saharan Africa*.== University of Wisconsin Press.
   - Shows how claims remain historically contingent and socially negotiated even where
     formal institutions exist. Use comparatively, not as a direct account of India.

9. **A, bundles and commons** Schlager, Edella, and Elinor Ostrom. 1992. "Property-
   Rights Regimes and Natural Resources: A Conceptual Analysis." *Land Economics*
   68 (3): 249-262.
   - Disaggregates ownership into access, withdrawal, management, exclusion and alienation.

10. **B** Federation Internationale des Geometres (FIG). 1995. *FIG Statement on the
   Cadastre*.
   - Concise authoritative definition of a parcel-based cadastre and rights, restrictions and
     responsibilities.
   - https://www.fig.net/resources/publications/figpub/pub11/FIG%20Statement%20on%20the%20Cadastre.pdf

11. **B** United Nations Economic Commission for Europe. 1996. *Land Administration
   Guidelines: With Special Reference to Countries in Transition*. United Nations.
   - Useful for the institutional functions of title, cadastre, valuation and registration.

12. **B** Food and Agriculture Organization. 2002. *Land Tenure and Rural Development*,
   FAO Land Tenure Studies 3.
   - Provides careful terminology for tenure, access, rights and administration.

13. **B, legal pluralism** von Benda-Beckmann, Franz, Keebet von Benda-Beckmann, and
   Melanie G. Wiber, eds. 2006. *Changing Properties of Property*. Berghahn Books.
   - Use selectively to avoid treating state law as the only normative order governing land.

14. **B, NIE bridge** North, Douglass C. 1990. *Institutions, Institutional Change and
   Economic Performance*. Cambridge University Press.
   - Use path dependence and institutional persistence to frame why inherited record systems
     survive changes in formal policy. The full NIE exposition belongs in Chapter 1.

15. **B, agrarian bridge** Polanyi, Karl. 1944. *The Great Transformation*. Farrar &
   Rinehart; later editions by Beacon Press.
   - Read the discussion of land as a fictitious commodity to distinguish land's social and
     ecological meanings from its administrative treatment as a transferable asset.

16. **B, property-rights proposition** Demsetz, Harold. 1967. "Toward a Theory of
   Property Rights." *American Economic Review* 57 (2): 347-359.
   - Introduce the efficiency/evolution proposition only briefly and test it against Platteau,
     Berry and the Indian historical evidence.

### Concepts to extract

Distinguish: land tenure; property rights; possession; ownership; title; deed; registration;
mutation; record of rights; fiscal cadastre; juridical cadastre; multipurpose cadastre;
cadastral map; survey and settlement; textual versus spatial record; presumptive versus
conclusive title.

### Drafting move

Open with a functional definition of land records, then unsettle it. Present fiscal, juridical,
economic, planning and welfare functions, but show that every function requires selecting
which people, claims, boundaries and transactions become recordable. End by distinguishing
the legal record from actual access and possession. This prepares the historical sections.

## 2.2 Evolution of Land Records and Titling Systems

### 2.2.1 Pre-colonial land records and practices

The purpose is not to produce a universal chronology. Use a few comparisons to show that
states recorded land for different combinations of tribute, taxation, military mobilisation,
irrigation, adjudication and social ordering.

1. **A** Kain, Roger J. P., and Elizabeth Baigent. 1992. *The Cadastral Map in the
   Service of the State: A History of Property Mapping*. University of Chicago Press.
   - The strongest comparative historical starting point for mapping, taxation and state
     formation.

2. **B** Ting, Lisa, and Ian Williamson. 1999. "Cadastral Trends: A Synthesis."
   *Australian Surveyor* 44. DOI: 10.1080/00050326.1999.10441903.
   - An evolutionary synthesis, useful if read alongside Tan's critique below.

3. **B, counterpoint** Tan, Willie. 1999. "The Development of Cadastral Systems: An
   Alternative View." *Australian Surveyor* 44.
   DOI: 10.1080/00050326.1999.10441922.
   - Rejects overly global or functionalist explanations and emphasizes national conflict,
   stakeholder interests and state action.

4. **B** Larsson, Gerhard. 1991. *Land Registration and Cadastral Systems*.
   - Read the historical chapters for the divergence between fiscal cadastres, juridical
     cadastres, deeds registration and title registration.

**Scope control:** This subsection should be brief and comparative. Avoid a civilisational
survey from Mesopotamia to the present. Select two or three examples demonstrating that
recording did not historically imply modern private ownership or conclusive title.

### 2.2.2 Colonial approaches to land records

1. **A** Home, Robert. 2006. "Scientific Survey and Land Settlement in British
   Colonialism, with Particular Reference to Land Tenure Reform in the Middle East,
   1920-50." *Planning Perspectives* 21.
   DOI: 10.1080/02665430500397048.
   - Connects cadastral science, colonial networks and land settlement across the British
     Empire.

2. **A** Edney, Matthew H. 1997. *Mapping an Empire: The Geographical Construction
   of British India, 1765-1843*. University of Chicago Press.
   - Essential for survey as a practice of colonial knowledge and territorial authority.

3. **A** Cohn, Bernard S. 1996. *Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge: The British

   - Read "The Survey" and related essays for enumeration, classification and colonial
     knowledge.

4. **B** Mitchell, Timothy. 2002. *Rule of Experts: Egypt, Techno-Politics, Modernity*.
   University of California Press.
   - Comparative account of expertise, property, mapping and colonial techno-politics.

5. **B** Divithure, H., and C. Tang. 2013. "Evolution of Land Registration and
   Cadastral Survey Systems in Sri Lanka." *Survey Review*.
   DOI: 10.1179/1752270612Y.0000000011.
   - A useful South Asian comparison of pre-colonial and successive Portuguese, Dutch and
     British systems.

6. **B, property and colonialism** Bhandar, Brenna. 2018. *Colonial Lives of Property:
   Law, Land, and Racial Regimes of Ownership*. Duke University Press.
   - Provides the strongest conceptual account of how modern property forms and racialised
     colonial rule developed together.

7. **B, imperial comparison** Home, Robert. 1997. *Of Planting and Planning: The
   Making of British Colonial Cities*. E & FN Spon.
   - Consult selectively where cadastral planning, settlement and colonial transplantation of
     property institutions intersect.

### 2.2.3 Post-colonial developments

1. **A** Platteau, Jean-Philippe. 1996. "The Evolutionary Theory of Land Rights as
   Applied to Sub-Saharan Africa: A Critical Assessment." *Development and Change*
   27 (1): 29-86.
   - Fundamental critique of the proposition that scarcity naturally generates increasingly
     individualised and formal rights.

2. **A** Feder, Gershon, and Akihiko Nishio. 1998. "The Benefits of Land Registration
   and Titling: Economic and Social Perspectives." *Land Use Policy* 15 (1): 25-43.
   - Canonical statement of the tenure-security, transfer and credit arguments. Treat as an
     argument to assess, not as settled fact.

3. **A, counterpoint** Bromley, Daniel W. 2009. "Formalising Property Relations in the
   Developing World: The Wrong Prescription for the Wrong Malady." *Land Use Policy*
   26 (1): 20-27. DOI: 10.1016/j.landusepol.2008.02.003.
   - Shows why legal title cannot repair the political and economic conditions that make
     claims insecure.

4. **A, counterpoint** Sjaastad, Espen, and Ben Cousins. 2009. "Formalisation of Land
   Rights in the South: An Overview." *Land Use Policy* 26 (1): 1-9.
   - A concise critical review of theory and evidence.

5. **B** Deininger, Klaus. 2003. *Land Policies for Growth and Poverty Reduction*.
   World Bank and Oxford University Press.
   - Authoritative policy synthesis. Its assumptions and policy orientation should be
     critically examined in Chapter 3.

6. **B** Toulmin, Camilla. 2009. "Securing Land and Property Rights in Sub-Saharan
   Africa: The Role of Local Institutions." *Land Use Policy* 26 (1): 10-19.
   - Counters one-size-fits-all titling through attention to local institutions.

7. **A** Sjaastad, Espen, and Daniel W. Bromley. 1997. "Indigenous Land Rights in
   Sub-Saharan Africa: Appropriation, Security and Investment Demand." *World
   Development* 25 (4): 549-562.
   - Questions the equation of private title with tenure security and investment incentives.

8. **B** Bruce, John W., and Shem E. Migot-Adholla, eds. 1994. *Searching for Land
   Tenure Security in Africa*. Kendall/Hunt for the World Bank.
   - Important for reconstructing the empirical debate that preceded later formalisation
     critiques.

### 2.2.4 Contemporary and neoliberal approaches: brief introduction

1. **A, position to be examined** de Soto, Hernando. 2000. *The Mystery of Capital:
   Why Capitalism Triumphs in the West and Fails Everywhere Else*. Basic Books.

2. **A, empirical critique** Payne, Geoffrey, Alain Durand-Lasserve, and Carole Rakodi.
   2009. "The Limits of Land Titling and Home Ownership." *Environment and
   Urbanization* 21 (2): 443-462.

3. **B** Enemark, Stig, Keith Clifford Bell, Christiaan Lemmen, and Robin McLaren.
   2014. *Fit-for-Purpose Land Administration*. FIG and World Bank.
   - Represents the shift from expensive comprehensive titling toward flexible,
     coverage-first approaches.

4. **B** Deininger, Klaus, Harris Selod, and Anthony Burns. 2012. *The Land Governance
   Assessment Framework*. World Bank.

5. **A, internal synthesis** Deininger, Klaus, and Gershon Feder. 2009. "Land
   Registration, Governance, and Development: Evidence and Implications for Policy."
   *World Bank Research Observer* 24 (2): 233-266.
   - A more qualified mainstream synthesis than simple de Soto-style formalisation.

6. **B, agrarian critique** Borras, Saturnino M., Jr., and Jennifer C. Franco. 2010.
   "Contemporary Discourses and Contestations around Pro-Poor Land Policies and Land
   Governance." *Journal of Agrarian Change* 10 (1): 1-32.
   - Use to introduce competing political meanings of "tenure security."

Use this subsection only to introduce the contemporary turn. The detailed institutional and
ideological analysis belongs in Chapter 3.

### Drafting move for 2.2

Organise the global history around changes in **purpose and authority**, not technological
progress:

1. Revenue and tribute records.
2. Cadastral territorialisation and colonial settlement.
3. Postcolonial development and titling programmes.
4. Contemporary integrated land-administration systems.

At each stage ask whether the record was fiscal, juridical or multipurpose; whether it
recorded persons or parcels; and whether it recognised layered rights or privileged exclusive
ownership.

## 2.3 History of Land Records in India

### 2.3.1 Pre-colonial record-keeping

1. **A** Habib, Irfan. 1999 [1963]. *The Agrarian System of Mughal India, 1556-1707*,
   second revised edition. Oxford University Press.
   - The indispensable account of Mughal agrarian relations, revenue assessment, zamindars,
     peasants and administrative categories.

2. **A** Moosvi, Shireen. 1987. *The Economy of the Mughal Empire, c. 1595: A
   Statistical Study*. Oxford University Press.
   - Use for the structure and evidentiary limits of Ain-i-Akbari revenue data.

3. **A** Moreland, W. H. 1929. *The Agrarian System of Moslem India*. Cambridge
   University Press.
   - An important older work, but read historiographically and against Habib.

4. **B** Sharma, R. S. 1980. *Indian Feudalism, c. 300-1200*. Macmillan.
   - Relevant for land grants, intermediaries and the contested feudalism debate.

5. **B** Stein, Burton. 1980. *Peasant State and Society in Medieval South India*.
   Oxford University Press.
   - Important counterweight to North Indian and Mughal-centred history.

6. **B, primary/colonial interpretation** Abul Fazl. *Ain-i-Akbari*, translated by
   H. Blochmann and H. S. Jarrett.
   - Use as a primary administrative text, not as a transparent description of actual village
     relations.

7. **C, historiographical caution** Baden-Powell, B. H. 1896. *The Indian Village
   Community*. Longmans, Green.
   - Valuable evidence of colonial debates about village property, but not an authoritative
   account of pre-colonial reality.

8. **B** Raychaudhuri, Tapan, and Irfan Habib, eds. 1982. *The Cambridge Economic
   History of India, Volume I: c. 1200-c. 1750*. Cambridge University Press.
   - Consult relevant chapters to situate revenue institutions within agrarian production and
     state extraction.

9. **B** Fukazawa, Hiroshi. 1991. *The Medieval Deccan: Peasants, Social Systems and
   States, Sixteenth to Eighteenth Centuries*. Oxford University Press.
   - Corrects the excessive Mughal/North Indian bias and is relevant to Maratha-era
     institutional inheritances.

**Historiographical warning:** Do not state that rights were "more or less similar throughout
Indian history until the British." Pre-colonial India contained major regional and temporal
variation. Nor should Mughal revenue records be redescribed as modern ownership records.

4. **A** Sturman, Rachel. 2012. *The Government of Social Life in Colonial India:
   Liberalism, Religious Law, and Women's Rights*. Cambridge University Press.
   - Read Part I, especially "Property between Law and Political Economy."

5. **A** Stokes, Eric. 1959. *The English Utilitarians and India*. Clarendon Press.
   - Establishes the ideological context of colonial land-revenue systems and administrative
     experimentation.

6. **A** Kumar, Dharma. 1965. *Land and Caste in South India: Agricultural Labour in
   the Madras Presidency during the Nineteenth Century*. Cambridge University Press.
   - Prevents settlement history from becoming detached from caste, labour and agrarian
     relations.

7. **B** Bayly, C. A. 1996. *Empire and Information: Intelligence Gathering and Social

   - Useful for the interaction of colonial information systems with indigenous knowledge
     intermediaries.

8. **B, primary** Baden-Powell, B. H. 1892. *The Land-Systems of British India*, three
   volumes. Clarendon Press.
   - Major administrative compendium. Use as a primary colonial source and check its

9. **B, primary** Baden-Powell, B. H. 1894. *A Short Account of the Land Revenue and
   Its Administration in British India*. Clarendon Press.

10. **B** Banerjee, Abhijit, and Lakshmi Iyer. 2005. "History, Institutions, and
    Economic Performance: The Legacy of Colonial Land Tenure Systems in India."
    *American Economic Review* 95 (4): 1190-1213.

11. **B** Iyer, Lakshmi. 2010. "Direct versus Indirect Colonial Rule in India:

12. **B, recent** Bhattacharya, Rishabh. 2021. "Know Your Rights: The (Un)making of

18. **B, NIE dialogue** Banerjee and Iyer (2005) should be paired with historical work,

## 2.4 Legal and Institutional Infrastructure for Land Records in India

### Essential analytical sources

1. **A** Goswami, Amlanjyoti, Sudeshna Mitra, Deepika Jha, Kaye Lushington, and
   Sahil Sasidharan. 2022. *Land Records Modernisation in India: An Institutional,
   Legal & Policy Review*. Centre for Policy Research.
   DOI: 10.24943/9788195489305.
   - The best single contemporary map of institutions, records, laws and departmental

### Institutional map to construct

Show separately:

- Revenue administration: RoR, mutation and collection of land revenue.
- Survey and settlement administration: parcel measurement, cadastral maps, classification
  and periodic settlement/resurvey.
- Registration department: registered deeds and stamp duty.
- Civil and revenue courts: adjudication of title, possession and record correction.
- Panchayats/urban local bodies and development authorities: taxation, planning and land use.

The analytical point is that no single institution historically produced a complete,
authoritative and continuously updated account of title, possession, boundaries and
encumbrances.

### Legal propositions to state carefully

- A registered sale deed is evidence of a transaction; registration does not by itself
  constitute a state guarantee of title.
- An RoR/revenue entry generally has evidentiary and fiscal significance, but its precise
  presumptions and legal effects depend on the governing state law.
- Mutation normally updates revenue administration and does not independently create title.
- "Presumptive title" describes the system, but the legal basis of an individual's claim may
  include deeds, inheritance, possession, decrees and statutory grants.
- "Conclusive title" and Torrens registration are not synonymous with perfect accuracy.
  Mirror, curtain and indemnity principles are institutional ideals implemented differently
  across jurisdictions.

## Reading Method

For every source, record:

1. What type of record or institution is being discussed?
2. What function did it serve at that historical moment?
3. What conception of property, ownership or tenure does the author assume?
4. Did the record document existing rights or help create/reorder them?
5. Which actors gained authority and which claims became invisible?
6. How were textual records, maps, possession and registered transactions connected?
7. What evidence supports the argument?
8. What does the source explain for Odisha and what cannot be transferred to Odisha?

This produces an argument-led literature review rather than a sequence of author summaries.
