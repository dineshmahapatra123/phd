## Core Argument

Kapoor, Esposito & Anand (2024) survey India’s land record architecture and argue that while digitisation (notably DILRMP and Bhu-Aadhaar/ULPIN) has materially advanced record-keeping and accessibility, deep legal, institutional, and political frictions (fragmented departmental silos, ambiguous presumptive titling, backlogs, data heterogeneity, and exclusion of customary claims) limit the translation of digital records into secure, inclusive, and enforceable property rights. The paper positions Distributed Ledger Technologies (DLT) as a promising but contingent tool: DLT can improve immutability, auditability, and interoperability but cannot substitute for legal reform, grievance resolution, or legitimacy on the ground.

## Key Concepts

- Presumptive vs conclusive titling; Torrens principles (mirror, curtain, indemnity)
- DILRMP / NLRMP / Bhu-Aadhaar (ULPIN) rollout and state-level variation
- Data heterogeneity, departmental silos (Revenue / Survey / Registration), and backlog risks
- Exclusionary impacts: derecognition of commons, marginalised groups, and non-landowning farmers
- DLT potential and limits (immutability vs garbage-in/garbage-out; privacy; legal recognition)

## Empirical & Case Evidence

- State dashboards: heterogeneous georeferencing & RoR linkage across states (table summarising % completion; national avg ~68% maps digitised, ~50% georeferenced)
- Case pilots: Andhra Pradesh (APCRDA + ChromaWay; Amaravati), Telangana (MoUs, PPPs, Tech Mahindra Blockchain District), Georgia, Ghana, Sweden — varied outcomes show technology adoption is path-dependent and contingent on governance capacity.
- Tamil Nadu CAG audit: digitisation improved access but revealed 61% villages with significant area discrepancies and systemic linkage failures between Registration and Revenue departments.

## Policy Implications

- Technical upgrades (ULPIN, georeferencing, NGDRS, multilingual records) are necessary but insufficient without legal reforms (conclusive titling, indemnity rules), administrative integration, and grievance redress capacity.
- DLT pilots should prioritise well-governed, high-data-quality sub-systems (e.g., encumbrance registries, bank-collateral workflows) before national rollouts.
- Safeguards: privacy, dispute-resolution flows, correction mechanisms, and anti-capture measures to protect commons and marginalised claimants.

## Critique & Limitations

- The paper leans toward techno-optimism in early sections; while it problematises digitisation later, the policy roadmap sometimes understates political economy risks of titling (elite capture, land-banking).
- Limited empirical detail on how digitisation affected on-the-ground tenure security for women, tenants, and pastoralists (calls for micro-studies).
- DLT analysis is high-level; lacks technical governance design patterns for permissioned vs permissionless models tied to legal jurisdictions.

## Thesis Relevance

- Strongly useful for chapters on institutional design of land administration and for the DILRMP case-study. Use to argue: (a) digitisation is necessary but not sufficient; (b) DLT can be an enabling technology but inheriting weak records reproduces exclusion; (c) policy sequencing matters (data-cleaning → legal reforms → pilots → scaled DLT).
