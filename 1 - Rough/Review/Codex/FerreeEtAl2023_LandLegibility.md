# Land and Legibility: When Do Citizens Expect Secure Property Rights in Weak States?

**Authors**: Karen E. Ferree, Lauren Honig, Ellen Lust, and Melanie L. Phillips  
**Year**: 2023  
**Type**: Journal article

### 1. Core Argument

Ferree et al. separate two attributes that land-titling research often treats as one: the authority that validates a land claim and the documentary visibility of that claim. They argue that written documentation can improve perceived tenure security even when a customary authority rather than the state issues it. A conjoint survey experiment in Malawi finds that respondents regarded documented land as less vulnerable to attempted and successful expropriation and as more desirable to purchase. The contribution is therefore not a general defence of titling, but a narrower claim that legibility can be a citizen-controlled resource used across state, customary, familial, and community forums.

> 'Legibility is not a guarantee of secure rights: it is a resource that citizens can use to support their land claims in a variety of different forums' (Ferree et al., 2023, p. 45).

### 2. Conceptual Architecture

**Legibility distinct from authority.** The article defines legibility as the visibility of land rights to actors inside and outside the community. State title combines documentary visibility with statutory authority, but the two features are analytically separable. A chief's letter, notebook entry, receipt, or witnessed agreement may be legible without being a state title.

> 'Legibility ... may be a valuable attribute of property rights on its own, independent of the authority governing these rights' (Ferree et al., 2023, p. 42).

**Legibility as a strategic resource.** Written rights are tangible evidence that claimants can mobilise against neighbours, family members, chiefs, or state officials. Their value lies in portability across forums rather than guaranteed enforcement by one sovereign authority.

> 'Written rights serve as evidence that can be leveraged against the state, customary authorities, neighbors within the community, family members, and others' (Ferree et al., 2023, p. 45).

**Perceived tenure security.** The study operationalises tenure security as the perceived probability that a parcel will not be expropriated. This subjective dimension matters because investment decisions respond to what rights holders believe will happen, not only to formal legal status.

**Relational property rights.** The security of a claim depends partly on whether other community members uphold or challenge it. Property is therefore not reducible to a claimant-authority dyad.

> 'The perspectives of others are therefore constitutive of one's tenure security on the ground' (Ferree et al., 2023, p. 45).

**Documentary pluralism.** The article identifies property documentation outside statutory titling: receipts, handwritten agreements, chiefs' registers, letters, customary certificates, and community maps. These instruments can strengthen claims while retaining plural authority structures.

**Distributional legibility.** Documentation strengthens the person named in it but can weaken omitted claimants. Legibility is therefore neither uniformly emancipatory nor uniformly coercive.

> 'The rights of claimants that are excluded from a document will be weakened relative to those who are included' (Ferree et al., 2023, p. 55).

### 3. Empirical Terrain

The study examines Malawi, where customary tenure remains dominant, statutory title is uncommon, land scarcity is substantial, and citizens trust customary authorities more than national political institutions. This makes Malawi useful for separating confidence in documentation from confidence in the state.

The main design is a single-profile conjoint experiment embedded in a telephone survey of 4,893 adults across twenty-eight of Malawi's twenty-nine districts between August and October 2020. Respondents evaluated a hypothetical one-hectare rural land purchase. Randomised attributes included the buyer's gender and marital status, whether permission came from a chief or the Ministry of Lands, whether written documents were supplied, local coethnicity, and whether neighbours helped one another. Outcomes measured anticipated attempted expropriation, anticipated successful expropriation, and willingness to recommend the purchase.

Written documents reduced the expected probability of an attempted taking by about five percentage points and a successful taking by about four percentage points. They raised purchase recommendations by about three percentage points. The authors found no interaction between documentation and the issuing authority: papers from chiefs and state agencies both increased perceived security and desirability. Documentation mattered more where neighbours were less helpful, for women purchasers on one security outcome, and among more educated respondents.

### 4. Theoretical Lineage

The paper directly revises the Scottian tradition. Scott treats legibility primarily as a technology through which states simplify, tax, and control populations. Ferree et al. retain the informational insight but shift the actor and direction of demand: citizens may actively seek documentary visibility because it helps them defend claims.

The paper also intervenes in land-titling research associated with de Soto, Feder, Deininger, Platteau, and related development scholarship. Rather than asking whether state title as a package produces investment or credit, it decomposes that package into documentation and authority.

Berry's relational account is important to the article's claim that claims operate through multiple forums and social networks. The paper also complements Lund's recognition framework: documents may make claims recognisable without transferring all enforcement authority to the state. Sánchez-Talanquer's work provides a nearby demand-side correction, although Ferree et al. focus on ordinary citizens seeking documentary leverage rather than established owners selectively seeking state protection.

### 6. Internal Tensions and Silences

The treatment compares explicit reassurance that documents will exist with silence about documents. Respondents in the control condition may have assumed that Ministry-issued rights were written and chief-issued rights were oral. The authors therefore interpret documentation effects as lower-bound estimates, but the treatment does not compare identified document types or contents.

The experiment measures perceived security for a hypothetical purchaser rather than actual claim durability, investment, conflict, or dispossession. The paper establishes an expectation mechanism, not the legal effectiveness of informal documents.

The sample requires telephone access and is somewhat more educated, connected, urban, and wealthy than Malawi's population. This matters because education strengthens the documentation effect.

The vignette fixes attribute order and uses a buyer rather than an heir, tenant, sharecropper, or customary secondary claimant. Its gender findings may therefore not generalise to inheritance, where women's insecurity is often greatest.

The paper argues that documents travel across multiple forums but does not observe which forum ultimately validates or rejects a document. It therefore leaves the relationship between documentary legibility and institutional recognition partially unresolved.

Finally, the paper's policy optimism about customary certificates and community mapping is constrained by its own strongest caveat: every documentary system privileges recorded claimants over omitted rights holders. The experiment cannot identify those exclusion effects because the vignette contains a single purchaser and no rival or secondary claimant.

### 7. Thesis Relevance - PHD_LENS Filter

**Test 1 - Research Question Link**

- **RQ2**: Directly informs how different groups may use and value updated RoRs. It shows that perceived security may arise from documentary evidence itself, not only confidence in state enforcement.
- **RQ4**: Helps explain why record-reality gaps persist yet records retain practical value. A document can be strategically useful without conclusively settling possession or ownership.
- **RQ5**: Complicates a purely technocratic critique. Digitisation may provide citizen-facing documentary leverage, but its distributional effects depend on whose claims enter the record.
- **RQ6**: Supports evaluating alternative documentation and integration mechanisms by their cross-forum usability, not only by whether they approximate conclusive state title.

**Test 2 - Geographic and Institutional Grounding**

Malawi is non-Indian, but it passes as a comparable plural-tenure setting for a named theoretical reason: customary and state authorities coexist, documentation varies independently of formal title, and state capacity is limited. The transferable mechanism is the separation of documentary legibility from issuing authority. Malawi-specific effect sizes should not be generalised to Odisha.

### 8. Ideas and Questions Sparked

- **Create a document-forum matrix.** This advances Ch. 5.6 because fieldwork can record each document villagers hold and the forums in which it is accepted: family, village, panchayat, revenue office, bank, welfare office, police, and court.
- **Separate demand for RoRs from trust in the revenue state.** This opens an argument that villagers may value Bhulekh printouts as portable evidence even while distrusting mutation procedures or dispute resolution.
- **Treat digitisation as reducing evidentiary friction, not necessarily resolving title.** This advances Ch. 3.4 because it identifies a limited but real benefit of modernisation without equating digital availability with legal or spatial accuracy.
- **Ask whether documentation substitutes for social capital.** This is a fieldwork question: are papers more important to migrants, women, weakly connected households, caste minorities, or residents without influential kin?
- **Audit omitted rights in every record.** This advances Ch. 5.5 because the relevant unit is not only the named owner but also heirs, tenants, sharecroppers, possessors, common users, and persons holding secondary rights.
- **Distinguish documentary legibility from institutional recognition.** This extends [[Contracts of Recognition]]: a document may make a claim visible without compelling a Tehsildar, court, bank, or customary authority to recognise it.
