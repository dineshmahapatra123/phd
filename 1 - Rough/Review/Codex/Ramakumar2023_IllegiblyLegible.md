# Illegibly Legible: Outcomes of a Land Records Modernisation Programme in South India
**Authors**: Ramakumar R and Padmini Ramesh  
**Year**: 2023  
**Type**: Article

## Core Argument

This paper argues that land records modernisation in Telangana failed because it was detached from the historical and political economy of land. The programme did not simply inherit technical defects; it reproduced colonial legacies, landlord manipulation, bureaucratic opacity, and tenancy exclusion in a new digital form. The core irony is captured in the title itself: records were made formally "legible" to the state, but remained socially and politically illegible on the ground.

The article’s strongest claim is that the LRUP was not a neutral administrative reform. It was designed to serve the Rythu Bandhu cash transfer scheme, and therefore to identify beneficiaries rather than to resolve land relations. In that sense, the scheme shifted from agrarian reform toward targeted welfare and free-market land governance.

## Conceptual Architecture

The article is organised around a small but powerful set of concepts that carry the argument.

- **Land as resource and commodity**: land is simultaneously a site of production, a commodity, a site of competing non-agricultural uses, and an object of state administration. This is the paper’s starting contradiction.
- **Legibility / illegibility**: the state seeks to convert locally complex and historically layered land relations into administratively readable records, but the digital system ends up reproducing error and exclusion.
- **Path dependence**: post-colonial land administration remains locked into colonial record systems and landlord-led manipulation.
- **Technocracy**: the paper treats technocracy as depoliticising governance, insulating expert systems from democratic correction while claiming efficiency.
- **Presumptive title vs conclusive title**: LRUP sits between these two logics, but is really neither; it reprocesses presumptive titles without building a genuine conclusive record.
- **Ownership vs possession**: the paper repeatedly distinguishes the legal owner recorded in the system from the actual cultivator or possessor on the ground.
- **Welfare targeting**: the programme is reoriented toward identifying cash-transfer beneficiaries, not toward agrarian justice.

## Evidence and Examples Used

The empirical terrain is Telangana, especially Adilabad district and Talamadagu mandal, with the paper also tracing the colonial and post-colonial genealogy of land records in India more broadly. The authors combine official reports, secondary literature, interviews with revenue officials at multiple administrative levels, field observations, and complaint data from a farmer helpline.

The key evidence is administrative and institutional rather than causal in the econometric sense. The paper shows how three record systems operate in silos, how resurvey is rare, how old cadastral maps remain the governing spatial baseline, how the sethwar benchmark distorts updating, and how tenants were excluded from the new system. It also documents complaint volumes, office bottlenecks, corruption, legal uncertainty, and public resistance.

## Theoretical Lineage

The paper stands in the intersection of Marx, Polanyi, Scott, and the Indian political economy literature on land reforms and land records. Marx appears in the account of land as a revenue object for the state rather than a social relation for cultivators. Polanyi supplies the critique of land as a fictitious commodity. Scott frames the legibility project. Byres, Appu, Wadhwa, and Deininger/Feder-style titling debates anchor the historical and policy discussion.

The paper is also in direct conversation with neoliberal titling orthodoxy, including de Soto, World Bank-inspired conclusive titling arguments, and the EoDB logic. Its Indian comparator is particularly strong in the discussion of Bhoomi in Karnataka and the critique by Benjamin and Raman that digitisation was not merely technical but politically reordering land access.

## Key Passages

### Legibility and colonial inheritance

> "made not for the people, who cultivate the soil, nor for the holder, who owns it, but for the government that taxes it" (p. 731)  
**Gloss**: This is the paper’s clearest summary of why colonial land records are structurally misaligned with ownership and possession.

> "the task of land administration came to be identified as one of transforming these ‘illegible’ records into ‘legible’ records" (p. 732)  
**Gloss**: The paper uses Scott’s framework to show that record modernisation is a state project of simplification, not a neutral technical upgrade.

> "the illegibility of land records not as a ‘political condition’ but as a ‘normative’ problem" (p. 733)  
**Gloss**: The Bhoomi critique is central because it shows how neoliberal reform depoliticises land conflict.

### Neoliberal turn

> "land records must be modernised not to transform agrarian relations but to create a free land market" (p. 733)  
**Gloss**: This captures the shift from land reform to market facilitation.

> "the administrative and political domination of society by a state elite … through the application of instrumentally rational techniques" (p. 733)  
**Gloss**: Technocracy is presented as a governance style that weakens democratic accountability while claiming efficiency.

> "conclusive titling" (p. 733)  
**Gloss**: The Torrens-style ideal is the target of critique, especially when applied without resurvey and institutional integration.

### Indian land-record fragmentation

> "These three departments have historically worked in compartments or silos" (p. 734)  
**Gloss**: This is the structural reason why digitisation alone cannot fix the system.

> "the last resurvey in most Indian villages was conducted during the colonial period" (p. 735)  
**Gloss**: The paper shows that the spatial base of Indian land records is often decades out of date.

> "the absence of up-to-date land records is a serious obstacle in the implementation of land reforms" (p. 736)  
**Gloss**: This grounds the post-independence argument that records matter, but also shows how reform was blocked.

### LRUP design and failure

> "a special timebound, purification and updation of land records" (p. 737)  
**Gloss**: The paper shows that the government framed the scheme as purification, but this was really a targeted political intervention.

> "the objective of LRUP was fundamentally different in its motivation" (p. 748)  
**Gloss**: The scheme was tied to welfare targeting, not agrarian restructuring.

> "a curious third way" (p. 747)  
**Gloss**: LRUP was neither a genuine fresh resurvey nor a simple presumptive update.

> "even if the area was more or less by just one gunta than in the sethwar, the portal did not accept the data" (p. 749)  
**Gloss**: This shows how a rigid portal design amplified historical error instead of correcting it.

> "the government does not have details of tenant farmers" (p. 750)  
**Gloss**: The exclusion of tenants is not an accident; it is a conscious political choice.

### Concluding claim

> "the nature of political economy around land can frustrate attempts by the neoliberal state to dislocate land from the bundle of claims and counterclaims" (p. 751)  
**Gloss**: This is the final synthesis of the paper’s political economy argument.

> "the ‘illegibility’ of records is a technical problem of administrative dysfunction" (p. 751)  
**Gloss**: The paper rejects the technocratic assumption that the problem is merely administrative.

## Internal Tensions and Silences

The paper is strong on political economy, but it leaves some openings.

First, it criticises technocracy while still accepting that updated records are necessary for agrarian reform and welfare delivery. That creates a productive but unresolved tension: the paper wants modernisation, but not the kind that current state capacity can easily deliver.

Second, it is much stronger on tenancy, landlordism, and bureaucratic manipulation than on gender. Women’s land claims, women’s names in PPBs, and intra-household title effects are only marginally visible.

Third, the paper is persuasive on Telangana’s case, but its comparison with other states remains brief. It gestures to Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Kerala, but does not fully compare their resurvey or digital update models.

Fourth, the paper critiques the portal architecture, but does not deeply unpack procurement, software design, or vendor politics. That is a gap if one wants to study the political economy of digital infrastructure itself.

## Ideas and Questions Sparked

- This paper gives a strong comparative benchmark for Odisha because it shows what happens when digitisation is treated as a substitute for resurvey. This advances Ch. 4.5 because Telangana becomes a cautionary case for DILRMP outcomes.
- The LRUP makes me ask whether Odisha’s Bhulekh/Bhunaksha integration is solving a record problem or merely reformatting old contradictions. This opens an argument about digital legibility versus social legibility.
- The tenant exclusion finding suggests a direct link between land records and welfare exclusion. This advances Ch. 3.3 and Ch. 5.8 because records become an eligibility filter, not just an administrative archive.
- The paper’s treatment of the sethwar and outdated cadastral base suggests that old survey documents can become a hidden constitutional layer of land governance. This is a question for fieldwork: how many local disputes in Odisha are actually disputes about inherited survey baselines rather than current ownership?
- The critique of technocracy points to a larger question about complaint redressal. This opens an argument about whether online grievance systems reduce corruption or simply relocate discretion into less visible administrative layers.

## Conclusion

This is a high-value paper for the thesis. It directly supports the argument that land records modernisation fails when it is detached from land reform, tenant recognition, and accurate ground-level survey. It is especially useful for the chapters on DILRMP design, political economy, and the gap between records and possession.
