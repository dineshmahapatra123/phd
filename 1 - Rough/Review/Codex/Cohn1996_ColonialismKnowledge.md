# Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge: The British in India

**Author(s)**: Bernard S. Cohn | **Year**: 1996 | **Type**: Book

---

### 1. Core Argument

Cohn's central argument is that British colonial rule in India was built not only through conquest, revenue extraction, and military force, but through the production, reordering, and institutional use of knowledge. Colonialism worked by converting Indian languages, legal traditions, histories, objects, territories, bodies, and customs into administrative facts. These facts were then arranged into reports, grammars, surveys, legal digests, censuses, museums, maps, and official categories that made India governable by a foreign state.

The book's most important thesis-facing contribution is the concept of **investigative modalities**: structured ways of gathering and transforming information into usable forms for rule. Cohn's frame directly helps explain how colonial land administration became a knowledge system before it became a technical records system. The land-settlement process, Survey of India, legal codification of property and inheritance, and categorisation of people and places were not neutral administrative improvements. They were technologies for fixing, bounding, classifying, and settling India.

Defining quote: Cohn writes that the British "invaded and conquered not only a territory but an epistemological space as well" (p. 4). For the thesis, this sentence is the bridge between colonial land records and present-day land records modernisation: the problem is not only whether records are accurate, but what epistemology of land they encode.

---

### 2. Conceptual Architecture

**Investigative modalities**

Cohn defines an investigative modality as a body of needed information plus the procedures by which it is gathered, ordered, classified, and converted into administrative form. Its outputs include "published reports, statistical returns, histories, gazetteers, legal codes, and encyclopedias" (p. 5). This is the book's load-bearing concept. For the thesis, land settlement, survey, cadastral mapping, mutation registers, RoRs, Bhulekh, and Bhunaksha can be read as historically layered investigative modalities.

**Documentation project**

The colonial state governed by producing durable documentary forms. Reports, commissions, statistical tables, revenue accounts, surveys, and legal digests created the information infrastructure of rule. Cohn notes that documentation "created and normalized a vast amount of information" that formed the basis of state capacity (p. 3). This matters for land records because the RoR is not only evidence; it is a historical artefact of state documentation.

**Officializing procedures**

Cohn situates colonial knowledge within the wider growth of modern state capacity. European states extended power by "defining and classifying space," recording property transactions, counting populations, and standardising languages and scripts (p. 3). This concept is directly relevant to DILRMP: digitisation is a new officializing procedure layered over older revenue and settlement procedures.

**Historiographic modality**

The historiographic modality produced histories that made British rule appear legitimate, necessary, and continuous with prior Indian forms. For land, the key point is that settlement reports emerged from inquiries into "customs and local histories" relating to land tenure (pp. 5-6). These reports helped transform local tenure practices into district-wise administrative knowledge.

**Survey modality**

Survey is the most directly land-records-relevant modality. Cohn stresses that "survey" meant more than measuring land: it included mapping, inspection, valuation, exploration, and classification. In India, survey covered everything from mapping territory to "the most minute measuring of a peasant's fields" (p. 7). It created an "imaginary grid" through which the government could locate, classify, and administer sites (p. 8).

**Enumerative modality**

Enumeration created governable categories by counting and classifying. Cohn's discussion of census operations is crucial because it warns against treating enumeration as passive description. He argues that the census produced "social categories by which India was ordered for administrative purposes" (p. 8). The parallel for land records is clear: digitised land categories may not simply reflect land relations; they may produce and harden them.

**Legal modality / codification**

Cohn's chapter on law shows how British judges and Orientalists sought a fixed body of Hindu and Muslim law in texts, digests, and translations. The goal was to make Indian legal knowledge accessible to British courts and to reduce dependence on Indian legal specialists. This matters to land governance because property, contract, succession, inheritance, and revenue liability became legible through British legal categories.

**Objectification**

Objectification is the process by which Indian forms of knowledge and practice were converted into European objects of knowledge. Grammars, legal codes, revenue reports, museum catalogues, and ethnographic categories transformed living systems into extractable and administrable forms. For the thesis, this concept helps name the conversion of socially embedded land claims into state-recognised records.

**Classification and reification**

Cohn repeatedly shows that classification changes what it classifies. Caste, language, law, art, and custom became fixed through administrative and scholarly classification. This has direct relevance to RoRs and cadastral maps: when messy possession, inheritance, commons, encroachment, tenancy, and multi-owner claims are forced into record categories, the record may reify one version of land relations.

**Colonial self-mimesis**

In the legal chapter, Cohn shows that British attempts to recover "authentic" Indian law eventually produced something resembling English case law. This is the strongest conceptual warning for reform programmes: even when the state claims to preserve existing rights, its administrative form may remake those rights in its own image.

### 3. Empirical Terrain or Evidence and Examples Used

Cohn studies British India from the late seventeenth through nineteenth centuries, with particular attention to the East India Company state, Bengal, north India, south India, Mysore, Madras, Calcutta, and imperial circuits between India and Britain. His evidence is historical-anthropological rather than statistical. He reads colonial records, grammars, dictionaries, legal digests, settlement reports, survey projects, museum collections, military dress regulations, paintings, travel accounts, and administrative correspondence as texts of rule.

The most thesis-relevant empirical terrains are:

**Language and command**

Cohn traces how Company officials moved from dependence on Indian intermediaries to the production of grammars, dictionaries, class books, and translations. The British needed language to issue commands, collect taxes, maintain law and order, and classify society. This is directly relevant to the revenue bureaucracy because linguistic command was a precondition for revenue command.

**Land settlement and revenue**

In the introduction, Cohn identifies land settlement as "the most extensive and continuous administrative activity of the British" (p. 5). In the legal chapter, he shows why revenue collection required identifying who "owned" land or who could be made responsible for revenue. British officials faced Persian accounts, local usages, customary claims, and quasi-feudal rights that did not fit familiar Western legal categories.

**Survey and mapping**

Cohn uses Rennell, Lambton, Mackenzie, Buchanan Hamilton, and Cunningham to show how survey moved beyond measurement into a total documentation project. New territory triggered new surveys, and survey knowledge ranged from maps and boundaries to zoology, geology, economic products, ethnography, history, and sociology. For the thesis, this is vital for situating cadastral surveys within a wider colonial knowledge project.

**Law and property**

The chapter on law examines Hastings, Jones, Colebrooke, Hindu law, Muslim law, the Dewani courts, and the transformation of Indian legal traditions into British-administered legal forms. The most important land-records point is that British revenue administration depended on legal classification: Was a payment rent or tax? Was the receiver a landlord, ruler, proprietor, zamindar, or intermediary? Was the payer tenant or subject?

**Objects, artefacts, and value**

The chapter on museology and objects is less directly relevant to land records, but it is conceptually useful. It shows how value is administratively and politically assigned. Objects became art, antiquities, specimens, commodities, or trophies through colonial classification. This helps think about land as a classified object: plot, khata, commons, wasteland, private land, government land.

**Cloth, dress, and custom**

The final chapter is less central to land records, but it is useful for one thesis question: how colonial classifications of "custom" become enforceable expectations. Cohn argues that once British officials defined something as custom or proper conduct, deviation could be treated as disorder. This provides a useful analogy for record-of-rights regimes: once the state records a claim in one form, alternative social claims become deviations from the record.

### 4. Theoretical Lineage

Cohn's work sits at the intersection of historical anthropology, colonial studies, South Asian history, and the anthropology of the state. Nicholas Dirks's foreword explicitly places Cohn before and alongside Foucault and Said in the study of knowledge and power. The book is Foucauldian in method but not derivative: Cohn uses archival detail to show how rule was built through procedures of classification, translation, survey, and documentation.

The book is also in dialogue with:

- **Foucault**: power/knowledge, classification, discipline, surveillance, documentary state forms.
- **Edward Said / Orientalism**: Orientalist knowledge as part of imperial rule, though Cohn gives a more institutionally grounded account.
- **Historical institutionalism**: not named in those terms, but the book is a strong account of path dependence, institutional inheritance, and state formation.
- **Legal anthropology**: especially in the transformation of Hindu law into Anglo-Indian case law.
- **Anthropology of colonial modernity**: colonial India as a laboratory for modern techniques of documentation, classification, and state capacity.

For the PhD lens, Cohn should not be used as a generic "colonial knowledge/power" theorist. His relevance is strongest where he supplies mechanisms: survey, land settlement, legal codification, revenue classification, property categories, and the documentary state.

### 6. Internal Tensions and Silences

**The framework can become too capacious.** Nearly every colonial knowledge practice can be described as an investigative modality. For thesis use, the concept must be narrowed to land-administration mechanisms: settlement, survey, revenue inquiry, legal codification, cadastral mapping, and record production.

**Cohn is stronger on colonial production than postcolonial persistence.** The book gestures toward the survival of colonial modalities in modern states, but it does not trace independent India's land records institutions in detail. The thesis should use Cohn as genealogy, not as evidence for DILRMP outcomes.

**The land argument is distributed rather than central.** Cohn repeatedly discusses land settlement, revenue, survey, property, and law, but the book is not a land-records monograph. Its relevance passes the lens because these passages are institutionally direct, not merely metaphorical. Still, chapters on clothing and museology should be mined cautiously.

**The book risks overstating British coherence.** Cohn knows colonial projects were contradictory, diffuse, and sometimes improvised. Yet the modality framework can make colonial rule appear more systematised than it was. For Odisha fieldwork, this matters: revenue offices and digital platforms may reproduce inherited logics while remaining fragmented, under-capacitated, and inconsistent.

**Indian agency appears unevenly.** Cohn notes that Indians were not passive and that they repurposed colonial knowledge technologies. But the strongest sections emphasise British classification and objectification. The thesis should leave room for villagers, revenue intermediaries, officials, and claimants to strategically use RoRs, Bhulekh, and cadastral maps.

**Custom is treated through colonial interpretation.** Cohn shows that British ideas of custom froze living practices. For land records, this is powerful, but the thesis must still ask which customary practices were exclusionary, caste-bound, gendered, or contested. Not every non-state claim is emancipatory.

#### Active Debates Engaged

**Debate 3 - Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy of reform**

Cohn's strongest contribution is the claim that techniques of knowledge are techniques of rule. Survey, enumeration, legal coding, and documentation do not merely solve administrative problems; they define the problem in terms the state can act on.

**Debate 4 - Colonial revenue record to postcolonial title document**

The book is load-bearing for this debate. It shows that colonial land administration began as revenue, classification, and control, not as a secure-title project for citizens. This is central to the structural mismatch between revenue records and title claims.

**Debate 5 - Spatial-textual disjuncture**

Cohn's survey modality shows how spatial knowledge becomes gridded and textualised. It supports a historical account of why maps and records can acquire authority even when their relation to ground reality is incomplete or politically mediated.

**Debate 7 - NIE efficiency logic vs. agrarian social relations**

Cohn is useful as a counterweight to efficiency-oriented accounts. He shows that institutional standardisation can erase social complexity while appearing to rationalise it. Use cautiously: Cohn does not debate NIE directly, but he supplies a historical-anthropological critique of administrative rationalisation.

### 8. Ideas and Questions Sparked

**Idea: Treat land records modernisation as a new investigative modality.**

This advances Ch. 3.4 because DILRMP can be analysed not only as digitisation but as a system for gathering, ordering, classifying, and transforming land claims into state-usable data.

**Idea: Separate accuracy from epistemology.**

This opens an argument about whether a perfectly digitised RoR would still misrecognise possession, inheritance, commons, gendered claims, and informal tenancy if the categories themselves are inherited from a revenue-state logic.

**Idea: Use Cohn to historicise the word "record."**

This advances Ch. 2.1 because land records can be framed as part of a broader documentary state form: record as evidence, record as command, record as fiscal instrument, and record as political technology.

**Idea: Read Survey and Settlement as both spatial and textual power.**

This advances Ch. 2.3.2 and Ch. 4.2.1 because survey fixes land spatially while settlement fixes rights, liabilities, and categories textually.

**Idea: The "ownership" question is historically produced.**

This advances Ch. 5.5 because Cohn shows that British revenue administration needed to identify who "owned" land so that revenue and legal responsibility could be assigned. This helps explain why possession, cultivation, inheritance, and social claim continue to fit awkwardly into official records.

**Idea: DILRMP's efficiency language echoes older claims about efficient use of facts.**

This advances Ch. 3.4 because the programme's promise of integration, computerisation, and uniformity should be read alongside Cohn's account of administrative power arising from fact production.

**Idea: The village record room and the digital portal are not opposites.**

This opens an argument about continuity: Bhulekh and Bhunaksha may appear modern, but they inherit older documentary forms and categories from settlement and revenue administration.

**Question for fieldwork: When villagers dispute Bhulekh or RoR entries, do they challenge the data, the category, or the authority of the record itself?**

This advances Ch. 5.6 and Ch. 5.7 because it distinguishes technical error from epistemic mismatch.

**Question for fieldwork: Which local land claims become invisible when officials ask only for record-backed ownership?**

This advances Ch. 5.5 and Ch. 5.8 because welfare inclusion, partition, encroachment, tenancy, and possession may all depend on how the record recognises or fails to recognise claims.

**Question for Chapter 6: Does parcel-level measurement reproduce Cohn's "imaginary grid" at village scale?**

This advances Ch. 6.2 because physical parcel measurement can be used to test whether cadastral and digital grids align with possession and use.
