## Core Argument

Bernard Cohn's groundbreaking work reframes colonialism fundamentally: **colonialism was not primarily military conquest but an epistemological conquest—a systematic project to convert Indian knowledge systems into European objects of knowledge, thereby making India "knowable," "legible," and ultimately "ruleable."**

The central concept is **"investigative modalities"**: the techniques, procedures, and institutional arrangements through which British colonisers collected, classified, and transformed information about India. These modalities included historiography (constructing India's "history" to legitimise British rule), surveying (mapping and fixing spatial boundaries), enumeration (census-taking to classify and categorise populations), and law (extracting and codifying "Hindu law" and "Muslim law" from Sanskrit and Persian texts).

Cohn's crucial insight: **These modalities were not neutral administrative tools**. Rather, they were technologies of domination that simultaneously (1) served immediate extraction needs (taxation, legal jurisdiction, military control) and (2) produced fundamental transformations in how Indians understood themselves, their society, and their relationship to land, law, and identity.

For land records and DILRMP specifically: Cohn provides the genealogy of how colonial "investigative modalities" (surveys, revenue records, legal codification) transformed land from a socially-embedded asset (with complex kinship, ritual, and communal meanings) into a state-legible commodity measurable, taxable, and controllable. DILRMP inherits and reproduces this colonial logic.

## The Investigative Modalities Framework

Cohn identifies six core modalities, each a distinct technique for making India "knowable":

### 1. **Historiographic Modality**: Writing India's Past to Justify the Present

The British constructed India's "history" to legitimise their conquest. This history took three forms:

- **Ideological histories** (Alexander Dow, James Mill, Robert Orme): Narratives depicting India as a despotic, stagnant civilisation that only British rule could modernise. This narrative justified conquest as a "civilising mission."
- **Settlement reports**: District-by-district investigations of "customs and local histories" relating to land tenure. These were framed as neutral documentation but actually served to identify taxable assets and controllable populations.
- **Popular histories**: Emblematic stories (Black Hole of Calcutta, Tipu Sultan, siege of Lucknow) that consolidated British power through emotional narratives, creating memorials and "sacred spaces" that physically embedded British authority.

**Relevance to thesis**: DILRMP is a contemporary historiographic modality. It constructs a narrative of India's land records as "defective," "chaotic," "unmapped"—thereby justifying technological modernisation as inevitable and beneficial. This narrative obscures that colonial record-keeping was designed for extraction, not livelihood. DILRMP reproduces this justification.

---

### 2. **Linguistic Modality**: Converting Language into a Command Tool

Cohn's essay on "The Command of Language and the Language of Command" reveals how British officials systematically appropriated Indian languages (Persian, Sanskrit, Bengali) not for understanding but for commanding and controlling.

**Key transformation**: In the 17th century, when the British arrived, they relied on Indian intermediaries (banians, vakils, munshis) who were multilingual and possessed deep knowledge of courts, trade networks, and political protocols. These intermediaries had interpretive power.

By the 1770s-1820s, the British produced grammars, dictionaries, and teaching aids in Indian languages, designed to give British officials "working knowledge" sufficient to:
- Issue commands without Indian intermediaries
- Collect taxes
- Maintain legal jurisdiction
- Classify social groups

**Cohn's example**: When Warren Hastings wanted to implement his plan to govern by "ancient Indian institutions" (particularly Hindu law), he had to extract and codify "the laws of the Hindus." He assembled pandits to compile Sanskrit legal texts, which were then translated into Persian (by a Muslim scribe), then into English (by N.B. Halhed, who knew little Sanskrit).

> "The knowledge of languages was necessary to issue commands, collect taxes, maintain law and order—and to create other forms of knowledge about the people they were ruling."

**Critical outcome**: What emerged was not authentic "Hindu law" but a British-codified version that (1) treated fluid, contextual legal traditions as fixed, written codes, (2) privileged elite Brahmanical texts over local practice, and (3) created a legal system that could be administered by English judges with minimal input from Indian scholars.

**Relevance to DILRMP**: DILRMP follows this exact logic. It uses digital technology (replacing Persian/Sanskrit with databases) to extract and codify "land rights" into uniform, state-legible categories. Digital records reduce the need for local intermediaries (village councils, customary authorities) just as grammar books reduced reliance on Indian linguistic intermediaries. The result: land becomes state property, knowable to bureaucrats, standardised across regions, extractable through taxation and policy.

---

### 3. **Surveying Modality**: Mapping to Fix and Control

Cohn documents how British surveys transformed India from a fluid, politically-divided space into a fixed, bounded, measurable territory. James Rennell, Colin Mackenzie, William Lambton, and Alexander Cunningham conducted systematic surveys that:
- Created an "imaginary grid" covering India
- Described and classified terrain, zoology, geology, botany, ethnography, economic products, history, and sociology
- Transformed knowledge into archives, gazetteers, and administrative reports

**The outcome**: India became knowable as an object. Not in the sense of being understood from Indian epistemic frameworks but as an object to be studied, measured, controlled by external observers (British surveyors, directors, policy-makers).

**Relevance to DILRMP**: Land-record surveys similarly aim to create a uniform grid over India's land. Traditional knowledge about land (based on local hydrology, seasonal use, ritual significance, kinship claims) is erased in favour of Cartesian measurement. Boundaries are fixed where they were fluid. Rights are standardised where they were contextual. The result: land becomes knowable to the state, manageable through policy, legible to markets.

---

### 4. **Enumerative Modality**: Censuses and the Reification of Categories

Cohn's analysis of the 1881 Census reveals how enumeration is not neutral counting but a technology that **creates and reifies social categories**. The Census asked questions about age, occupation, caste, religion, literacy, place of birth. In answering these questions, British officials and Indian enumerators were not discovering pre-existing categories but **creating them**.

> "It is my hypothesis that what was entailed in the construction of the census operations was the creation of social categories by which India was ordered for administrative purposes...The British assumed that the census reflected the basic sociological facts of India. This it did, but through the enumerative modality, the project also objectified social, cultural, and linguistic differences among the peoples of India."

**Crucial consequence**: Caste, religion, occupation—categories that had been fluid, contextual, overlapping—became fixed, countable, standardised. This standardisation enabled administrative control: the state could now identify "criminal tribes," allocate resources by religion, regulate occupations.

**Relevance to DILRMP**: DILRMP uses enumerative modality to standardise land categories. Instead of recognising land as having multiple, overlapping claims (livelihood, spiritual, communal, individual), digital records classify land into discrete, mutually exclusive categories: "agricultural," "wasteland," "urban," "village commons." Each plot is assigned a unique identifier, a standardised area, an enumerated list of "rights holders." This standardisation enables administrative extraction: easier taxation, easier identification of "surplus" land for acquisition, easier commodification.

---

### 5. **Legal Modality**: Codifying "Hindu Law" and "Muslim Law"

Cohn's essay "Law and the Colonial State in India" documents how British officials extracted and codified law from Sanskrit and Persian texts, producing documents like Halhed's *Gentoo Code* and later Sir William Jones' projects.

**The transformation**: Indian law had been contextual, practised by pandits and maulavis who tailored decisions to local circumstance, caste, kinship. British officials demanded fixed, written, hierarchically-ordered legal codes. They then translated these codes into English, so that English judges could administer them with minimal reliance on Indian "lawyers."

> "Jones wanted to provide the English courts in India, Crown and Company, with a sure basis on which they could render decisions consonant with a 'true' or 'pure' version of Hindu law. Then the pandits, Brahmans, and Indian 'lawyers,' Jones believed, henceforth could not 'deal out Hindoo law as they please, and make it at reasonable rates, when they cannot find it ready made.'"

**Outcome**: Law became a tool of state control, not community justice. Disputes over land, inheritance, marriage were now decided by English judges applying codified "Hindu law" rather than by local councils applying customary practice.

**Relevance to DILRMP**: DILRMP similarly aims to extract and codify land law. Rather than recognising land disputes as embedded in social relations (kinship claims, customary rights, ritual obligations), DILRMP treats them as questions of "ownership" (who has a title-deed?) and "procedure" (follow the official dispute-resolution process). By standardising land law, DILRMP enables centralised state adjudication, reducing the autonomy of local dispute-resolution mechanisms.

## The Conceptual Architecture: Three Mechanisms of Colonial Power

Cohn's analysis reveals three mechanisms through which these investigative modalities enabled colonial rule:

### 1. **Objectification**: Converting Knowledge into Objects

Indians possessed complex, contextual knowledge about land, law, language, caste, ritual. British officials treated this knowledge as information to be extracted, standardised, and converted into objects (grammars, legal codes, gazetteers, census reports). This conversion from knowledge-in-use to knowledge-as-object enabled control: once objectified, knowledge could be archived, transmitted, standardised, applied.

**For land**: Indigenous knowledge about land (which areas flooded seasonally, which were sacred, which supported commons) was extracted and converted into standardised survey maps and revenue records. These objects could then be archived in colonial offices, transmitted to new administrators, standardised across regions.

### 2. **Categorisation & Reification**: Making Social Fluidity Legible

Indian society was characterised by fluid categories: overlapping caste, occupation, kinship claims; contextual rights to land and resources; multiple legitimate authorities (village councils, Brahman scholars, Muslim judges, lineage heads). British officials required fixed categories to administer, tax, and control.

Through investigative modalities, fluid categories became fixed and reified. Caste became a fixed identity rather than contextual status. Land became a fixed property rather than a fluid set of overlapping rights. Occupation became a fixed census category rather than a contextual livelihood practice.

**Outcome**: Diversity and fluidity were replaced by standardisation and hierarchy. This standardisation enabled administrative control but destroyed the flexibility and resilience of traditional institutions.

### 3. **Distancing**: Replacing Local Intermediaries with State Officials

By extracting knowledge (through surveys, grammars, legal codes), British officials could replace local intermediaries. Instead of relying on pandits to interpret law, English judges could consult codified "Hindu law." Instead of relying on munshis to translate, British officials could learn grammar. This distancing—replacing intermediaries with direct state command—weakened local institutional authority and strengthened state power.

## Path Dependence: Colonial Investigative Modalities Persist

Cohn's most important implication: **These investigative modalities did not end with political decolonisation**. Independent India inherited colonial administrative systems (census, survey, law codes) and the associated epistemology: the assumption that knowledge must be standardised, objectified, hierarchically ordered, and deployed by state officials for purposes of control.

Postcolonial land records, land reform programmes, land titling initiatives—all continue the colonial project of making land legible, extractable, and controllable. The digital transformation (DILRMP) is not a break from colonialism but a continuation, intensification, and modernisation of it.

## Critical Assessment: What Cohn's Framework Reveals About DILRMP

### 1. **DILRMP as Investigative Modality**

DILRMP is a contemporary investigative modality (specifically, an enumerative and legal modality):
- **Enumerative**: It counts and classifies every plot of land, every claim to rights, every administrative boundary.
- **Legal**: It codifies property rights into standardised categories (ownership, tenancy, encumbrance).
- **Result**: Land becomes state-legible (known, counted, categorised), enabling extraction through taxation and policy.

### 2. **The Epistemological Violence**

Cohn emphasises the "epistemological violence" of colonial knowledge production: it is not merely that colonisers extracted information but that they converted knowledge from one epistemological framework (contextual, social, relational) to another (standardised, individual, market-rational).

DILRMP commits the same epistemological violence:
- **Indigenous epistemology**: Land is a multifaceted entity with social (kinship, caste), spiritual (ritual, sacred sites), ecological (seasonal use, commons), and economic (livelihood, subsistence) dimensions. Multiple overlapping claims to land can be legitimate simultaneously.
- **DILRMP epistemology**: Land is a standardised, measurable, tradable commodity. A plot has a single owner, fixed boundaries, and quantifiable value. Rights are individual and exclusive.

By converting land from indigenous to state-modernist epistemology, DILRMP enables market transactions but destroys the contextual, relational, and communal dimensions of land-use.

### 3. **Distancing Local Authorities**

Just as colonial law codes reduced reliance on pandits, DILRMP reduces reliance on village councils and customary authorities:
- Before DILRMP: Land disputes were resolved by village councils (panchayats, caste councils), which applied customary practice and had local knowledge and legitimacy.
- After DILRMP: Land disputes are resolved through official courts and administrative appeal systems, applying standardised property law, with evidence based on digital records maintained by distant bureaucrats.

Result: Customary dispute-resolution mechanisms are weakened; state power over land increases.

## Internal Limitations

1. **Macroscale Focus**: Cohn emphasises system-level transformation (how investigative modalities enabled state formation). Less attention to micro-level resistance and appropriation. How did Indians themselves use, adapt, subvert these investigative modalities?

2. **Causality Ambiguity**: It is sometimes unclear whether investigative modalities enabled extraction or whether extraction needs drove the development of modalities. Likely both: causal feedback loop.

3. **Postcolonial Trajectory**: Cohn hints at but doesn't fully develop what happened to these modalities after independence. How did independent India inherit and transform colonial investigative modalities?
