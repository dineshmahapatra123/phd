# One-Eyed State: The Politics of Legibility and Property Taxation

DOI: 10.1017/lap.2020.7

## Core Argument

Sánchez-Talanquer challenges the standard proposition that cadastral legibility is produced mainly by revenue-seeking rulers and resisted by landowners who fear taxation. Landholders may instead demand registration when an official record protects their ownership against redistribution, occupation, or competing claims. They can then reduce the fiscal cost of visibility by influencing cadastral valuation, keeping registered land systematically undervalued.

The resulting state is "one-eyed": it sees and legally recognises selected owners, but does not see property value accurately and may exclude competing claimants. Registration, valuation, taxation, adjudication, and enforcement therefore need not develop together. State capacity can expand in one institutional function while remaining weak or politically captured in another.

For this PhD, the paper's most important contribution is the concept of **selective legibility**. It explains why modernised land records may become more complete as owner-identification systems without becoming correspondingly accurate descriptions of possession, inheritance, tenancy, parcel geometry, land value, or competing claims. Partial modernisation may be a political equilibrium, not merely an unfinished technical project.

## Conceptual Architecture

### Legibility Has Benefits as Well as Costs

The conventional model emphasises the costs of being visible: taxation, regulation, and redistribution. Sánchez-Talanquer adds the benefits. Cadastral inscription can:

- validate an ownership claim;
- supply probative evidence in legal disputes;
- strengthen demands for state enforcement;
- assist eviction or defence against rival claimants;
- convert an uncertain claim into an officially recognised one.

Registration is therefore a strategic choice. Its attractiveness depends on whether legal protection is worth the fiscal exposure.

### The Three-Part Incentive Model

Landholders' willingness to enter cadastral records depends on:

1. **Threat to the property claim**: registration becomes more attractive when reform, expropriation, occupation, or rival claims become credible.
2. **Legal benefit of registration**: the record must have practical value in courts, administration, enforcement, or transactions.
3. **Expected fiscal cost**: valuation and collection must be weak, avoidable, or susceptible to influence.

This model is highly portable. The thesis need not reproduce Colombia's party structure to use it. It can ask whether Odisha landholders update records selectively when mutation, welfare, acquisition, inheritance, or litigation changes the value of official recognition.

### The Cadaster as Adjudication

The article distinguishes cadaster from full title registration, but insists that cadastral entry still has constitutive effects. Attaching a named person to a parcel is not neutral description. It privileges one claim, weakens others, and creates an administratively actionable owner.

This fits closely with [[Christian Lund]]: recognition of a property claim also strengthens the authority of the institution that recognises it. It also extends [[James C. Scott]]: legibility is not only imposed from above; actors may appropriate the state's classificatory machinery for private protection.

### Selective Legibility

Selective legibility operates along two axes:

- **Who is visible**: established owners may be registered while settlers, tenants, informal possessors, women heirs, or customary claimants remain outside the record.
- **What is visible**: ownership may be recorded while value, possession, encumbrances, subdivisions, land use, or competing interests remain inaccurate or invisible.

This is more precise than treating a land-record system as simply legible or illegible. It requires analysis at the level of individual fields, institutional functions, and social groups.

### Disaggregated State Capacity

The article rejects state capacity as a single national attribute. Capacity varies:

- geographically, across municipalities and political territories;
- functionally, across registration, valuation, taxation, courts, and enforcement;
- distributionally, according to which groups benefit from each capacity.

This is directly useful for analysing DILRMP components. Computerisation, map digitisation, textual-spatial linkage, resurvey, mutation integration, ULPIN, valuation, and correction should not be combined into one progress measure.

## Empirical Terrain or Evidence and Examples Used

### Historical Setting

The paper studies Colombia during two Liberal-led agrarian-reform episodes:

- Law 200 of 1936 during the Liberal Republic;
- the 1961 agrarian reform at the beginning of the National Front period.

Both reforms threatened Conservative landholders more intensely because Colombia's partisan conflict structured land disputes, courts, local administration, and fears of redistribution.

### Data

Sánchez-Talanquer assembles municipal-level historical data on:

- properties registered in cadastral records;
- official cadastral land values;
- municipal tax revenues;
- party strength;
- agrarian structure;
- geography, rurality, literacy, and prior state presence.

The empirical strategy compares changes in historically Conservative and Liberal municipalities before and after the two reform episodes.

### Main Findings

The evidence produces a consistent three-part pattern:

1. Property registration increased more in Conservative municipalities after Liberal reforms.
2. Official property values did not increase proportionately.
3. Tax revenues did not rise with the broader cadastral base and sometimes fell relatively.

After the 1936 reform, the estimated increase in registered properties was roughly 80 per cent larger in the average Conservative municipality than in the average Liberal municipality. After the 1961 reform, registration again rose more in Conservative areas, while growth in land-tax revenue per person was approximately 3.5 per cent lower.

The findings are consistent with a strategy of becoming visible as an owner while remaining fiscally undervalued.

### Qualitative Support

The historical discussion supports the statistical pattern by showing:

- cadastral entry had practical importance in establishing and defending property claims;
- land reform increased incentives to assemble documentary evidence;
- local political actors influenced cadastral administration;
- declared and official land values remained substantially below market values;
- courts and legal formalities could be used to obstruct redistribution.

The qualitative evidence makes the mechanism plausible, although it does not directly observe every landholder's decision or every act of institutional capture.

## Theoretical Lineage

### Scott: State Legibility

Scott treats cadastral mapping as a central technology through which states simplify territory for taxation and control. Sánchez-Talanquer accepts the constitutive power of the cadaster but changes the direction of agency. Landholders may seek legibility when it protects their claims.

The synthesis is:

- Scott: the state makes society legible.
- Sánchez-Talanquer: powerful actors help determine what the state sees.

### Levi and Tilly: Extraction and State Building

Ruler-centred approaches expect information systems to expand because governments seek revenue. The Colombian evidence shows that greater information about named ownership need not strengthen extraction. Social actors can build one component of the state while deflecting another.

### Property-Rights and Formalisation Literature

Formalisation theories often treat registration as a general institutional improvement. This paper demonstrates that the benefits of registration may be privately appropriated. Stronger recognition for record-holders can coexist with weak public revenue and greater vulnerability for unrecorded claimants.

### Lund: Recognition and Authority

The cadaster's recognition of an owner is an act of practical adjudication even where it is not legally conclusive title. This is directly relevant to India's presumptive RoR: the record may not finally prove title, but it can still determine whose claim is administratively heard.

### Berry: Negotiated Claims

Berry explains why social claims continue outside formal records. Sánchez-Talanquer explains why powerful claimants may strategically enter those records. Together they show that formalisation does not replace politics; it reorganises the arena in which claims are negotiated.

## Internal Tensions and Silences

### Mechanism Is Stronger Than Direct Proof of Capture

The registration, valuation, and tax patterns fit the theory well, and the historical evidence establishes strong incentives for manipulation. Nevertheless, the quantitative design does not directly observe individual landholders causing undervaluation. Alternative mechanisms could include:

- weaker assessment offices in Conservative municipalities;
- differences in land quality or market development not fully measured;
- uneven collection rather than valuation alone;
- administrative disruption associated with conflict;
- local political bargains not reducible to landholder strategy.

The paper addresses several alternatives, particularly property fragmentation and tax collection, but the mechanism remains an inference from converging evidence.

### Difference-in-Differences Assumptions

The design depends on Conservative and Liberal municipalities having sufficiently comparable counterfactual trends. Historical data limitations make a modern event-study demonstration of pre-trends difficult. Two reform episodes produce similar findings, which strengthens confidence, but do not eliminate all concerns about differential trajectories.

### Partisanship Is a Proxy for Threat

Municipal partisan history captures exposure to Liberal reform, but it may also correlate with agrarian structure, state presence, violence, elite organisation, and regional development. The models include extensive controls and fixed effects, yet the meaning of partisan territory remains institutionally dense.

### Cadaster Is Not the Same as Title

The article carefully notes that a separate registry governed transactions and titles. The cadaster still had important probative and administrative effects. This distinction is critical for India: Bhulekh/RoR, registered deeds, cadastral maps, and civil title must not be collapsed into one object.

### The Marginalised Appear Mainly as Excluded Counterclaims

The article is analytically centred on landholder strategy. Peasant settlers and rival claimants appear primarily as the threat that induces registration or as groups excluded from state recognition. Their own strategies of documentation, possession, litigation, and resistance receive less systematic analysis.

### Valuation Is Central in Colombia but Not the Only Odisha Blind Spot

Odisha's land-record problem is not primarily property-tax undervaluation. The transferable insight is functional unevenness, not the exact fiscal mechanism. For this thesis, the relevant hidden dimensions may include:

- actual possession and cultivation;
- tenancy and sharecropping;
- oral partition and inheritance;
- women's claims;
- parcel subdivision and boundary change;
- land-use conversion;
- encumbrances and litigation;
- commons and government land;
- textual-spatial mismatch.

### Digital Systems Are Outside the Paper

The article studies historical cadastral institutions, not digital databases, GIS, interoperability, or ULPIN. Applying it to DILRMP is a thesis extension. The extension is justified because digitisation can amplify selective recognition, but it must be stated rather than attributed directly to the author.

## Ideas and Questions Sparked

### A Selective-Legibility Matrix for Fieldwork

For every sampled parcel or household, separate whether the state sees:

| Dimension | Visible and current? | Visible but stale? | Absent or contested? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recorded owner | | | |
| Possessor | | | |
| Cultivator/tenant | | | |
| Co-sharers and heirs | | | |
| Parcel geometry | | | |
| Subdivision/partition | | | |
| Land use | | | |
| Encumbrance/dispute | | | |
| Welfare-linked claimant | | | |

This converts selective legibility from an abstract concept into a parcel-level diagnostic.

### Questions for Recorded Owners

- When did you last update the RoR, and what event made updating necessary?
- Which parts of the record did you seek to change?
- Were possession, partition, boundary, and land-use details updated at the same time?
- Did a dispute, welfare application, sale, mortgage, or acquisition trigger the update?
- Was there any advantage in leaving co-sharers, tenants, or informal partitions unrecorded?

### Questions for Officials

- Which fields are easiest to update and which require field verification or adjudication?
- Can textual mutation occur without map correction?
- Which discrepancies persist because no party has an incentive to initiate correction?
- Who benefits from the current entry remaining unchanged?
- Are correction requests concentrated around sale, welfare, acquisition, inheritance, or litigation?

### A Stronger Interpretation of the Record-Reality Gap

The thesis can distinguish:

1. **Passive gap**: delay, missing records, staffing shortages, or technical incapacity.
2. **Social gap**: continuing negotiation over possession, inheritance, tenancy, and belonging.
3. **Selective gap**: actors strategically update dimensions that protect their interests while leaving other dimensions invisible.

This three-part typology integrates Wadhwa, Berry, and Sánchez-Talanquer.

### A Caution for DILRMP Evaluation

Coverage indicators can reward the one-eyed state. A database may count a parcel as digitised because a named holder and polygon exist, even when the record is stale, the map is obsolete, possession differs, co-sharers are missing, or correction is inaccessible.

The evaluative question should therefore be:

> Which property relations have become more visible, for whose benefit, and with what corresponding obligations or exclusions?

## Overall Assessment

This is a high-value theoretical and empirical article for the thesis. Its strongest contribution is not the Colombia-specific finding that Conservative municipalities registered more property after Liberal land reforms. It is the demonstration that land-record capacity is divisible and politically appropriable.

The paper gives the thesis a mechanism connecting Scott's legibility, Lund's recognition, Berry's negotiated access, and the observed architecture of DILRMP. It supports a sharper claim than "digitisation may reproduce old errors": modernisation may selectively strengthen the state's recognition of recorded ownership while leaving possession, secondary claims, spatial accuracy, and correction weak because different forms of visibility distribute benefits and costs differently.

Use the paper centrally in Chapters 1 and 3, comparatively in Chapter 2, and operationally in Chapters 4-6. Do not use Colombia's partisan coefficients as evidence about Odisha. Use the mechanism to design disaggregated indicators and field questions.
