## By Thesis Chapter

### Chapter 1 - Conceptual and Analytical Framework

> "The documentation that was involved created and normalized a vast amount of information that formed the basis of their capacity to govern." (p. 3)

Use for: land records as state capacity, not neutral mirrors.

> "There was widespread agreement that this society, like others they were governing, could be known and represented as a series of facts." (p. 4)

Use for: critique of the assumption that social land relations can be reduced to administratively stable facts.

> "In coming to India, they unknowingly and unwittingly invaded and conquered not only a territory but an epistemological space as well." (p. 4)

Use for: broad framing only; must be followed by land/revenue evidence.

> "An investigative modality includes the definition of a body of information that is needed, the procedures by which appropriate knowledge is gathered, its ordering and classification..." (p. 5)

Use for: defining DILRMP as a contemporary investigative modality.

### Chapter 2 - History and Evolution of Land Records

> "Starting in the 1770s in Bengal, the British began to investigate, through what they called 'enquiries,' a list of specific questions to which they sought answers about how revenue was assessed and collected." (p. 5)

Use for: Bengal revenue enquiries as the origin of formal land-record knowledge production.

> "Out of this grew the most extensive and continuous administrative activity of the British, which they termed the land-settlement process." (p. 5)

Use for: land settlement as the central colonial administrative activity.

> "Entailed in this enterprise was the collection of 'customs and local histories,' which in the British discourse related to land tenure." (p. 5)

Use for: settlement reports as documents that translated local tenure into British administrative discourse.

> "The survey as an investigative modality encompasses a wide range of practices, from the mapping of India... to the most minute measuring of a peasant's fields." (p. 7)

Use for: cadastral survey, parcel measurement, and Gunter's chain discussion.

> "The result was the vast official documentation project... an imaginary grid on which the government could locate any site in India." (p. 7)

Use for: cadastral grid, Bhunaksha, GIS, ULPIN.

> "...knowledge were transformed into textual forms such as encyclopedias and extensive archives that were deployed by the colonial state in fixing, bounding, and settling India." (p. 8)

Use for: records/maps as fixing and bounding instruments.

### Chapter 2.4 - Legal and Institutional Infrastructure

> "The courts established and protected property rights and were the instrument for enforcing payment of the 'king's share of the revenue'." (p. 59)

Use for: property rights as tied to revenue enforcement.

> "The British in India initially tried to find who 'owned' the land, so that person could be made responsible for payment of revenue." (p. 59)

Use for: ownership identification as revenue liability, not conclusive title.

> "Forms of knowledge that would enable the foreign rulers to frame regulations... had to be acquired or created." (p. 59)

Use for: record categories as acquired and created knowledge.

> "The collector would have mixed executive and judicial powers in a defined area, a 'district'..." (p. 60)

Use for: genealogy of district/revenue administration.

> "The best indicator of efficiency of the administration was its capacity to collect 100 percent of the assessed revenues." (p. 61)

Use for: colonial efficiency as revenue collection.

> "If payment were made... by an agriculturalist to a superior... these payments were 'rent,' the receiver was a 'landlord,' and the payer was a 'tenant'." (pp. 61-62)

Use for: creation of landlord/tenant categories through administrative classification.

> "A fixed body of knowledge that could be objectified into Hindu and Muslim law had to be found." (p. 69)

Use for: codification as objectification.

> "The object was to find and fix a Hindu civil law... concerned with... the ownership and transmission of property." (p. 71)

Use for: inheritance, partition, mutation, property transmission.

> "...the chain of interpretations of precedents by the English judges... became enshrined as Hindu law..." (pp. 74-75)

Use for: colonial transformation of Indian legal categories.

> "What had started with Warren Hastings and Sir William Jones as a search for the 'ancient Indian constitution' ended up... with English law as the law of India." (p. 75)

Use for: legal transformation and postcolonial inheritance.

### Chapter 3 - Political Economy of Modernisation

> "Other modalities, such as the survey and the census, were more highly defined and clearly related to administrative questions." (p. 5)

Use for: survey/census as administrative technologies.

> "A number was, for the British, a particular form of certainty to be held on to in a strange world." (p. 8)

Use for: quantification and digitisation as certainty-producing practices.

> "It is my hypothesis that what was entailed in the construction of the census operations was the creation of social categories by which India was ordered for administrative purposes." (p. 8)

Use for: RoR categories as administrative ordering; use as analogy, not direct land evidence.

### Chapter 4 - Evolution of Land Rights and Land Records in Odisha

Use for: Bengal-Odisha revenue genealogy where applicable.

> "Hastings had invented the emblematic figure of British imperialism... the man on the spot who knew 'the natives,' who was to represent the forces of 'law and order.'" (p. 60)

> "Hastings encouraged a group of younger servants... to study the 'classical' languages of India... aimed a creating a body of knowledge that could be utilized in the effective control of Indian society." (p. 61)

### Chapter 5 - Village Studies

> "If the British could not master the details of the revenue system, they would be dependent on those 'subtle natives,' who could 'perplex' them at every turn." (p. 59)

> "At every step they came up against quasi feudal rights and obligations which defied any interpretation in familiar Western terms." (p. 59)

Use for: why local tenure resists clean record categories.

> "...tradition recorded only in memory and customs embedded in a variety of local usages wielded an authority equal to that of any written code." (p. 59)

Use for: oral partitions, customary possession, local memory.

### Chapter 6 - Parcel-Level Findings
