## Core Argument

Ghosh & Deodhar (2025) trace India’s land governance from ancient and medieval regimes through Mughal and British rule to contemporary reforms, arguing that the colonial doctrine of eminent domain and extractive land policies (Permanent Settlement, cadastral mapping, and the Land Acquisition Act 1894) created long-run dispossession, institutional inequality, and recurring land struggles. Modern policy reforms (titling, DILRMP) inherit this legacy: technical fixes without legal and distributive remedies risk reproducing dispossession.

## Key Concepts

- Eminent domain as legal rationale for dispossession
- Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, Mahalwari: institutional substitutes for extraction
- Cadastral surveys and mapping as technologies of legibility
- Presumptive vs conclusive titling; Torrens principles as reform agenda
- Political economy: elite capture, indemnity failures, and dispossession

## Evidence & Case Material

- Historical narrative from ancient sources (Arthashastra, Dharmashastras) through Mughal revenue regimes to British reforms (Permanent Settlement, Great Trigonometrical Survey, cadastral mapping).
- Colonial drivers: opium, tea, and plantation economies as motives for coercive acquisition.
- Legal genealogy: Bengal Regulation I (1824), LAA 1894 and subsequent practices of undervalued compensation and exclusion of customary rights.
- Modern audits and pilots (Tamil Nadu CAG findings, AP/Telangana blockchain pilots) as illustrations of continuity and contingent technology adoption.

## Policy Implications

- Digitisation and DLT pilots must be sequenced with legal reforms (titling, indemnity), grievance redress, and corrective surveys to avoid legitimising erroneous records.
- Recognise and protect customary and collective rights (tribal commons, jamma lands) in any titling/digitalisation programme.
- Audit and correction workflows must precede immutability guarantees (DLT) to avoid entrenching errors.

## Critique & Limitations

- The paper is wide-ranging historically; empirical claims about compensation valuation and dispossession would benefit from systematic quantitative backing (regional case panels).
- Future versions should explicitly map historical land dispossession to present-day distributional outcomes (Gini, landholding concentration) with data.

## Thesis Relevance

- Essential source for chapters on colonial legacies, land acquisition law, and the political economy of titling. Use this as primary historical framing when connecting LAA (1894) to DILRMP and present-day exclusionary outcomes.
