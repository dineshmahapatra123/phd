# Land Reforms to Land Titling — PHD-LENS Review

Pradeep Nayak | SAGE / Indian Institute of Advanced Study | 2021

## Core Argument

Nayak argues that India's shift from a presumptive Record of Rights (RoR) to a conclusive, Torrens-style land titling regime (via CLR → NLRMP → DILRMP) is a political–economic reorientation: technocratic formalisation is serving market-making and elite interests while sidelining redistributive land reform. The book combines legal history, comparative titling failures, and detailed policy analysis to show that titling, implemented without attention to layered tenure, tends to dispossess secondary-rights holders and intensify land inequality.

## Conceptual Architecture

- Presumptive vs conclusive title (central distinction)
- Torrens system principles: mirror, curtain, and indemnity (critically examined)
- Bundle-of-rights framing: how titling severs overlapping social claims
- Scott's legibility and Polanyi's fictitious commodity as meta-theoretical frames
- PHD_LENS fit: direct engagement with institutional, legal, and political-economy frameworks relevant to Chapters 1–4.

## Empirical Terrain / Evidence Used

- Primary sources: government policy documents (NLRMP/DILRMP guidelines, Five-Year Plans, NITI Aayog), legislative drafts (Draft Land Titling Bill 2011), and court judgements.
- Comparative case studies: Thailand, Philippines, Indonesia, Nicaragua, Peru, various African cases, China as a counterfactual.
- India-focused empirical tracing across states and program reports; discussion of tribal, urban, and welfare-linked (PM-Kisan) consequences.

## Theoretical Lineage

- Institutional economics / NIE (Deininger & Feder; Demsetz)
- Critical political economy (Polanyi; Harvey)
- Commons scholarship (Ostrom)
- Colonial land-administration history (Permanent Settlement / Ryotwari / Mahalwari)

## Key Passages (selected, relevance-weighted)

"The National Land Records Modernization Programme…seeks to achieve a transition through four cardinal principles: a single-window mechanism, the mirror principle, the curtain principle, and title insurance." (Introduction)

"The shift from presumptive records to conclusive title is being done in an apolitical way — reform by stealth — without public debate." (Preface/Introduction)

"The bundle of rights in India is distributed across multiple parties; any conclusive titling that vests rights in a single registered holder inevitably severs this bundle, dispossessing secondary interest holders." (Ch.1/Ch.5 synthesis)

"No empirical study by independent scholars has found positive evidence that land titling programmes have provided land access to the poor and vulnerable sections of society." (Ch.2 conclusion)

"The NLRMP/DILRMP is technically ambitious but lacks social and historical knowledge required for equitable insertion into Indian land relations." (Conclusion)

## Internal Tensions and Silences

- Limited measurement: while the book marshals comparative and documentary evidence, it lacks systematic micro-level panel data from India demonstrating causal pathways from digitisation to dispossession.
- Post-2015 tech-politics: treatment of very recent digital innovations (ULPIN, Bhulekh integration, Smart Cities interactions) is present but could be updated with implementation audits.
- State variation: Kerala/West Bengal successes in redistributive reform get less comparative attention despite being salient counter-cases.
