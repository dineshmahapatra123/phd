## By Thesis Chapter

### Chapter 1 - Conceptual Framework

#### QB-01 - Colonialism as cultural control
- Book location: Foreword, pp. ix-x.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 1.1; Ch. 1.2.
- Relevant concept: colonial knowledge-power.
- Passage substance: Dirks frames colonialism as more than conquest by arms, capital, or administration. Colonial rule was sustained by cultural technologies that produced categories of rule and transformed Indian society into an object of colonial knowledge.
- How to use: One framing paragraph in the introduction/literature review. Use Cohn to establish that colonial land administration should be read as a knowledge system, not only a fiscal system.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial land records were part of a wider colonial apparatus that made people, territory, and rights administratively knowable.

#### QB-02 - Knowledge as both cause and product of conquest
- Book location: Foreword, pp. ix-x.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 1.1; Ch. 2.2.2.
- Relevant concept: knowledge-power; historical institutionalism.
- Passage substance: Colonial knowledge enabled conquest, but conquest also generated new knowledge practices. The relationship is reciprocal, not sequential.
- How to use: Helps avoid a simplistic account where the British first conquered and then documented. In land administration, conquest and settlement documentation developed together.
- Thesis sentence: In colonial India, territorial control and knowledge production were mutually reinforcing.

#### QB-03 - Cohn before Foucault/Said
- Book location: Foreword, pp. ix-x.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 1.2.
- Relevant concept: intellectual lineage.
- Passage substance: Dirks places Cohn before the later theoretical vocabulary of Foucault and Said, showing that Cohn's South Asian archival work anticipated knowledge-power and Orientalism debates.
- How to use: Use this when justifying Cohn as a serious theoretical source, not a loose postcolonial flourish.
- Thesis sentence: Cohn provides an India-specific historical anthropology of colonial knowledge that complements, and in some ways anticipates, later theories of knowledge and power.

#### QB-04 - Colonial state as documentation project
- Book location: Foreword, pp. xi-xiv.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 1.1; Ch. 2.1; Ch. 3.4.
- Relevant concept: documentation project.
- Passage substance: Colonial regulation depended on reports, investigations, commissions, data, certification, and representation. The colonial state made subjects governable by classifying and naturalising identities.
- How to use: This is the conceptual bridge to Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, ULPIN, e-mutation, and DILRMP.
- Thesis sentence: DILRMP can be analysed as a digital phase of an older documentation project.

#### QB-05 - Modern state power shifts from spectacle to records
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 3-4.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 1.1; Ch. 2.1.
- Relevant concept: officialising procedures.
- Passage substance: Cohn contrasts premodern displays of power with modern state's administrative power: classification of space, property records, population counting, birth/death registration, language standardisation, public education, and state histories.
- How to use: Introduce land records as part of modern state capacity. This supports the claim that records are instruments of governance, not merely passive archives.
- Thesis sentence: The modern state makes power durable through records, classifications, and standardised categories.

#### QB-06 - Facts as the basis of administrative power
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 4-5.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 1.1; Ch. 3.4.
- Relevant concept: state facts; legibility.
- Passage substance: The British assumed Indian society could be represented as facts, and that efficient administrative use of facts would produce governing power.
- How to use: This is a key bridge to the DILRMP assumption that cleaner digital data will improve governance.
- Thesis sentence: Land records modernisation inherits the assumption that administrative power improves when facts become more complete, standardised, and usable.

#### QB-07 - Epistemological conquest
- Book location: Chapter 1, p. 5.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 1.1; Ch. 2.2.2.
- Relevant concept: epistemological space.
- Passage substance: Cohn argues that the British entered an Indian epistemological world whose categories did not match their own, but they believed translation could make the unfamiliar knowable.
- How to use: This can frame the mismatch between living land relations and official revenue/legal categories.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial land administration translated Indian land relations into British administrative categories, thereby changing what could count as a recognisable right.

### Chapter 2 - History and Evolution of Land Records

#### QB-08 - Investigative modalities as administrative knowledge practices
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 5-6.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.2.2; Ch. 2.3.2.
- Relevant concept: investigative modalities.
- Passage substance: Cohn defines investigative modalities as organised procedures for gathering, classifying, and converting information into reports, legal codes, gazetteers, statistical returns, histories, maps, and official proceedings.
- How to use: Treat survey and settlement as one such modality rather than a purely technical activity.
- Thesis sentence: Survey and settlement operations were investigative modalities that converted local tenure into state-readable records.

#### QB-09 - Historiographic modality and land-settlement process
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 5-6.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.2.2; Ch. 2.3.2.
- Relevant concept: settlement reports; colonial land administration.
- Passage substance: From the 1770s in Bengal, British enquiries into revenue assessment and collection expanded into the land-settlement process, producing district-level settlement reports based on customs and local histories.
- How to use: Directly relevant to the genealogy of RoR, settlement reports, and district revenue records.
- Thesis sentence: The land-settlement process began as an enquiry into revenue but became a durable system for recording tenure, custom, and administrative geography.

#### QB-10 - Custom becomes land-tenure evidence
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 5-6.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.3.2; Ch. 5.5; Ch. 5.7.
- Relevant concept: custom; record-ground gap.
- Passage substance: Cohn notes that customs and local histories were collected as part of settlement, but once inserted into British discourse they were reframed as land-tenure evidence.
- How to use: Useful for explaining why local practices become distorted when converted into official categories.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial settlement did not merely preserve custom; it extracted custom into administratively usable land-tenure evidence.

#### QB-11 - Survey modality: mapping, measuring, supervising, valuing
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 7-8.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.1; Ch. 2.2.2; Ch. 2.3.2; Ch. 6.
- Relevant concept: cadastral survey; Bhunaksha.
- Passage substance: Survey meant examining, measuring boundaries, supervising places/persons, assessing value, mapping territory, collecting natural/social data, and measuring individual fields.
- How to use: Use this to define survey as broader than map-making. It includes valuation, supervision, and classification.
- Thesis sentence: The cadastral map is only one output of a wider survey modality that made land measurable, taxable, and administratively visible.

#### QB-12 - 1765 Bengal survey as origin point
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 7-8.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.2.2; Ch. 2.3.2.
- Relevant concept: Bengal; colonial survey origin.
- Passage substance: Cohn dates systematic survey in British India to Clive's assignment of James Rennell to survey the newly acquired Bengal territories in 1765.
- How to use: Good historical anchor for Bengal Presidency and, by extension, parts of Odisha's later administrative genealogy.
- Thesis sentence: The genealogy of Indian land records begins with the early Bengal survey-revenue complex after the Company's acquisition of territorial power.

#### QB-13 - Survey of India and the imaginary grid
- Book location: Chapter 1, p. 8.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.2.2; Ch. 4.3; Ch. 6.
- Relevant concept: spatial legibility; cadastral grid.
- Passage substance: The Survey of India eventually covered the territory with a grid through which the government could locate sites and organise information.
- How to use: Relate to cadastral maps, Bhunaksha, ULPIN, and parcel-level location systems.
- Thesis sentence: The digital parcel grid is a contemporary continuation of the spatial legibility first institutionalised through colonial survey.

#### QB-14 - Surveys classified more than land
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 7-8.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.2.2; Ch. 4.1.
- Relevant concept: totalising survey.
- Passage substance: Cohn emphasises that new territorial surveys described zoology, geology, botany, ethnography, economic products, history, and sociology, not just boundaries.
- How to use: Shows why settlement operations were knowledge regimes, not mere boundary exercises.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial surveys bundled spatial measurement with social, economic, and historical classification.

#### QB-15 - Enumerative modality and census classification
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 8-9.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.2.2; Ch. 5.4; Ch. 5.8.
- Relevant concept: enumeration; caste; welfare categories.
- Passage substance: The census counted age, occupation, caste, religion, literacy, birthplace, and residence, while census reports narrated caste, religion, fertility, household organisation, and economy.
- How to use: Use as background to land records as enumerative instruments that classify rights-holders and beneficiaries.
- Thesis sentence: Enumeration converts social identities into state categories that later shape entitlement, exclusion, and administrative treatment.

#### QB-16 - Census as objectification of social difference
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 8-9.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 5.4; Ch. 5.8.
- Relevant concept: caste and administrative identity.
- Passage substance: Cohn argues the census objectified and hardened social, cultural, and linguistic differences while presenting them as sociological facts.
- How to use: Extend carefully to caste-linked landholding patterns and welfare eligibility categories.
- Thesis sentence: Administrative enumeration can harden social categories that are then treated as neutral facts by governance systems.

#### QB-17 - Surveillance modality and unsettled groups
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 10-11.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 5.5.2; Ch. 5.7; Ch. 5.8.
- Relevant concept: settled civil society; encroacher/criminal categories.
- Passage substance: Groups that appeared mobile or outside settled order were investigated, classified, and sometimes stigmatised as criminal tribes/castes.
- How to use: Use cautiously. This can support discussion of how state categories marginalise groups outside settled/documented tenure, but it is not directly about land records.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial classification treated mobility and non-settled claims as administrative problems requiring surveillance.

#### QB-18 - Postcolonial social science as continuation of classification
- Book location: Chapter 1, pp. 11-15.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 1.2; Ch. 3.1.
- Relevant concept: development knowledge; modernisation theory.
- Passage substance: Cohn extends the argument beyond colonial India to anthropology, area studies, HRAF, development policy, and modernisation paradigms that classify societies along a tradition-to-modernity trajectory.
- How to use: Use only if Chapter 3 discusses development orthodoxy and technocratic policy.
- Thesis sentence: Development policy often inherits older classificatory habits by treating societies as objects to be made legible, comparable, and modern.

### Chapter 3 - Political Economy of Land Records Modernisation

- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.4; Ch. 3.4; Ch. 5.7.2.
- Relevant concept: administrative translation; intermediaries.
- Passage substance: Cohn shows that language knowledge was necessary to issue commands, collect information, assess taxes, maintain order, and classify groups.
- How to use: Useful for an analogy to digital literacy and the new language of e-governance, but keep it secondary.
- Thesis sentence: Every administrative system depends on translation between local knowledge and official command languages.

#### QB-20 - Indian intermediaries as knowledge producers

- How to use: Strong for fieldwork interpretation: RI, Amin, data-entry operator, and local brokers translate ground reality into records.
- Thesis sentence: Official land knowledge is never purely centralised; it depends on intermediaries who mediate between local practice and state form.

#### QB-21 - Discursive formation of administrative language

- Relevant concept: language of command; digital language of command.
- Passage substance: Grammars, dictionaries, translations, and teaching aids converted Indian knowledge into European objects and administrative tools.
- How to use: Secondary support for showing how Bhulekh/Bhunaksha interfaces and data standards may similarly reshape what counts as valid knowledge.

#### QB-22 - Fort William and knowledge standardisation

- Relevant concept: institutional production of knowledge.

- How to use: Optional. Use only to show how knowledge standardisation requires institutions, funding, and official curricula.
- Thesis sentence: The colonial state invested in knowledge infrastructure because administrative rule required standardised categories and trained personnel.

#### QB-23 - British distrust of Indian interpretation

- Lens mapping: Ch. 3.4; Ch. 5.7.
- Relevant concept: disintermediation; technocratic cleansing.
- Passage substance: British officials often relied on Indian scholars but distrusted their interpretive authority, seeking direct access to supposedly pure texts or facts.
- How to use: This is useful for interpreting the modern desire to bypass local intermediaries through digitisation, while still depending on them in practice.
- Thesis sentence: Technocratic systems often try to eliminate intermediaries while reproducing dependence on them at the point of data entry and correction.

### Chapter 4 - Odisha Land Tenure History and Administration

#### QB-24 - Company state after Plassey and Dewani
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 57-59.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.3.2; Ch. 4.1.
- Relevant concept: East India Company as revenue state.
- Passage substance: Cohn describes the East India Company becoming a territorial power with state functions: war, taxation, justice, and revenue collection after Plassey and the Dewani grant.
- How to use: Establish why land records in colonial India emerged inside a revenue state rather than a rights-protection state.
- Thesis sentence: The Indian land-record apparatus originated in a Company state whose central problem was revenue extraction.

#### QB-25 - Revenue collection and property rights are linked
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 59-60.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.3.2; Ch. 2.4; Ch. 4.1.
- Relevant concept: revenue liability; property right.
- Passage substance: Cohn explains that British tax assessment was linked to courts and property rights: the state needed to know who held land so that revenue could be enforced.
- How to use: Key passage for the argument that revenue records are not the same as title, even though revenue administration pushed toward property identification.
- Thesis sentence: The search for revenue liability encouraged the colonial state to transform complex tenure into legally recognisable property categories.

#### QB-26 - The search for who owned the land
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 59-60.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.3.2; Ch. 4.1; Ch. 5.5.
- Relevant concept: ownership vs. possession.
- Passage substance: Cohn shows that British officials sought the person who owned the land, but encountered quasi-feudal rights, obligations, Persian accounts, memory-based tradition, and local usages.
- How to use: Directly supports RQ4: record-ground mismatch has roots in the mismatch between British ownership categories and layered local rights.
- Thesis sentence: The colonial search for a single owner simplified layered relations of possession, use, obligation, and revenue responsibility.

#### QB-27 - Famine, revenue failure, and administrative knowledge
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 59-60.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.3.2; Ch. 4.1.
- Relevant concept: revenue crisis; state capacity.
- Passage substance: Cohn links early British revenue mismanagement in Bengal to famine, uncultivated land, disorder, and the need to create reliable information.
- How to use: Use as context for why revenue administration sought more systematic knowledge.
- Thesis sentence: Information systems for land emerged partly from administrative crisis and fiscal failure.

#### QB-28 - Hastings and the Collector
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 60-62.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.4; Ch. 4.2.
- Relevant concept: Collector; district.
- Passage substance: Hastings's plan created the Collector as a district officer combining executive and judicial authority over revenue and civil litigation.
- How to use: Crucial for tracing the genealogy of district revenue administration to Tehsildar, RI, Amin, and Collectorate structures.
- Thesis sentence: The Collector institutionalised the fusion of revenue, executive, and judicial authority that still shadows Indian land administration.

#### QB-29 - Dewani and Faujdari courts
- Book location: Chapter 3, p. 62.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.4; Ch. 4.2.
- Relevant concept: legal infrastructure.
- Passage substance: Cohn describes Hastings's dual court structure: revenue/civil litigation in Dewani courts and criminal/order matters in Faujdari courts, with Hindu and Muslim law officers advising.
- How to use: Use for the historical roots of revenue-legal overlap.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial land administration emerged within an institutional design where revenue collection and civil adjudication were closely intertwined.

#### QB-30 - Despotic model and property insecurity
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 62-65.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.3.2; Ch. 4.1.
- Relevant concept: Oriental despotism; property security.
- Passage substance: British writers used a despotic model of India that associated arbitrary rule with insecure property and absence of stable law.
- How to use: This helps explain why the British imagined codification and secure property as necessary reforms.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial property reform was justified through a theory that Indian political order lacked stable legal protection for property.

#### QB-31 - Classical/theocratic model of Indian law
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 65-68.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.4; Ch. 4.1.
- Relevant concept: Hindu law; textualisation.
- Passage substance: Hastings and Orientalist officials treated Sanskrit and Persian legal traditions as sources for governing Indians according to supposedly indigenous law.
- How to use: Use for legal infrastructure, especially inheritance and succession rules affecting land.
- Thesis sentence: The colonial state claimed to preserve Indian law but preserved it through a textual and court-administered form that altered its institutional character.

#### QB-32 - Gentoo Code as colonial compilation
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 66-68.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.4; Ch. 5.5.1.
- Relevant concept: codification; inheritance; property succession.
- Passage substance: Cohn's account of Halhed's code shows that British officials assembled legal topics through pandits under colonial administrative priorities, especially those relevant to courts and property.
- How to use: Useful for tracing how inheritance/succession became record-relevant and litigation-prone.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial codification filtered Indian legal traditions through the administrative needs of courts, inheritance, and property transmission.

#### QB-33 - Jones and direct access to pure law
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 68-70.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.4; Ch. 3.4.
- Relevant concept: purification; disintermediation.
- Passage substance: Jones wanted British judges to check native interpreters by creating a digest that would give direct access to Hindu and Muslim legal principles.
- How to use: Strong analogy for DILRMP's desire for a clean official record independent of local mediation.
- Thesis sentence: The colonial search for pure legal knowledge parallels the modern search for clean land data.

#### QB-34 - Digest organised by European legal method
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 69-70.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.4; Ch. 4.1.
- Relevant concept: classification; legal ordering.
- Passage substance: Jones proposed arranging Indian legal material into books, chapters, sections, indexed and translated for British judicial use.
- How to use: Shows how administrative form changes substantive legal knowledge.
- Thesis sentence: Codification transformed legal knowledge by making it searchable, hierarchical, and usable within British courts.

#### QB-35 - Whig property focus: contracts and succession
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 70-72.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.4; Ch. 5.5.1.
- Relevant concept: property; inheritance; succession.
- Passage substance: Cohn emphasises that the digest focused on civil-law questions that mattered to British political/legal concerns: contracts, inheritance, succession, ownership, and transmission of property.
- How to use: Very strong for multi-owner khata, inheritance, partition, and why property succession became central to land administration.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial legal codification privileged property transmission and inheritance because these issues were central to revenue, courts, and political order.

#### QB-36 - Common law flexibility vs. fixed Hindu law
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 71-73.
- Lens mapping: Ch. 2.4; Ch. 4.1; Ch. 5.5.
- Relevant concept: legal rigidity; custom.
- Passage substance: Cohn shows the contradiction: English common law was historically flexible, but British officials imagined Hindu usages as fixed from time immemorial.
- How to use: Powerful for explaining why living agrarian practices get treated as deviations from records.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial law froze Indian custom even while British law at home remained flexible and precedent-based.

#### QB-37 - Colebrooke and schools of Hindu law
- Book location: Chapter 3, pp. 72-74.

- How to use: Relevant for inheritance and partition complexities, especially where personal law shapes land records.
- Thesis sentence: Colonial legal scholarship converted plural interpretive traditions into formal schools that could be administered by courts.

#### QB-38 - Hindu law becomes English case law

- Passage substance: Cohn concludes that the effort to recover Indian law ended with Hindu law being transformed into English-style case law through court decisions and precedent.
- How to use: One of the most important passages for legal infrastructure.
- Thesis sentence: The colonial state's attempt to preserve indigenous law produced a hybrid legal order structured by English courts and precedent.

## Best Working Thesis Paragraph

Cohn's value for this PhD is to show that colonial land administration was not merely a fiscal apparatus but a knowledge apparatus. Through survey, settlement, enumeration, legal codification, and district reporting, the colonial state converted land, custom, and social relations into official facts. Contemporary land records modernisation inherits this architecture: Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, e-mutation, and ULPIN pursue cleaner digital legibility, but they may reproduce the older disjuncture between administrative categories and lived possession.
