## Core Argument

Feder & Feeny (1991) argue that the structure and security of land rights critically shape incentives for investment, intensity of cultivation, land prices, and the operation of credit markets; secure, enforceable rights reduce uncertainty, improve access to credit, and raise land values, but provision of such rights is costly and context-dependent.

## Key Concepts

- Property rights as institutional bundles (exclusivity, transferability, inheritability, enforceability)
- Four tenure regimes: open access, communal, private, state
- Security of rights, transaction costs, asymmetric information
- Interaction between rights security and credit markets

## Formal Model (brief)

A simple model links the probability of tenure loss to expected returns on investments and hence to optimal investment and land prices. Greater tenure security raises investment and land values, amplifying credit availability when land is acceptable collateral.

## Empirical Evidence

The paper cites historical and empirical evidence (Thailand case) showing titling and cadastral surveys accompany commercialization and increases in land value; Thai cadastral reforms and titling in late 19th–20th century increased documentation and market transactions in core agricultural regions.

## Policy Implications

- Titling/cadastral surveys can promote investment and credit, but benefits depend on local enforcement capacity and institutional congruence with customary norms.
- Public infrastructure (courts, registries, surveying) is complementary to secure rights; without it, formal titles may be nominal.
- Where communal or customary arrangements perform well, blanket titling may be inefficient or socially disruptive.

## Critique & Limitations

- Distributional effects underplayed: elites who control reform may capture gains (noted in Thai example).
- Costs of setting up reliable registries and enforcement are underemphasized; informal enforcement and private protection can substitute where public goods are weak.
- Less attention to multi-layered overlapping claims (gender, lineage, customary use), which complicate simple titling.

## Thesis Relevance

Feder & Feeny provide formal and empirical support for claims that security of rights affects investment and credit—useful for chapters on institutional drivers of land markets, and for critiquing technocratic titling programmes (e.g., DILRMP) that ignore enforcement and customary pluralism.
