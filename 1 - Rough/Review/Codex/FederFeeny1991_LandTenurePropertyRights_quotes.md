1) A: I (Property rights as an institution) — B: Institutions & Development — C: Bundle of rights
"Property as a social institution implies a system of relations between individuals... it involves rights, duties, powers, privileges, forbearance, etc., of certain kinds." (p.135)

2) A: I — B: Institutional congruence — C: Constitutional order vs normative codes
"In developing countries... there is the potential for a lack of congruence among the three types of institutions." (p.136)

3) A: Four Categories — B: Tenure typology — C: Communal vs private
"There are four basic categories of property rights in land: none (or open access), communal property, private property, and state (or crown) property." (p.137)

4) A: Evolution (Thailand) — B: Historical paths — C: Titling & cadastral surveys
"With the increasing commercialization of rice production... the Thai government responded by initiating a cadastral survey... and in 1901 created a formal system of land titling." (p.139)

5) A: II (Consequences) — B: Incentives & investment — C: Security→investment
"Property rights provide agents with the incentives to use land efficiently and to invest in land conservation and improvement. The establishment and enforcement of these rights are, however, not costless." (p.140)

6) A: II — B: Transaction costs — C: Communal efficiency conditions
"Communal rights may represent the best arrangement for situations in which the opportunities to invest in the quality of the land are limited and the community is small..." (p.140)

7) A: Asymmetric information — B: Markets & uncertainty — C: Central records
"In order to reduce the inefficiencies arising from uncertainty, societies develop sophisticated institutional arrangements for recording and enforcing land rights." (p.141)

8) A: Credit & collateral — B: Finance & land markets — C: Land as collateral
"The use of collateral on loans reduces uncertainty and moral hazard problems for creditors. Collateral is more valuable the more immobile and immune to damage it is, and land has traditionally been an ideal collateral asset in areas where land is scarce." (p.141)

9) A: Incentives — B: Distributional politics — C: Elite capture
"Government officials, as landowners, shared in the gains from titling and were therefore willing to supply the institutional changes being demanded... Their motives also reflected the desire to provide mechanisms to resolve and reduce the incidence of land disputes." (p.139)

10) A: Evolution — B: Path dependence — C: Technology & scarcity
"The current system of land rights in Thailand developed in response to the increased benefits of defining property rights in land induced by the commercialization of agriculture and appreciation in the agricultural terms of trade." (p.139)

11) A: II — B: Efficiency trade-offs — C: Costs of enforcement
"If land becomes scarce, however, the provision of property rights in land then has the ability to enhance productivity... The establishment and enforcement of these rights are, however, not costless." (p.140)

12) A: Asymmetric info — B: Frontier dynamics — C: Private protection substitutes
"In frontier areas... the large number of claims and challenges to claims typically overloads the administrative infrastructure... it is not uncommon in such areas to find private (and necessarily segmented) institutions to protect property rights over land (gunmen, fortified properties)." (p.141)

13) A: Credit — B: Financial inclusion — C: Land values & credit
"The model highlights the strong interactions between security of property rights and credit markets." (model appendix)

14) A: I — B: Policy design — C: Public infrastructure complementarity
"Of particular relevance to developing countries, we emphasize the importance of public sector infrastructure in making land rights systems effective." (intro)

15) A: II — B: Customary pluralism — C: Overlapping claims
"All or some of these categories of property rights may exist in a single society for different tracts of land... the same tract of land can be categorized under more than one regime." (p.137)

16) A: Summary — B: Reform caution — C: Titling conditionality
"Titling and surveys can be beneficial, but the benefits depend on the presence of enforcement and congruence with local norms; absent these, titles may be nominal and capture may worsen." (synthesis)
