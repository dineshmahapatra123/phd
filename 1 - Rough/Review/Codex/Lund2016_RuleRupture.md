# Rule and Rupture: State Formation through the Production of Property and Citizenship
**Author(s)**: Christian Lund | **Year**: 2016 | **Type**: Article

---

### 1. Core Argument

Lund argues that political authority is not prior to property and citizenship; authority is produced through the practical recognition of claims to resources and membership. The article's core move is to treat property and citizenship as co-produced with state authority through "contracts of recognition." This is highly relevant to the thesis because it turns land records from passive documents into instruments through which the state recognises, denies, and ranks claims. The governing claim is that "The state is always in the making" (p. 1199).

For the Odisha thesis, the article matters less as a comparative Africa/Indonesia text and more as a conceptual bridge: RoR, cadastral maps, mutation, ULPIN, and welfare eligibility can be read as mechanisms of recognition. The paper strengthens the argument that digital land records do not merely modernise information; they reorganise which claims can become administratively visible.

---

### 2. Conceptual Architecture

**State as process, not object**  
Lund treats the state as an ongoing effect of institutional practice. The point is not to ask whether the state is weak or strong in the abstract, but to ask which institutions successfully recognise and enforce claims. This concept functions as the frame for reading DILRMP as state formation through digital record infrastructure.

**Claims, rights, and authority**  
The article's most important mechanism is that "Claims to rights prompt the exercise of authority" (p. 1201). Rights are not simply distributed by a completed authority; they trigger, test, and constitute authority. This is directly portable to land-record disputes: a mutation application, RoR correction petition, or welfare claim forces the revenue state to recognise or deny a claimant.

**Contracts of recognition**  
Recognition is reciprocal. When a person or group seeks recognition of a land claim, they also recognise the institution as capable of validating it. When the institution successfully validates and enforces the claim, it acquires state quality. In the thesis, this explains why villagers line up before the RI, Amin, Tehsildar, Sub-Registrar, or court: they are not just seeking paperwork; they are seeking state recognition.

**Property as recognised claim**  
Lund defines property as a legitimised claim to something valuable sanctioned by authority. This shifts property away from the narrow idea of absolute ownership. It helps explain why RoR, possession, registered deed, inheritance, tenancy, and social recognition may all represent different layers of property rather than one settled legal truth.

**Citizenship as recognised membership**  
Citizenship is meaningful membership in a political body. In land governance, membership is not only formal national citizenship; it may be local belonging, caste/community status, settlement history, or recognition as a landholding farmer. For Odisha, this is central to PM-Kisan, CM-Kisan, KALIA, and other land-record-mediated benefits.

**Property-citizenship nexus**  
The paper links land rights and political membership. Who can hold property and who counts as a rights-bearing subject are not separate questions. This gives the thesis a language for RoR as both property record and welfare/citizenship gate.

**Rupture**  
Rupture is an open moment when old institutional arrangements are unsettled and the construction of authority becomes visible. Colonialism is Lund's key example, but the concept can also be used carefully for post-independence land reform and digital modernisation. DILRMP is not a violent rupture like colonial settlement, but it is a soft administrative rupture because it changes the infrastructure of recognition.

**Territorialization**  
Lund connects legal control over property with political control over territory. Classification, registration, and mapping do not merely describe space; they organise how space can be governed. This is directly relevant to Bhunaksha, cadastral maps, ULPIN/Bhu-Aadhaar, and the spatial-textual disjuncture in Odisha.

**Jurisdictional competition**  
Authority over land is often split among multiple institutions. Lund's account supports the thesis' focus on the fragmentation of revenue, registration, settlement, and judicial institutions. DILRMP can be read as an attempt to integrate competing jurisdictions, but also as a risk: it may harden one institutional version of reality without resolving plural claims.

### 3. Evidence and Examples Used

This is a theoretical introductory article to a special issue, not a primary empirical study. It uses comparative examples to demonstrate the rights-authority mechanism.

**Colonialism and Domesday Book**  
Lund uses colonialism as the clearest historical rupture in property and subject formation. The Domesday Book example shows how recording land and persons can simultaneously establish property and sovereignty. The key thesis-use is not England itself, but the logic: land records can constitute authority rather than merely reflect ownership.

**Ghana**  
The Ghana example shows how chiefs and the statutory state jointly shaped land allocation, stranger status, land rents, and local citizenship. This is useful only as a theoretical comparator, not as direct empirical evidence for Odisha. It illustrates how statutory and customary institutions can collude or compete in recognising land claims.

**Indonesia**  
The Indonesia example shows fragmented authority over land and livelihood in post-authoritarian conditions. Its thesis relevance is limited but useful for thinking about non-state or para-state recognition of land claims, especially where formal institutions are weak or strategically absent.

**Special issue summaries**  
The later article summaries are less important for the thesis. They show the range of cases where property and citizenship form authority, but they should not be mined deeply unless a later chapter needs comparative language on rupture, post-conflict state formation, or territorialization.

### 4. Theoretical Lineage

Lund is in dialogue with the state-formation, legal pluralism, recognition, property, and citizenship literatures. The central influences include Sally Falk Moore on semi-autonomous social fields, Arendt and Somers on citizenship and the right to rights, Honneth/Taylor/Fraser on recognition, Scott on state visibility, and Sikor and Lund's own property-authority-access tradition.

For the PhD, Lund extends but also corrects the NIE tradition. North and Feder treat the state mainly as a supplier of enforcement and institutional reliability. Lund asks how the authority of the supplier itself is produced through recognition. This distinction matters because Odisha's land-record problem is not simply enforcement failure; it is a problem of whose claims the state can see and validate.

Lund also complements Scott and Cohn. Cohn explains colonial categories and administrative knowledge. Scott explains legibility. Lund explains why recognised claims recursively authorise the recogniser. Together, these three give a strong Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 architecture: colonial knowledge, state legibility, and recognition.

### 6. Internal Tensions and Silences

**The article is theoretically powerful but geographically indirect.** Its strongest examples are not Indian land records or Odisha. It passes the PHD_LENS only when used for a named theoretical purpose: recognition, state formation, jurisdictional competition, and territorialization.

**Recognition risks becoming too capacious.** If every administrative interaction is treated as recognition, the concept may lose precision. For thesis use, it should be limited to cases where a claim is converted into an actionable right or entitlement: RoR entry, mutation, cadastral boundary, welfare eligibility, compensation claim, court order.

**The paper underplays technology.** It does not discuss digital records, databases, ULPIN, Aadhaar, or e-governance. The DILRMP application is a thesis extension, not Lund's own empirical claim.

**Rupture may overstate DILRMP if used loosely.** Colonial conquest is a rupture; DILRMP is a gradual administrative-modernisation programme. It is better to call DILRMP a soft or infrastructural rupture, not equivalent to colonial dispossession.

**Economic development is secondary.** Lund does not test investment, credit, land-market efficiency, or productivity. His contribution to Chapter 3 is political-institutional, not econometric.

Direct. The article explains disjuncture as a recognition gap: some social claims fail to become institutionally recognised rights.

### 8. Ideas and Questions Sparked

**Idea: Treat RoR as a contract of recognition, not merely a record.**  
This advances Ch. 3.3 and Ch. 5 because it explains why being named in Bhulekh matters for welfare, compensation, credit, and local legitimacy.

**Idea: DILRMP is recognition consolidation.**  
This advances Ch. 3.2.2 because integration of RoR, registration, maps, and ULPIN can be analysed as consolidation of institutional recognition across fragmented departments.

**Question for fieldwork: Who is recognised by the village but not by the state?**  
This is central to Ch. 5: tenants, daughters, widows, possessors after oral partition, commons users, and encroachers may have social recognition without RoR recognition.

**Question for fieldwork: Which institution do villagers treat as the final recogniser?**  
This advances Ch. 4.2 because it identifies actual authority: RI, Amin, Tehsildar, Sub-Registrar, court, panchayat, broker, political representative, or local caste/community body.

**Idea: Spatial-textual disjuncture is also recognition disjuncture.**  
This advances Ch. 4.3 because an old cadastral map and an updated RoR may recognise different realities.

**Idea: Welfare eligibility is property-citizenship in practice.**  
This advances Ch. 3.3 because schemes such as PM-Kisan, CM-Kisan, and KALIA convert land-record recognition into state benefit recognition.

**Caution: Do not overuse Ghana/Indonesia empirics.**  
This protects the thesis from weak analogies. Use those cases only for conceptual vocabulary, not for evidence about Odisha.
