# Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge: The British in India

**Author(s)**: Bernard S. Cohn | **Year**: 1996 | **Type**: Book

---

### 1. Core Argument
Cohn argues that the British colonial project was as much an **epistemological conquest** as it was a military or economic one. Through a series of "Investigative Modalities," the British converted the fluid, context-dependent social and property relations of India into a "fixed," legible, and officialized body of knowledge. The central claim is that "The power to define is the power to rule," and that the colonial state was built upon a "Documentation Project" that misread Indian realities through European administrative categories.

> "The British had to learn the languages of India in order to issue commands, to collect information, and to settle the land revenue." (p. 1)

---

### 2. Conceptual Architecture
- **Investigative Modalities**: The procedures by which the colonial state gathered information, ordered it, and transformed it into "knowledge." These include the Historiographic, Survey, Enumerative, Museum, and Surveillance modalities.
- **The Survey Modality**: Not just a map-making exercise, but a "technocratic ritual" that established the grid as the primary way of seeing the land, stripping it of its social and ritual meanings.
- **The Documentation Project**: The massive archival effort to record every aspect of Indian life, which created the "Official Version" of India that eventually replaced the ground reality in the minds of the rulers.
- **Legal Reconstitution**: The process of "fixing" Hindu and Muslim law by extracting "pure" Ur-texts, thereby marginalizing the living, fluid interpretations of local communities.

---

### 3. Empirical Terrain
Cohn draws on extensive archival research from the **Bengal Presidency** (1765–1860). He focuses on:
- The **Permanent Settlement (1793)** and the early revenue experiments of Warren Hastings.
- The **Mackenzie Collection**: The massive survey of South India (Mysore) under Colin Mackenzie.
- The creation of the **Asiatic Society of Bengal** and the work of Orientalist scholars like Sir William Jones and H.T. Colebrooke.
- The **Census of India**: How enumeration transformed "caste" from a local identity into a rigid, pan-Indian administrative category.

---

### 4. Theoretical Lineage
Cohn is a foundational figure in **Historical Anthropology** and **Subaltern Studies**. 
- He extends **Foucault’s** "Knowledge/Power" framework to the colonial context.
- He is in dialogue with (and critical of) the "Whig Interpretation of History," arguing that colonial law was not a gift of "rule of law" but a tool of state-building and dispossession.
- He complicates the **New Institutional Economics (NIE)** view of property by showing that property rights were not "discovered" for efficiency but "constructed" for revenue and control.

---

### 5. Key Passages

> **"The British initially tried to find who 'owned' the land, so that person could be made responsible for payment of revenue. In theory this seemed simple, but in practice... it was fraught with difficulties."** (p. 59)
> **Gloss**: This supports the thesis argument that the "Search for the Proprietor" is the root of the modern record-ground disjuncture.

> **"Englishmen were 'unfit to conduct the collection of revenues and to follow the subtle native through all his arts, to conceal the real value of his country, to perplex and elude the payments.'"** (p. 59)
> **Gloss**: Identifies the "Subtle Native" (Amin/RI) as the gatekeeper of local knowledge against the "Perplexed" state.

> **"The survey modality... converted fluid Indian social and property relations into fixed, 'official' European categories."** (p. 7)
> **Gloss**: Foundational quote for the "Genealogy of the Gap" in Chapter 2.

---

### 6. Internal Tensions and Silences
- **The Bengal Bias**: Cohn’s focus on the Zamindari system of Bengal leaves a silence regarding the **Ryotwari** and **Gadjat** systems of Odisha, where the "Survey Modality" faced different resistance.
- **The "Passive" Native**: While Cohn mentions that Indians were not passive, the book primarily focuses on the *state's* actions. The daily "micro-resistance" at the village level is less explored than the high-level policy of Jones and Hastings.
- **Technocratic Continuity**: Cohn implies a break between pre-colonial and colonial knowledge, but doesn't fully explore if the "Mughal" system he describes was already a "Documentation Project" of its own.
