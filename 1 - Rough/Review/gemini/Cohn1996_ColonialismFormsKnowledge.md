# Literature Review: Colonialism and its Forms of Knowledge (Bernard S. Cohn, 1996)

## Core Argument

Bernard Cohn’s central thesis is that British colonialism in India was fundamentally a **cultural project of control**. Territorial conquest was not merely sustained by military or economic might, but by the creation of an authoritative epistemological space. The British invaded this space through translation, documentation, and classification, constructing a body of "officialized" facts that converted dynamic, complex, and localized Indian social realities into static, legible categories suitable for colonial administration. 

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Defining Core Quote**: 
> *"In coming to India, they unknowingly and unwittingly invaded and conquered not only a territory but an epistemological space as well. The 'facts' of this space did not exactly correspond to those of the invaders. Nevertheless, the British believed they could explore and conquer this space through translation: establishing correspondence could make the unknown and the strange knowable."* (Page 4)

---

## Conceptual Architecture

### 1. Investigative Modalities (Page 5)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"An investigative modality includes the definition of a body of information that is needed, the procedures by which appropriate knowledge is gathered, its ordering and classification, and then how it is transformed into usable forms such as published reports, statistical returns, histories, gazetteers, legal codes, and encyclopedias."*
*   **Significance**: Underpins the land-settlement and census operations as state tools to "fix, bound, and settle" the population.

### 2. The Historiographical Modality (Pages 5–6)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"The historiographic practices and narrative genres of these writers can obviously be subjected to critical analysis, but beyond this they can be seen to have begun the formation of a legitimizing discourse... Out of this grew the most extensive and continuous administrative activity of the British, which they termed the land-settlement process."*
*   **Significance**: In land administration, this is realized as the "land-settlement process" and the production of district settlement reports, converting local customs into histories of land tenure to justify and stabilize tax collection.

### 3. The Survey Modality (Pages 7–8)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"The survey as an investigative modality encompasses a wide range of practices, from the mapping of India to collecting botanical specimens, to the recording of architectural and archaeological sites of historic significance, or the most minute measuring of a peasant's fields."*
*   **Significance**: Represents the systematic exploration and geometric bounding of the landscape, turning local territories into legible, cartographic resources for administrative control.

### 4. The Enumerative Modality (Page 293)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"A number was, for the British, a particular form of certainty to be held on to in a strange world. But when they turned to early attempts to enumerate the population... they found that even the simplest of enumerative projects raised problems of classification."*
*   **Significance**: Unpacks how population counts and censuses reified caste, occupation, and religion into rigid, numerical categories for state planning and control.

### 5. Substantive Theory of Authority (Page 114 / 132)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"Hindus and Muslims operated with an unbounded substantive theory of objects and persons. The body of the ruler was literally his authority, the substance of which could be transmitted in what Europeans thought of as objects. Clothes, weapons, jewels, and paper were the means by which a ruler could transmit the substance of his authority to a chosen companion."*
*   **Significance**: Contrasts the Western "sign-based" contractual model of property with the indigenous concept of physical, relational incorporation.

### 6. Epistemic Translation and Violence (Page 4, Page 71)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: The process of forcing fluid, oral, and contextual indigenous knowledge into rigid, Western textual equivalents to serve the state's legal and revenue apparatus.
*   **Significance**: Strips local practices of their context (e.g., separating religious or ethical duties from property rules) to isolate clean, Whiggish categories of contract and inheritance.

## Empirical / Evidence Terrain

Cohn builds his historical anthropology of the colonial state through several rich archives:
*   **The Bengal Revenue Debates (1760–1790)**: Administrative records, letters from Robert Clive, and Warren Hastings's 1772 Plan for the Administration of Justice, tracking the shift of the East India Company from a commercial operator to a territorial sovereign after the 1765 Dewani of Bengal.
*   **Indological Translational Texts**: Halhed’s *A Code of Gentoo Laws* (1776), Sir William Jones's legal manuscripts, and H. T. Colebrooke's translation of *The Digest of Hindu Law on Contracts and Successions* (1798).
*   **Survey Archives**: The Mysore Survey (1800–1810) files, tracking Colin Mackenzie's use of Telugu Brahman assistants (C. V. Boria and Luchmiah) to collect "local tracts" and temple registers.
*   **The Breast-Cloth Controversy Files (Travancore, 1859)**: Resident General Cullen's reports and missionary records tracking caste riots, state decrees, and the reinterpretation of Queen Victoria's 1858 Proclamation.

## The Quote Bank

### Theme A: The Sovereignty-Revenue Paradox & The "Collector"

#### 1. The Journey into the Unknown (Page 59)
> *"When in 1772 the British attempted to control their Indian subordinates by going into the 'field,' it was... 'a journey into the unknown.' At every step they came up against quasi feudal rights and obligations which defied any interpretation in familiar Western terms. The hieroglyphics of Persian estate accounts baffled them."*
*   **Gloss**: Captures the initial epistemological friction when the British tried to superimpose Western models of property onto the complex, nested agrarian relations of Bengal.

#### 2. The Tax-Property Nexus (Page 59)
> *"In the British cultural system, the capacity to assess taxes was inextricably linked with law. The courts established and protected property rights and were the instrument for enforcing payment of the 'king's share of the revenue.' The British in India initially tried to find who 'owned' the land, so that person could be made responsible for payment of revenue."*
*   **Gloss**: The foundational link: property rights were not created for social welfare, but as an administrative mechanism to secure liability for revenue.

#### 3. Administrative Efficiency as 100% Extraction (Page 61)
> *"Throughout the history of the company and its successor, the imperial Government of India, the best indicator of efficiency of the administration was its capacity to collect 100 percent of the assessed revenues."*
*   **Gloss**: Explains the path-dependent obsession of the revenue bureaucracy with rigid ledger-balancing, which persists in modern DILRMP metrics.

#### 4. The Superimposition of Whig Land Categories (Pages 61–62)
> *"The British logic of administration rested on the capacity to classify actions into prefixed domains. If payment were made in cash or in kind by an agriculturalist to a superior, who appeared to have 'rights' to the land, these payments were 'rent,' the receiver was a 'landlord,' and the payer was a 'tenant.'"*
*   **Gloss**: Demonstrates how British commercial frameworks collapsed multi-layered, nested, and relational agrarian shares into the clean, binary, and adversarial categories of landlord and tenant.

#### 5. Hastings and the Invention of the "Collector" (Page 60)
> *"The crucial actor in Hastings' plan for the better administration of Bengal was to be a British officer designated a 'collector.' The collector would have mixed executive and judicial powers in a defined area, a 'district,' whose boundaries followed preexisting Mughal revenue units termed circars..."*
*   **Gloss**: Identifies the origin of the administrative concentration of power (Tehsildar/Collector) that still governs land administration in postcolonial India.

### Theme B: Legal Reification & Epistemic Violence

#### 6. The Inversion of Common Law (Page 71)
> *"While English jurisprudence of Jones's time sought certainty in the law, through either 'rationality' or an ultimate appeal to ideas of natural law, Hindu jurisprudence sought flexibility through fixed means to interpret what had been revealed to man... English common law... was essentially seen as case law... flexible and... subject to multiple interpretations..."*
*   **Gloss**: Highlights the tragic irony: while English judges enjoyed a flexible common law at home, they assumed Indian law was an unchanging, ancient textual fossil.

#### 7. Bypassing Living Experts to Free Indian Law (Page 69)
> *"Jones wanted to provide the British courts in India, the Crown, and the East India Company with a sure basis on which they could render decisions consonant with a true or pure version of Hindu law. Then the pandits... could not 'deal out Hindoo law as they please...'"*
*   **Gloss**: Exposes the deep colonial mistrust of local, living native scholars, choosing instead to freeze a textual "Ur-text" to establish direct, unmediated bureaucratic control.

#### 8. Freezing the Time-Space of Custom (Page 71)
> *"Unlike the British with their case law... the Hindus therefore lived a timeless existence, which in turn meant that differences in interpretations offered by pandits must have arisen from ignorance or venality."*
*   **Gloss**: Explains the colonial assumption of an "unchanging India," which justified the violent pruning of local variations to fit a centralized legal ledger.

#### 9. The Whig Lens on Substantive Property Rights (Page 71)
> *"The object was to find and fix a Hindu civil law concerned with... those rights, public and private, that affected the ownership and transmission of property."*
*   **Gloss**: Shows how the British systematically stripped out the ethical, ritual, and duties-based aspects of Dharma-shastra to isolate property law.

#### 10. Regional "Schools of Law" as a Superimposed Grid (Pages 73–74)
> *"Colebrooke organized the differences conceptually, in what he termed 'schools' of Hindu law... 'engrafted upon Hindu law an element which was foreign to it'... Colebrooke viewed the commentaries... as reflecting 'the actual law of the land.'"*
*   **Gloss**: Demonstrates how colonial administrators forced fluid regional variations into a symmetrical, rigid classification grid modeled on Islamic and Roman law.

#### 11. The Creation of Indo-British Case Law (Page 75)
> *"What had started with Warren Hastings and Sir William Jones as a search for the 'ancient Indian constitution' ended up with what they had so much wanted to avoid—with English law as the law of India."*
*   **Gloss**: The ultimate path-dependent outcome: the attempt to apply native law ended up institutionalizing English common law, creating a structural misfit between state record and ground reality.

### Theme C: The Survey Modality & Indigenous Mediation

#### 12. Survey as Exploration and Bounding (Page 7)
> *"The survey as an investigative modality encompasses a wide range of practices, from the mapping of India to collecting botanical specimens, to the recording of architectural and archaeological sites of historic significance, or the most minute measuring of a peasant's fields."*
*   **Gloss**: Shows how the spatial and the social were integrated under a single state imperative: to catalog, limit, and "know" the conquered territory.

#### 13. The Mysore Survey as an Imperial Matrix (Page 80)
> *"Wellesley recognized the need for the systematic collection of information... to this end Wellesley established several surveys... [to] cover any systematic and official investigation of the natural and social features of the Indian empire."*
*   **Gloss**: Traces the transition of surveying from simple cartography to a totalizing documentation project of social and natural sciences.

#### 14. Mackenzie and the Brahman Mediators (Pages 81–82)
> *"Mackenzie credited his meeting and subsequent association with Cavelli Venkata Boria, a Telugu brahman, in 1796 with enabling him to enter into 'the portal of Indian knowledge.' Boria... directed a growing staff of Indians... collecting texts, inscriptions, artifacts, and all kinds of historical and sociological information."*
*   **Gloss**: Highlights that colonial knowledge was co-produced by local elite intermediaries (pandits, amins, writers) who negotiated, shaped, and sometimes subverted what the state recorded.

#### 15. The Indian State as Non-Political (Page 79)
> *"The traditional Indian state was epiphenominal and it was found to have no political order, rather India turned out to be a land of unchanging institutions based on family, caste, and the village community."*
*   **Gloss**: Captures the "village republic" myth used to bypass indigenous state claims, focusing instead on local, micro-level caste and village divisions.

---

### Theme D: Caste, State Power & The Breast-Cloth Controversy

#### 16. Travancore as a "Pure" Caste State (Page 137)
> *"...'perhaps the only kingdom in India which preserves its original caste, religion, customs, manners and institutions etc.'... Travancore is one of the very few remaining specimens of a pure Hindu government, the institutions of which have never been affected by the Mohammedan conquest."*
*   **Gloss**: Explains why Travancore was the ideal site for the study of rigid, spatial-somatic hierarchies enforced directly by state revenue and judicial power.

#### 17. The Somatic-Spatial Code of Caste (Page 137)
> *"There was a highly specified code of respect and avoidance behavior enforced by the state... Nadar women could not carry pots on their hips nor could they cover the upper part of their bodies."*
*   **Gloss**: Underlines how social power and status were written directly onto the body, regulated and policed by the state apparatus.

#### 18. The Reassertion of Caste Privileges (Page 138)
> *"The Nairs and idle officials interpreted the proclamation [of 1858] to mean that it not only prohibited all future interference with caste, 'but annulled all previous interventions.'"*
*   **Gloss**: Direct parallel to how dominant castes utilize state laws or technocratic reforms (like digitisation or direct cash transfers) to reassert traditional hierarchical dominance and exclude marginalized groups.

#### 19. The Missionary Agenda of Freeing Low Castes (Page 137)
> *"The missionary agenda, in addition to conversion of the Nadars, was to free them from what they saw as the thraldom of 'the heathen caste system.' To do this meant establishing communities, centered on chapels, churches, and schools..."*
*   **Gloss**: Demonstrates how external interventions (whether Christian missionaries or neoliberal NGOs/World Bank) act as institutional disruptors of local agrarian hierarchies.

#### 20. Decency Linked to the Expansion of the Market (Page 139)
> *"...decency and proper dress would be linked with the expansion of the markets for British industrialists, and all this could be done in the name of the advancement of civilization."*
*   **Gloss**: Tying moral "modernisation" directly to the capitalist logic of expanding market boundaries, mirroring how modern digital titling (DILRMP) is sold as "transparency" but facilitates land markets.

#### 21. Custom and Deviation as Rebellion (Page 161)
> *"Once the British had defined something as an Indian custom... any deviation from it was defined as rebellion and an act to be punished. India was redefined by the British to be a place of rules and orders; once the British had defined to their own satisfaction what they construed as Indian rules and customs, then the Indians had to conform to these constructions."*
*   **Gloss**: Connects to the idea that when the state digitises a record, any ground-level deviation from the database is defined as "informality" or "encroachment."

## Thesis Relevance (Filter)

### 1. Research Question Alignment
*   **RQ1 (DILRMP Motivation/Design)**: Cohn's analysis of the *Historiographical and Survey Modalities* (p. 5–8) unpacks the historical motivation behind the state's obsession with documentation. DILRMP is not a neutral IT project; it is the modern stage of a 250-year-old "investigative modality" designed to render the agrarian landscape legible for state calculation.
*   **RQ3 (Agrarian History and Social Relations)**: The Travancore case (p. 136–139) directly addresses how changes in state rules or social status are contested by dominant vs. marginalized castes. 
*   **RQ4 (Source of Disjuncture)**: Chapter 3 is the ultimate answer to why there is a gap between textual records and ground reality. The British *initially tried to find who "owned" the land solely to fix tax liability* (p. 59). By freezing dynamic, fluid customary usages into rigid common law precedents, they institutionalized the structural misfit that separates the Presumptive RoR from actual possession on the ground.

### 2. Geographic Grounding (Test 2)
*   Passes unconditionally. The source focuses on the historical core of British India (Bengal Presidency) and South India (Madras/Travancore), which directly shaped the revenue settlement systems (Zamindari and Ryotwari) that later mapped onto Odisha (coastal Zamindari vs. southern Ryotwari).

### 3. Chapter Mapping (Test 3)
*   **Chapter 2.2.2 (Colonial Approaches to Land Records)**: Cohn's "Survey Modality" and the Mysore Survey of Colin Mackenzie (p. 7–8, 80–84) provide a rich history of early land classification, cartography, and the co-production of geographic knowledge by local Brahman amins.
*   **Chapter 2.3.2 (Colonial Settlements & Permanent Settlement Legacy)**: The detailed breakdown of Warren Hastings's 1772 Plan, Clive's 1765 letter, and the resulting 1793 Permanent Settlement (p. 57–62) provides the primary historical bedrock.
*   **Chapter 3.4 (Critical Reflections on Technocratic Reforms)**: Cohn's argument that "information" was treated as a tool of rule shows that modern digital land records are not neutral, but rather *restate* old colonial administrative patterns in a digital format.
*   **Chapter 5.4 & 5.5 (Gender, Caste, and Agrarian Relations)**: The "Breast-Cloth Controversy" provides an excellent comparative theoretical model for how local agrarian hierarchies subvert, resist, or appropriate state-mandated status changes.

### 4. Active Debates Addressed
*   **Debate 1: Presumptive vs. Conclusive Title**: Explains why India's records remain presumptive—they were designed as revenue liability trackers, not title deeds.
*   **Debate 4: Colonial Revenue Record → Postcolonial Title**: The core structural misfit analyzed by Cohn in Chapter 3.
*   **Debate 7: NIE Efficiency vs. Agrarian Social Relations**: The Travancore study shows that land relations cannot be reduced to neo-classical property rights efficiency; they are deeply bound to somatic and spatial caste hierarchies, honor, and state-sanctioned exclusion.

## Ideas & Questions Sparked

*   **The "Digital Collector"**: Warren Hastings invented the "Collector" as an executive-judicial hybrid to enforce "law and order" through tax collection (p. 60). How has the modern Tehsildar or RI inherited this hybrid role, and does digitisation (e-mutation, online Bhulekh) dilute or reinforce this centralized, colonial bureaucratic power?
*   **The "Bhulekh Ur-Text"**: Sir William Jones sought a sacred, unchanging "Ur-text" (p. 69) to bypass "corrupt" living pandits. In the same way, does DILRMP seek a digital "Ur-text" (the online RoR) to bypass "corrupt" local revenue officials (Amins/RIs), and does this digital legibility create a new form of "epistemic violence" against the messy, dynamic reality of rural possession?
*   **Modern Somatic/Digital Codes**: If the 19th-century Travancore state wrote caste hierarchy onto the bare bodies of Nadar women (p. 137), does the postcolonial state write exclusion onto the digital RoR? How do marginalized castes in Odisha village studies navigate the "digital gaze" of Bhulekh to secure their tenancy, inheritance, and welfare eligibility?
