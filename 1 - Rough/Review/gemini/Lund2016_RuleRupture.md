# Literature Review: "Rule and Rupture" (Lund, 2016)

## 1. Core Argument

In *Rule and Rupture*, Christian Lund argues that the state is not a static, pre-existing administrative container that distributes rights; rather, it is a relational process that is constantly "in the making." Political authority and rights (conceptualized through property and citizenship) are contemporaneous and mutually constitutive. When an institution successfully defines and enforces property rights or community membership (citizenship), it recursively establishes and consolidates its own authority, thereby acquiring "state quality." Moments of "rupture" (colonialism, regime shifts, conflict, or democratization) serve as "open moments" or revelatory crises that expose the raw construction of these contracts of recognition, showing that rights do not simply flow from authority but actively constitute it.

> "The core element of both rights is recognition. The processes of recognition of claims to land and other resources as property, and of political identity as citizenship with entitlements, simultaneously invest the institution that provides such recognition with recognition of its authority to do so. That is to say, the act of authorizing recursively authorizes the authorizer." (p. 1204)

---

## 2. Conceptual Architecture

Lund builds his framework on five load-bearing concepts, defined with verbatim precision:

* **The State-in-the-Making**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Treating the 'state' as a finished product gets in the way of understanding it. The state is always in the making. Political authority is (re-)produced through its successful exercise over an important issue in relation to the social actors concerned." (p. 1200)
  * *Gloss*: Statehood is not defined by static Weberian compliance but by the active, daily reproduction of authority through the regulation of vital resources.
* **Property**:
  * *Verbatim*: "I therefore understand property as a legitimized claim to something of value sanctioned by some form of political authority... Struggles over property... can therefore be seen as struggles for the recognition of a wide variety of rights to access resources..." (p. 1203)
  * *Gloss*: Property is not a thing or an absolute legal status, but a socially and politically sanctioned relation that requires a validating "authorizer."
* **Citizenship**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Citizenship can be defined as meaningful membership of an organized political body. Struggles for citizenship are, generally, struggles for the recognition of the very right to have rights in a body politic..." (p. 1203)
  * *Gloss*: Citizenship represents local or national political "visibility" and agency, acting as the mechanism that determines who can legitimately claim property.
* **Reciprocal Recognition & State Quality**:
  * *Verbatim*: "I am looking for an effective recognition of rights by an institution and a reciprocal recognition of its authority by their subjects... The effective recognition of claims as rights produces state quality in recognizing institutions." (p. 1204)
  * *Gloss*: A dual-directional loop where individuals accept an institution's authority (paying taxes, showing deference) in exchange for the enforcement of their claims as rights.
* **Rupture**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Ruptures are 'open moments' when opportunities and risks multiply, when the scope of outcomes widens, and when new structural scaffolding is erected." (p. 1201)
  * *Gloss*: Significant historical crises that shatter established social contracts, making the underlying machinery of state-making and rights-enforcement visible to observers.

## 3. Empirical and Evidence Terrain

Lund substantiates his processual framework through historical examples and two main post-rupture case studies:

* **Historical Case: The Domesday Book (1086)**:
  * Following the Norman Conquest of England in 1066 (a massive political rupture), William the Conqueror compiled the Domesday Book in 1086. It recorded landholdings and identities, establishing that the propertied classes owed their rights entirely to the new sovereign. Thus, "the Domesday Book established the sovereign as much as it established rights" (p. 1202).
* **Case 1: Chieftaincy in Southern Ghana**:
  * *The Rupture*: Colonial indirect rule.
  * *Mechanism*: Colonial agents declared land as part of the "traditional" realm to keep it non-commoditized and cheap. They consolidated chiefs' power to allocate land to northern migrant cocoa farmers. Postcolonial state reforms (1979–1992) further entrenched chiefs by placing Customary Land Secretariats under their control.
  * *Citizenship Nexus*: In the 1990s, land scarcity prompted local youth to pressure chiefs to retroactively weaken the property rights of "strangers" (migrants) to favor "autochthons," showing how local citizenship categories dictate property security.
* **Case 2: Paramilitaries and Ormas in Indonesia**:
  * *The Rupture*: The fall of Suharto's New Order in 1998, democratization, and decentralization.
  * *Mechanism*: During the New Order, paramilitaries (*ormas*) operated as state-contracted muscle. The post-1998 democratic rupture fragmented central military control.
  * *State Quality*: In cities like Medan, *ormas* began controlling informal settlements and acquiring land. To mobilize voters for local politicians, they delivered protection (housing, services, dispute resolution) to poor squatters. By enforcing and protecting these informal rights, the *ormas* established themselves as local authorities, acquiring "state quality" from below.

## 4. The Quote Bank

### Theme A: State Formation and the "State-in-the-Making"
1. **"Treating the ‘state’ as a finished product gets in the way of understanding it. The state is always in the making."** (p. 1199 / p. 1200)
   * *Gloss*: The core ontology of the paper. We must analyze the state as a continuous, unfinished project of consolidation rather than a static administrative apparatus.
2. **"Political authority is (re-)produced through its successful exercise over an important issue in relation to the social actors concerned."** (p. 1200)
   * *Gloss*: Power is performance-based; public authority must be actively demonstrated to maintain its legitimacy.
3. **"Struggles over property and citizenship are therefore as much about the scope and constitution of political authority as they are about access to resources and membership of polities."** (p. 1200)
   * *Gloss*: Conflict over land is never just about dirt or boundaries; it is a battle over who has the sovereign right to rule.
4. **"Government institutions are not the only source of state effects."** (p. 1201)
   * *Gloss*: State-like power (taxation, adjudication, policing) can reside in non-statutory bodies like customary chiefs, peasant associations, or paramilitaries.
5. **"In a nutshell, it is a claim to ‘state’."** (p. 1201)
   * *Gloss*: When an individual appeals to an institution to secure a right, they are actively conjuring and validating that institution's public authority.
6. **"The capacity to institute membership, or citizenship, in a body politic, and the power to establish and defend rights, especially to property, is the essence of public authority; the essence of state."** (p. 1219)
   * *Gloss*: The ultimate distillation of what constitutes a state: the twin powers of membership definition and resource allocation.

### Theme B: The Dialectic of Rights and Authority
7. **"To grasp the dialectics of rights and authority, we need to dispense with simple assumptions that political authority exists prior to rights of property and citizenship. Rights and political authority are contemporaneous..."** (p. 1201)
   * *Gloss*: Rejects both the Hobbesian "state first" and lockean "natural rights first" models, proposing instead that rights and rulers are born together.
8. **"Conversely, effective rights do not represent pre-existing natural rights. They are political constructions and achievements."** (p. 1201)
   * *Gloss*: Rights do not exist in the abstract; they are only real when backed by an authority capable of enforcing them against challengers.
9. **"Claims to rights prompt the exercise of authority."** (p. 1200)
   * *Gloss*: The state does not act in a vacuum. It is the demands and petitions of citizens that force the administrative machinery of the state to mobilize.
10. **"Rights are not simply there, bestowed on people by a benevolent higher body; they are wrested from power."** (p. 1206)
    * *Gloss*: Focuses on agency and political economy; rights are the product of historic struggles and political negotiations.
11. **"Political subjectivity does not only concern relations to formal governments or singular institutions. It concerns relations to any institutional actor that recognizes claims..."** (p. 1207)
    * *Gloss*: If a local gang or customary chief secures your home when the police will not, your political subjectivity is bound to that gang or chief.
12. **"As rights and public authority are co-produced, the erosion of one also means the dissipation of the other."** (p. 1205)
    * *Gloss*: If the state loses the capacity to defend land titles, its own legitimacy and authority in the eyes of the landholders dissolve.

### Theme C: Ruptures as Epistemological Windows
13. **"Ruptures are ‘open moments’ when opportunities and risks multiply, when the scope of outcomes widens, and when new structural scaffolding is erected."** (p. 1201)
    * *Gloss*: Ruptures are periods of structural instability where the rules of resource distribution and political belonging are rewritten.
14. **"By looking at ruptures, however... we can inspect the very construction of new contracts of recognition."** (p. 1201)
    * *Gloss*: Explains the methodological choice of studying crises; it allows the researcher to see the state-making process in slow motion.
15. **"The Domesday Book established the sovereign as much as it established rights."** (p. 1202)
    * *Gloss*: A powerful historical example showing that state registration of property is primarily a tool to bind property owners to the sovereign.
16. **"Hence, when we use words like post-colonial, post-liberal, post-socialist, or post-authoritarian, it is to suggest a dramatic break... without implying that all colonial features have disappeared..."** (p. 1202-1203)
    * *Gloss*: Highlights institutional path dependency. New regimes are always built on the "institutional debris" and administrative categories of the past.
17. **"Indeed, post-colonial governments have in many instances preserved — sometimes even furthered — colonial patterns of exclusionary property and subject formation."** (p. 1203)
    * *Gloss*: Postcolonial continuity. The post-independence state frequently adopts the same divide-and-rule classification tactics as its colonial predecessor.

### Theme D: The Nexus of Property and Citizenship
18. **"I therefore understand property as a legitimized claim to something of value sanctioned by some form of political authority."** (p. 1203)
    * *Gloss*: Property is not an object but a social relation of exclusion and inclusion that requires institutional backing to exist.
19. **"Struggles over property — very often in the form of land — can therefore be seen as struggles for the recognition of a wide variety of rights to access resources..."** (p. 1203)
    * *Gloss*: Land conflicts are fundamentally legal-political conflicts over the recognition of claims.
20. **"Territorialization and the spatial ordering of people combine different political and legal techniques of classification, registration and mapping."** (p. 1203-1204)
    * *Gloss*: Mapping and registration are political strategies of territorialization used to control both physical space and social populations.
21. **"Citizenship can be defined as meaningful membership of an organized political body."** (p. 1203)
    * *Gloss*: Broadens citizenship beyond national passports, defining it as the recognized "right to have rights" within any functional polity.
22. **"There is only one thing worse than being seen by political authority, and that is not being seen."** (p. 1204)
    * *Gloss*: Highlights the desperation of the marginalized. Being mapped, registered, or taxed by the state is often preferred over total exclusion and invisibility.

### Theme E: Reciprocal Recognition and State Quality
23. **"The processes of recognition of claims... simultaneously invest the institution that provides such recognition with recognition of its authority to do so."** (p. 1204)
    * *Gloss*: The recursive loop: when you ask the state to register your land, you are validating the state's right to govern you.
    * *Citation*: Lund (2016: 1204)
24. **"The effective recognition of claims as rights produces state quality in recognizing institutions."** (p. 1204)
    * *Gloss*: Statehood is not a binary status; it is a quality acquired by any institution that successfully enforces property and membership rights.
25. **"In exchange for recognized valuable property rights in land... people recognize the political power of the institution by payment of tax in the form of money, tribute, labour, allegiance, or other resources."** (p. 1205)
    * *Gloss*: The fiscal-sociological contract: property rights are secured in exchange for resource extraction and political compliance.
26. **"The capacity to recognize rights is also the capacity to deny and expunge them."** (p. 1205)
    * *Gloss*: A warning on the absolute power of the state. The registry that gives you a title is the same registry that can write you out of existence.

### Theme F: Institutional Pluralism and Fragmented Jurisdictions
27. **"We can think of jurisdictions as overlapping and interfering fields of authority in the making, rather than discrete realms of defined legal scope."** (p. 1210)
    * *Gloss*: Rejects the neat legal-rational map of administration. In reality, multiple authorities compete to resolve the same disputes.
28. **"Clever collusion to reduce land users' rights."** (p. 1212)
    * *Gloss*: The state and customary elites often collaborate (e.g., in Ghana) to expropriate smallholders and share the revenues, rather than clashing.
29. **"Parallel, practical contracts of recognition emerge where authority and rights are functional and effective but have only faint connections to official norms and law."** (p. 1208)
    * *Gloss*: Explains the informal economy of land administration. People build informal structures to secure what they believe is legally theirs.
30. **"By forming health committees, market guilds... before there is a clinic, a marketplace... citizens enter the orbit of certain governing institutions and conjure up the exercise of authority..."** (p. 1208)
    * *Gloss*: The performative aspect of citizenship. Marginalized groups organize in anticipation of state structures to force the state to recognize them.

## 5. Thesis Relevance Filter (`PHD_LENS.md` Calibration)

This paper is highly load-bearing for the thesis on *Land Records Modernisation in Odisha* and maps directly onto several core components:

### Research Question Alignment
* **RQ2 (Harmony of objectives and outcomes)**: Lund's concept of the "visibility struggle" applies directly to how different classes in Odisha villages use updated Record of Rights (RoRs). Acquiring an RoR is not just an administrative transaction; it is a "claim to state" and an attempt to become visible to receive welfare (e.g., PM-Kisan/KALIA).
* **RQ4 (Source of disjuncture between records and possession)**: The disjuncture is not merely technical but political, arising from the coexistence of "practical contracts of recognition" (possession, inheritance, customary partitions) and the statutory, digital contracts represented by the Bhulekh database.
* **RQ5 (Technocratic vs. political economy of DILRMP)**: Digitisation is a technocratic reform that tries to bypass the contested social relations of land. Lund's thesis suggests that computerisation cannot resolve conflicts because struggles over land records are fundamentally struggles over "the scope and constitution of political authority" (p. 1200).

### Geographic Grounding (Test 2)
* Although the case studies are in Ghana and Indonesia, they provide direct theoretical frameworks for Odisha:
  * *The Ghana custom-state collusion* mirrors how local elites, corporate buyers, and state revenue machinery (Tehsildar, Revenue Inspector, Amin) navigate land acquisition by manipulating land classifications.
  * *The Indonesian Ormas' informal protection* mirrors how local political brokers, village committee heads, or influential factions in Odisha step in to resolve partition and possession disputes, bypassing the paralysis of the civil courts and formal revenue settlement operations.

### Chapter Mapping (Test 3)
* **Chapter 2.2.2 & 2.3.2 (Colonial Land Administration)**: Lund's analysis of the Norman Conquest and the Domesday Book (1086) provides a historical model to explain the East India Company's Permanent Settlement of 1793 as a tool of sovereign-building rather than just title registration.
* **Chapter 3.4 (Critical reflections on technocratic reforms)**: Lund's critique of the Weberian ideal-state failure will be used to analyze why DILRMP's push toward conclusive titling fails—it assumes a pre-existing, neutral state authority rather than recognizing the fragmented "patchwork of jurisdictions" at the local level.
* **Chapter 5.5 & 5.7 (Ground-level realities of land ownership vs. possession)**: Lund's definition of property as a "legitimized claim... sanctioned by some form of political authority" explains why villagers are willing to bypass formal mutation processes, relying instead on village-level recognition and local revenue office intermediaries.

## 6. Ideas & Questions Sparked for Thesis Writing

* **The Digital Domesday of Odisha**: Can we read the digitisation of Bhulekh and Bhunaksha under DILRMP as a modern "Domesday Book"? Instead of securing smallholder rights, is it a state project of territorialization designed to establish the postcolonial state's absolute sovereignty and facilitate land acquisition for industrial development?
* **Welfare and the Visibility Dilemma**: Lund notes that "there is only one thing worse than being seen by political authority, and that is not being seen." In Odisha, how does the requirement of an RoR for PM-Kisan and CM-Kisan force informal sharecroppers or female farmers to perform citizenship by seeking visibility in a bureaucratic system that structurally excludes them?
* **Twilight Revenue Administration**: How do the "practical norms" and rent-seeking behaviors of local revenue intermediaries (Amins and RIs) function as informal "contracts of recognition" that actually keep the land administration system running, despite the official statutory paralysis?
