# Literature Review: "Land, Power, and Property Rights" (Ribar, 2026)

## 1. Core Argument

In *Land, Power, and Property Rights*, Matthew K. Ribar resolves a major empirical anomaly in agrarian development: why do only 15% of African households possess a formal land title for their agricultural land despite widespread availability, international aid subsidies, and documented investment benefits? The paper argues that this uneven uptake is explained by the strategic interactions between **local politics** (specifically the strength of customary chiefs) and **national land regimes** (centralized vs. devolved). 

Households behave as rational actors, deciding to formalize their land only when the potential returns to agricultural investments outweigh the administrative costs and social risks of formalization. However, this demand-side calculus is heavily moderated by customary elites who act defensively to preserve their authority. In countries with **centralized land tenure regimes** (where titling is managed by distant national agencies), formalization removes land from customary arbitration, threatening the chiefs' role as development and dispute-resolution intermediaries. Consequently, strong customary institutions actively *impede* and *attenuate* the relationship between rising land values and titling. In contrast, in countries with **devolved land tenure regimes** (where village-level bodies make key titling decisions), chiefs can *capture* the process, staffing land committees with co-ethnics and extracting rents. In these devolved settings, strong customary institutions *reinforce* and *facilitate* the relationship between land values and titling, using the formalization process to entrench their authority and advance their political agendas by discriminating against out-groups.

> "In countries with centralized land tenure regimes, titling erodes the authority of intermediaries who then impede formalization when they can. In countries which devolve responsibilities for land tenure administration to local governments, customary elites facilitate land titling because they can capture the process and maintain their authority." (p. 399)

## 2. Conceptual Architecture

The paper establishes several core conceptual building blocks to analyze the political economy of demand-driven titling:

* **Centralized Land Tenure Regimes**:
  * *Verbatim*: "In countries with centralized land tenure regimes, decisions around land tenure formalization are made at the national level... Land administration and titling occur in Monrovia far from customary institutions... [or] is managed entirely by the state." (p. 403)
  * *Gloss*: Systems where state agencies directly map, adjudicate, and record titles without local customary intermediaries, making it harder for village chiefs to control the process or claim credit.
* **Devolved Land Tenure Regimes**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Other countries devolve their land tenure regimes to local authorities... where land tenure administration is devolved, chiefs are able to capture the land tenure formalization process." (p. 403)
  * *Gloss*: Systems where the statutory authority to adjudicate, issue, or recommend land certificates is nested within local municipal or village-level bodies, creating structural opportunities for chiefly capture.
* **Customary Institution Strength (Precolonial Hierarchy)**:
  * *Verbatim*: "To measure the extent to which chiefs are able to act on their incentives to impede or facilitate titling, I use georeferenced data from Murdock’s Ethnographic Atlas... the precolonial institution’s level of hierarchy, which Murdock measures [by the number of hierarchical layers]." (p. 405)
  * *Gloss*: The historical complexity and hierarchical depth of local traditional authorities, which determines their contemporary capacity to enforce decrees, sanction dissidents, or capture local state bodies.
* **Attainable Yield / Land Value**:
  * *Verbatim*: "I implement a novel measurement of 'attainable value': the value for which the maximum attainable yield (per hectare) could sell for on the international commodity market." (p. 405)
  * *Gloss*: A geospatial measure of potential agricultural land value calculated by multiplying GAEZ crop suitability indexes by international commodity prices, bypassing the illegibility of informal land markets.
* **Strategic Ambiguity**:
  * *Verbatim*: "In much of Africa, agricultural parcels are subject to overlapping ownerships which exist in a state of strategic ambiguity... Land titling forces the issue of hierarchy between these partial owners." (p. 401)
  * *Gloss*: The co-existence of multiple overlapping claims (usufruct, grazing, lineage ownership) on a single parcel, which is disrupted by the binary, zero-sum nature of formal titling.
* **Autochthone vs. Allochthone Divide**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Land tenure in Côte d’Ivoire pits autochthones, or sons of the soil, against allochthones, or relative newcomers... Village chiefs are almost always autochthones. Later arrivals are called 'allochthones,' or simply 'strangers.'" (p. 411)
  * *Gloss*: The deep social and political distinction between original settlers (who claim customary sovereignty and dominate local committees) and subsequent migrants (who hold usufruct rights but face exclusion from formal property titles).

## 3. Empirical and Evidence Terrain

Ribar employs a nested, mixed-method research design combining cross-national statistical analysis with subnational fieldwork:

* **Cross-National Dataset**:
  * **Sample**: 170,216 rural, land-owning household observations from 62 survey waves across 22 African countries (DHS and LSMS data from 2010–2021).
  * **Geospatial Control**: Integrates FAO's GAEZ model (v4) at a 10km-by-10km grid cell level to estimate land values and returns.
  * **Specification**: OLS linear probability models with country-survey wave fixed effects and triple interactions (Land Value $\times$ Precolonial Hierarchy $\times$ Devolved Land Regime).
  * **Placebo Test**: Evaluates the effect of land values on formal titles for *dwellings* (finding no relationship), ruling out household wealth as the primary causal driver.
* **Case Study: Côte d’Ivoire (Cocoa Belt)**:
  * **Sample**: Original survey of 801 household heads and 194 customary elites (village chiefs/headmen) across 80 villages in the Indénié-Djuablin and Haut-Sassandra regions.
  * **Key Findings**:
    * **CVGFR Staffing**: Autochthones overwhelmingly dominate Village Land Management Committees (CVGFRs) even in villages where they represent a minority or are equally split (e.g., in mostly allochthone villages, 15 out of 16 committees were mostly autochthonous).
    * **Inequal Uptake**: In Côte d'Ivoire (a devolved regime), a 1 SD increase in chiefs' political authority is associated with a 13-percentage-point *increase* in titling for autochthones (in-group) but a 9-percentage-point *decrease* for allochthones (out-group).
    * **Uneven Enforcement**: A 1 SD increase in chiefs' traditional authority decreases the perceived usefulness of titles (to keep land or resolve disputes) by 0.50–0.53 SDs for allochthones, indicating that powerful chiefs can overrule formal certificates to protect customary hierarchies.
    * **Rent Extraction**: 70% of respondents expected to pay chiefs to formalize a land title, compared to only 57% who expected to pay the legally mandated CVGFR.

## 4. The Quote Bank

### Theme A: The Political Economy of Customary Power and Land Regimes

1. **"Only 15% of African households possess a formal title for their agricultural land, despite the widespread availability of titles and their documented benefits. Local politics combine with national land regimes to explain this empirical anomaly."** (p. 399)
   * *Gloss*: Introduces the core puzzle of low and uneven formalization rates across Sub-Saharan Africa.
2. **"In countries with centralized land tenure regimes, titling erodes the authority of intermediaries who then impede formalization when they can. In countries which devolve responsibilities for land tenure administration to local governments, customary elites facilitate land titling because they can capture the process and maintain their authority."** (p. 399)
   * *Gloss*: The central theoretical mechanism explaining how the level of decentralization dictates chiefs' incentives.
3. **"In centralized land regimes, strong customary authorities attenuate the relationship between land values and land tenure formalization: a 1,000 USD increase in the returns to long-term agricultural investment is associated with a 32% increase in the likelihood of possessing a land title where chiefs are strongest and a 66% increase where chiefs are weakest."** (p. 399)
   * *Gloss*: Quantitative proof of chiefs acting as blockages in centralized regimes.
4. **"In contrast, strong customary authorities in devolved land regimes strengthen the relationship between returns to agricultural investment and land tenure formalization. In such countries, the same increase in the returns to investment is associated with a 57% increase where chiefs are strongest, and no increase where they are weakest."** (p. 399)
   * *Gloss*: Quantitative proof of chiefs facilitating titling when they can capture the administrative mechanisms.
5. **"By keeping land rights in the customary regime, chiefs maintain a long-term reservoir of political legitimacy... If control over land held in the customary system enhances chiefs’ political authority, then removing control of land to a centralized formal land regime will reduce their authority."** (p. 402)
   * *Gloss*: Highlights the institutional stakes: land administration is the foundation of traditional political authority.
6. **"Chiefs situated within hierarchical institutions are better able to prevent land titling because they are more empowered to enforce their decrees. Within weakly hierarchical institutions, customary elites may not have the political capital to enforce judgements."** (p. 402)
   * *Gloss*: Connects historical precolonial hierarchy (from Murdock's atlas) to contemporary enforcement capacity.
7. **"Where control of land is devolved, chiefs are able to capture the land tenure formalization process."** (p. 403)
   * *Gloss*: Defines the structural vulnerability of decentralized land registries to elite capture.
8. **"This capture of the titling process means that chiefs do not lose their power over land tenure, even when households seek title. As a result, chiefs can use titling to advance their own political agendas."** (p. 403)
   * *Gloss*: Explains why chiefs in devolved regimes actively encourage in-group formalization.

### Theme B: The Household Demand and Investment Calculus

9. **"Insecure property rights pose barriers to economic activity and stifle growth across the developing world... insecure land tenure constricts agricultural investments and reduces productivity."** (p. 399)
   * *Gloss*: Contextualizes the development costs of the property rights gap.
10. **"Households formalize their landholdings only when the value of the land, or the potential returns to agricultural investments enabled by titles, increases to the point that the benefits justify the costs."** (p. 399)
    * *Gloss*: The demand-side foundation: titling is a calculated investment driven by returns.
11. **"Land titling is costly... Bassett (2020, 144) enumerates 'over 20 steps to obtain a land certificate and another dozen to obtain a land title,' many of which involve a fee."** (p. 401)
    * *Gloss*: Illustrates the administrative friction that deters low-value or low-margin households from formalizing.
12. **"Land tenure formalization is not a one-to-one mapping of existing land use onto paper; it creates winners and losers. In much of Africa, agricultural parcels are subject to overlapping ownerships which exist in a state of strategic ambiguity."** (p. 401)
    * *Gloss*: Captures the social disruption of forcing fluid, customary rights into rigid statutory titles.
13. **"Households pursue titling despite the expense and the risk because land conflicts are an unfortunately common occurrence... Titling one’s land reduces the risk of losing it."** (p. 401)
    * *Gloss*: Property rights formalization is primarily sought as defensive protection against expropriation.
14. **"Where an asset is more expensive, households will go to greater lengths to protect it—including undertaking a costly titling process."** (p. 401)
    * *Gloss*: Echoes Demsetzian property rights theory on endogenous institutional change driven by price shocks.
15. **"Tree crops require a high up-front investment: households must purchase saplings or wait for trees to become productive. Coffee trees, for example, take five to seven years to become commercially viable."** (p. 405)
    * *Gloss*: Justifies using tree crop suitability as a proxy for long-term agricultural investment horizons.
16. **"Households who could make more money by using fertilizer are more likely to have a land title."** (p. 629)
    * *Gloss*: Links short-term investment capacity to the demand for formal documents.

### Theme C: Chiefly Capture, Rent Extraction, and Pluralism

17. **"Local land tenure arrangements are complex, heterogeneous, and often illegible to the state... As a result, chiefs play a large role in adjudicating land claims even when land administration is officially in the hands of the state. The important factor in my analysis is chiefs’ de facto control over titling, rather than their de jure control."** (p. 403)
    * *Gloss*: Decouples legal statutes from practical power; the state's structural legibility gap creates the space for chiefs to rule.
18. **"Village land management committees (CVGFRs) in Côte d’Ivoire investigate land claims... Village chiefs have no official position in CVGFRs, but they nevertheless almost always head the committee."** (p. 403)
    * *Gloss*: Shows the empirical reality of de facto capture of statutory local bodies in Côte d'Ivoire.
19. **"While chiefs have no official role in titling, 70% of the 801 respondents in my survey expected to have to pay chiefs to formalize a land title. Only 57% thought they would have to pay their CVGFR..."** (p. 828)
    * *Gloss*: Customary leaders extract substantial informal rents, outperforming the official state bureaucracy in revenue collection.
20. **"These statistics show that chiefs’ role in titling process not only buttresses their political authority, but allows them to extract rents."** (p. 828)
    * *Gloss*: Connects the political motivation of authority preservation with the economic motivation of rent extraction.
21. **"In Table C5... I show that trust in one’s chief does not increase either household titling or the extent to which households perceive land titles as valuable."** (p. 826)
    * *Gloss*: Rejects the trust-based hypothesis; households seek titles due to institutional dynamics, not subjective faith in traditional leaders.

### Theme D: The Autochthone-Allochthone Divide and Exclusion

22. **"Village chiefs discriminate against allochthones when they can because chiefs perceive the allochthone’s land to be merely borrowed."** (p. 704)
    * *Gloss*: Customary norms exclude migrants by framing their rights as temporary, preventing them from securing permanent titles.
23. **"In this devolved land regime, strong chiefs lead to more titles for the in-group (autochthones). However, in the Ivorian context, chiefly capture of land tenure administration means strong chiefs actually decrease titling rates among the outgroup (allochthones)..."** (p. 759)
    * *Gloss*: Devolved registries become tools of in-group entrenchment rather than universal formalization.
24. **"Table 5 shows that even in villages which are equally split or mostly allochthonous, autochthones dominate the CVGFRs."** (p. 769)
    * *Gloss*: Local demographic majorities do not translate to institutional control due to the entrenchment of customary elites.
25. **"All respondents think strong chiefs reduce the usefulness of titles, but this result is much stronger among allochthones, the out-group... Land titles are less of a constraint on the behavior of more powerful chiefs which allows them to capture land management and discriminate against allochthones."** (p. 767)
    * *Gloss*: Shows that formal property documents are ineffective shields for marginalized groups if local arbiters retain sovereign power.

### Theme E: Institutional Path Dependence and Policy Implications

26. **"Precolonial elites used the institutional discontinuity brought about by European rule to supplement their own authority... what today constitutes 'custom' reflects the powers that customary elites could convince the colonial state they possessed."** (p. 398)
    * *Gloss*: Highlights the colonial invention of custom as a path-dependent source of modern chiefly power.
27. **"Local politics affect how land tenure formalizations progress, which suggests a one-size fits all approach is unlikely to find consistent success."** (p. 874)
    * *Gloss*: Critiques uniform international development blueprints (like World Bank land policy reforms).
28. **"If chiefs facilitate land titling where they can capture the process, it also implies that African governments and donor organizations should consider the role of chiefs when designing land tenure formalization program."** (p. 874)
    * *Gloss*: Recommends integrating customary political structures rather than assuming they can be bypassed by technocratic design.

## 5. Thesis Relevance Filter (`PHD_LENS.md` Calibration)

This paper is highly load-bearing for the thesis *Land Records Modernisation in Odisha: A Study of History, Policy and Implications* and passes all three inclusion tests.

### Research Question Alignment
* **RQ1 & RQ2 (Implementation vs. Outcomes under DILRMP)**: Ribar's analysis of how local politics shape outcomes explains why Odisha's DILRMP (e.g., e-mutations, digitised RoRs) produces highly uneven outcomes. If the local land administration (RI/Amin/Tehsildar) is susceptible to capture by dominant local castes, digital modernisation will entrench existing agrarian hierarchies rather than provide secure title.
* **RQ3 & RQ4 (Disjuncture between records and ground reality)**: The paper's concept of *strategic ambiguity* (overlapping customary and usufruct rights) matches the Indian reality of unregistered partitions, sharecropping, and commons usage. Formalization (digitisation) forces a zero-sum resolution that widens the gap between the official record and actual village possession.
* **RQ5 (Technocratic reforms vs. political economy)**: Ribar directly addresses whether computerisation resolves conflicts or restates them. Modernisation programs are technocratic, but their success is dictated by local political structures (chiefly authority in Africa, caste/revenue bureaucracies in India).

### Geographic and Institutional Grounding (Test 2)
The paper is grounded in Sub-Saharan Africa and Côte d'Ivoire. However, it passes Test 2 because it contributes key comparative mechanisms for **Chapter 2.2 (Global Comparative Evolution)** and **Chapter 3.1.2 (World Bank/International Orthodoxy)**. The interaction between customary power and national land registries provides a direct analytical foil for India, where the postcolonial state inherited a colonial revenue apparatus that competes with local caste-panchayats (*caste sahils*) and informal tenure.

### Chapter Mapping (Test 3)
* **Chapter 1.1 (Conceptual Framework)**: Nuances the institutional economics (NIE) model of property rights by showing that elite capture, rather than just transaction costs, dictates the demand for titles.
* **Chapter 2.2.4 (Contemporary/Neoliberal Titling Orthodoxy)**: Critiques the de Soto-style model of "universal titling = economic growth" by showing how decentralization reforms (promoted by the World Bank/MCC) open the door to exclusionary elite capture.
* **Chapter 3.4 (Critical Reflections on Technocratic Reforms)**: Provides a framework to analyze why e-mutation and online RoRs (Bhulekh) do not resolve ground-level conflicts but are instead captured by dominant village factions to exclude sharecroppers or validate disputed inheritance claims.
* **Chapter 5.5 (Land Ownership vs. Possession)**: Direct comparative relevance for analyzing how dominant castes in Nayagarh villages act as "autochthones," staffing local committees and using digitised records to marginalize Scheduled Castes who hold usufruct or informal claims.

### Active Debates Engaged
* **Debate 2 (Formalisation as growth vs. dispossession)**: Ribar provides statistical and qualitative evidence of the "exclusionary caveat"—titling in Côte d'Ivoire led to the systematic exclusion and dispossession of allochthone migrants.
* **Debate 3 (Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy)**: Directly tests how local informal institutions moderate state-led digital/formalization drives.
* **Debate 7 (NIE efficiency vs. agrarian social relations)**: Tests whether the market-efficiency logic of titling holds when moderated by precolonial hierarchical structures and ethnic/migrant social divisions.

## 6. Ideas and Questions Sparked

* **The Caste-Autochthony Matrix in Odisha**: Translate the *autochthone-allochthone* divide into caste relations in Nayagarh. Do dominant landholding castes (e.g., Khandayats) act as "autochthones" who capture local land committees and digitisation processes (such as the verification of mutations) to block land record corrections for Scheduled Castes (e.g., Bauri or Kondh communities), whom they view as historical farm servants or temporary occupiers?
* **De facto Capture of the RI/Amin Bureaucracy**: Ribar shows that chiefs head village land committees (CVGFRs) despite having no legal role. In Odisha, analyze how local dominant-caste elites (such as village committee heads or lineage patriarchs) command the attention of the Revenue Inspector (RI) and Amin during field visits, guiding physical land measurements and mapping to partition land in favor of their lineage, effectively bypassing official procedures.
* **Informal Fees vs. Customary Rents**: Since 70% of Ribar's respondents expect to pay chiefs to title land, design a household survey module for Nayagarh to map the informal transaction costs of land mutations. Differentiate between official fees, speed money paid to the RI/Amin, and payments to village brokers (*dalals*) who act as intermediaries.
* **The "Dwelling Placebo" for Odisha**: Replicate Ribar's placebo design in the village surveys. Contrast the demand for agricultural RoRs (which are linked to state cash transfers like PM-Kisan/CM-Kisan and crop loans) with the demand for homestead land records (*Gharabari*). If the demand for agricultural records is significantly higher and more responsive to state schemes, it suggests that records are sought as welfare gateways rather than security instruments.
