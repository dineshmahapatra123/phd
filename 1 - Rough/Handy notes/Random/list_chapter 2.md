### §2.1 Land Records and Their Functions

This section establishes _what land records are_ and _what they do_ — laying the conceptual groundwork the rest of the chapter sits on. The argumentative move: land records are not neutral administrative artefacts but instruments of state power that combine fiscal, juridical, economic, and governmental functions in historically variable proportions.

#### Foundational theoretical works

- ★★★ ==**James C. Scott (1998)**==, _Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed_, Yale University Press. Especially the introduction and chapters 1–2 on legibility and the cadastral map. The single most important reference for this section.
- ★★★ ==**Bernard S. Cohn (1996)**==, _Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge: The British in India_, Princeton University Press. Read the introduction on "investigative modalities" — survey, enumerative, observational. Survey modalities explored both natural and social landscapes allowing for t szqahe mapping of India and identification of archaeological sites, and enumerative modalities created the census infrastructure. This pairs perfectly with Scott. [Baruch Honors](https://blogs.baruch.cuny.edu/karadamodernimperialism/?p=28)
- ★★★ ==**Michel Foucault (1991)**, "Governmentality"== in _The Foucault Effect: Studies in Governmentality_, eds. Burchell, Gordon and Miller, University of Chicago Press. For the move from sovereignty to government — useful for explaining why DILRMP is not just better tax records.
- ★★ ==**Christian Lund (2016)**, "Rule and Rupture: State Formation through the Production of Property and Citizenship,==" _Development and Change_ 47(6): 1199–1228. Argues property and citizenship are co-produced through records — directly applicable to your argument.
- ★★ ==**Sara Berry (1993)**, _No Condition is Permanent: The Social Dynamics of Agrarian Change in Sub-Saharan Africa_, University of Wisconsin Press.== The classic argument that even where records exist, claims to land are continuously negotiated. 

#### Recent work on legibility, taxation, and records

- ★★ **Mauricio Sánchez-Talanquer (2020)**, "One-Eyed State: The Politics of Legibility and Property Taxation," _Latin American Politics and Society_ 62(3): 47–75. Argues that when land ownership is disputed, landholders use inscription into state records to secure legal property rights, and to minimize tax liabilities, propertied interests may exploit opportunities to manipulate land valuations. A really useful counterpoint to Scott — landholders sometimes _want_ legibility. [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/latin-american-politics-and-society/article/abs/oneeyed-state-the-politics-of-legibility-and-property-taxation/E796B1A7F0B0C0913C6A2118646FA566)
- ★★ **Karen E. Ferree, Lauren Honig, Ellen Lust and Melanie L. Phillips (2023)**, "Land and Legibility: When Do Citizens Expect Secure Property Rights in Weak States?" _American Political Science Review_ 117(1): 42–58.
- ★★ **Lauren Honig (2022)**, "Land, Power, and Property Rights: The Political Economy of Land Titling in Sub-Saharan Africa," _American Political Science Review_. Very useful for the "demand side" of titling.
- ★ **Naím Bro (2023)**, "No taxation without informational foundation: on the role of legibility in tax state development," _Journal of Institutional Economics_.

#### Function-typology references

- ★★ **Daniel Bromley (2008)**, "Formalising Property Relations in the Developing World: The Wrong Prescription for the Wrong Malady," _Land Use Policy_ 26(1): 20–27. For the distinction between possession and recognised property.

### §2.3 History of Land Records in India

This is the longest and most argumentatively dense section. The move: today's Indian land records system is a _palimpsest_ — pre-colonial revenue records overlaid with colonial property records overlaid with post-independence reform records — and DILRMP is trying to flatten this without acknowledging its layered nature.

#### §2.3.1 Pre-colonial record-keeping

**Mughal land revenue and records:**

- ★★★ **Irfan Habib (1999, revised edn.)**, _The Agrarian System of Mughal India 1556–1707_, Oxford University Press. The canonical work. Especially the chapters on revenue assessment and the _zabt_ system. Habib documents how the Mughal _patwari_ and _qanungo_ maintained sophisticated village and pargana-level records well before British arrival.
- ★★★ **Irfan Habib (1982)**, _An Atlas of the Mughal Empire_, Oxford University Press.
- ★★ **Shireen Moosvi (1987)**, _The Economy of the Mughal Empire c. 1595: A Statistical Study_, Oxford University Press. For quantitative depth.
- ★★ **Tapan Raychaudhuri and Irfan Habib (eds.) (1982)**, _The Cambridge Economic History of India, Vol. 1: c. 1200 – c. 1750_, Cambridge University Press. Use chapters 1, 7, 8, 9.
- ★★ **Sumit Guha (1995)**, "An Indian Penal Régime: Maharashtra in the Eighteenth Century," _Past & Present_ 147: 101–126. For Maratha-period revenue practices — directly relevant to Odisha given Maratha rule.
- ★★ **Sumit Guha (2013)**, _Beyond Caste: Identity and Power in South Asia, Past and Present_, Brill. For pre-colonial state forms.
- ★ **Abu'l Fazl** (1590s), _Ain-i-Akbari_, English translation by H. Blochmann and H.S. Jarrett. The primary source on the Mughal bandobast — accessible in standard libraries.
- ★ **Burton Stein (1980)**, _Peasant State and Society in Medieval South India_, Oxford University Press. For the _Vijayanagara_ and earlier South Indian record-keeping.
- ★ **Noboru Karashima (2001)**, _History and Society in South India: The Cholas to Vijayanagar_, Oxford University Press.
- ★ **Hiroshi Fukazawa (1991)**, _The Medieval Deccan: Peasants, Social Systems and States, Sixteenth to Eighteenth Centuries_, Oxford University Press.

**The point to drive home:** The Mughal _zabt-dahsala_ system established by Todar Mal in 1580 was a sophisticated revenue-assessment system, but it was a _fiscal_ system, not a _property rights_ system in the modern sense. For each pargana, the cultivated area of each crop, the yield in maunds per bigha, and the prevailing market prices over the years from 1570–71 to 1579–80 were averaged. From the resulting average yield, the state demanded one-third in kind, valued at the ten-year average price, and converted into a cash rate per bigha that was then entered into the dastur-ul-amal. This distinction is crucial — your section needs to make it visible. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahsala_system)

### §2.5 Genesis of DILRMP

The argumentative move: DILRMP did not arise from a vacuum — it emerged from the convergence of (a) decades of unresolved post-reform record obsolescence, (b) the World Bank/de Soto global titling consensus of the 1990s–2000s, and (c) India's broader Digital India agenda. Tracing this genealogy sets up Chapter 3.

#### The lineage scheme by scheme

- ★★★ **Department of Land Resources (various years)**, official documents on:
    - Computerisation of Land Records (CLR), 1988-89
    - Strengthening of Revenue Administration and Updating of Land Records (SRA&ULR), 1987-88
    - National Land Records Modernization Programme (NLRMP), 2008
    - DILRMP, 2016 (Central Sector Scheme conversion)
    - 2021–22 to 2025–26 extension
- ★★★ **Solomon Benjamin, R. Bhuvaneswari, P. Rajan, Manjunatha (2007)**, _"Bhoomi: 'E-Governance', Or, an Anti-Politics Machine Necessary to Globalize Bangalore?"_, CASUMM Working Paper. Foundational critical assessment of the _first_ major land records digitisation in India. The Bhoomi program on the digitization of twenty million land records is considered a 'best practice' model of 'e-governance' to be replicated in other parts of India and elsewhere in poor countries. The main findings, at two levels, contrast conventional wisdom. First the digitization of land records led to increased corruption, much more bribes and substantially increased time taken for land transactions. At another level, it facilitated very large players in the land markets to capture vast quantities of land. [Wordpress](https://casumm.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2008/09/bhoomi-e-governance.pdf)

### How V3 differs from V1 and V2

### The classical economics tradition: integrating Adam Smith and predecessors

Citing Smith directly gives your critique a longer historical reach and shows your committee you're working with the tradition rather than around it. Smith also matters because Ranajit Guha's _Rule of Property for Bengal_ (which you already have) is _partly_ about how Adam Smith's contemporaries in Calcutta read Smith — Philip Francis was reading Smith and the physiocrats.

**§2.3.2 Colonial India.** This is where Smith does the most work. ### Tying V1, V2, and V3 together: the unified argumentative spine

The chapter now operates with a **three-tradition spine** that you can mobilise across every section. Let me lay out how to think about it visually:

**Three lenses, one object:**

- **Land records as state-legibility** (Scott, Cohn, Foucault — V1) — answering _how_ the state knows land.
- **Land records as agrarian transition** (Marx, Byres, Bernstein, Harvey, Levien — V2) — answering _for whom_ land is made knowable.
- **Land records as institutional infrastructure** (Smith, Coase, North, Demsetz, de Soto, Deininger, Acemoglu — V3) — answering _whether_ secure title is sufficient for development.

**Three regimes, one palimpsest:**

- **Pre-colonial tributary** (Habib, Kosambi, Stein) — records as fiscal-extraction instruments.
- **Colonial-capitalist-failed** (Guha, Cohn, Stokes, Bhattacharya, Banerjee-Iyer, Banaji) — records as failed installation of capitalist property relations.
- **Post-independence redistributive-failed** (Appu, Patnaik, Bhaduri, Lerche) — records as the unfinished business of land reform.
- **Neoliberal-titling** (de Soto, Deininger, Levien, Pratyush, Benjamin) — records as the contemporary moment of accumulation by dispossession through digital legibility.

The argumentative thread that ties all twelve cells in this 3×4 matrix together is your unified claim: **DILRMP cannot be understood through any one lens. It is simultaneously a state-legibility project (extending biometric governance), a capitalist-transition project (commodifying land for accumulation), and an institutional-economics project (lowering transaction costs for market exchange) — and the friction between these three logics produces the village-level contradictions documented in Chapter 5.**

That sentence — or your refinement of it — is the _gravitational centre_ of Chapter 2.

### Adjusting the section-by-section logic for V3

Here is how each section now reads when you bring all three traditions to bear:

**§2.1 Functions of land records.** Open with a typology that names all three lenses: legibility, transition, transaction costs. Each lens names a different function (or rather, weights the four functions differently). Use Polanyi's "fictitious commodity" idea as the synthesising concept — land is not naturally a commodity, but is _made_ into one administratively, fiscally, and ideologically. Records do this work.

**§2.2 Global evolution.** A crisp historical arc: medieval/feudal records (Domesday, _terriers_) → early modern cadastres (Napoleonic) → colonial cadastres (in the European empires) → post-WWII titling (in Africa, Latin America, Asia) → neoliberal titling consensus (post-1990s, World Bank, de Soto). Smith and Locke do most of the philosophical work for the early modern transition; Coase, North, Demsetz, de Soto for the contemporary one.

**§2.3 India.** Now reads as a three-layered argument:

- §2.3.1 — Mughal records as fiscal-tributary infrastructure (Habib).
- §2.3.2 — Colonial records as failed capitalist-transition project (Guha + Banaji + Banerjee-Iyer + Cohn). Banerjee-Iyer is now central because it gives you the empirical bridge between V3 (institutional economics) and V2 (Marxist agrarian economy). Both Permanent Settlement and Ryotwari were _bad_ in different ways for different reasons; Banerjee-Iyer measure the long-run fingerprint.
- §2.3.3 — Post-independence records as redistributive-reform-as-paper (Appu + Patnaik + Bhaduri + Lerche + Bardhan). The mode of production debate adjudicates whether Indian agriculture is capitalist or semi-feudal; the institutional-economics literature asks whether secure individual property rights would unlock growth. Both literatures speak to the _same empirical reality_.

**§2.4 Legal-institutional infrastructure.** This section now does explicit transaction-cost analysis: India's deeds-based system has high transaction costs, deters investment, encourages litigation. The Carnegie India argument (already in V1) gets sharpened by Coase and North. But Bardhan and Ostrom remind you that lowering transaction costs is not the only thing that matters — distributive politics, commons, customary rights, and gendered exclusions cannot be solved by titling alone.

**§2.5 Genesis of DILRMP.** DILRMP now appears as the convergence of _four_ currents (one more than in V2):

1. Unresolved post-reform record obsolescence (the "bypassed agrarian transition" — Lerche/Byres).
2. The neoliberal turn in Indian state policy post-1991 (Levien/Harvey/Kohli).
3. The Washington Consensus and its NIE intellectual basis (Williamson/North/Deininger/de Soto).
4. The Digital India / JAM trinity infrastructural turn (Khera/Drèze/Masiero).

The closing paragraph of §2.5 should suggest that Chapter 3 will pursue this convergence as a political-economy problem, while Chapters 4–6 will examine its material outcomes in Odisha villages.

### A final note on tone

Three traditions in a single chapter risks feeling unbalanced — like you're auditioning theories rather than arguing one. The discipline that prevents this: **always make all three traditions answer the same question, in the same paragraph, where it matters.**

For example, on the Permanent Settlement, in §2.3.2, you should write a paragraph that says (paraphrasing): "Three different scholarly traditions converge on a single observation about Permanent Settlement: that it created landlordism without creating productive capitalist agriculture. The agrarian-political-economy tradition (Habib, Guha, Banaji) reads this as the failure of capitalist transition under colonial conditions. The state-legibility tradition (Cohn, Bhattacharya) reads it as a colonial knowledge-construction project that produced 'the landlord' as a juridical subject. The new institutional economics tradition (Banerjee and Iyer 2005) reads it as a property-rights configuration whose long-run consequences for agricultural investment can be empirically measured. These are three different questions about the same historical moment, and they yield three complementary, not competing, accounts."

This is the move that transforms three reading lists into one chapter.
