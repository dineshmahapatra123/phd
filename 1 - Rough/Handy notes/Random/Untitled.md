## §2.1 Land Records and Their Functions

This section establishes _what land records are_ and _what they do_ — laying the conceptual groundwork the rest of the chapter sits on. The argumentative move: land records are not neutral administrative artefacts but instruments of state power that combine fiscal, juridical, economic, and governmental functions in historically variable proportions.

### Foundational theoretical works

- ★★★ **James C. Scott (1998)**, _Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed_, Yale University Press. Especially the introduction and chapters 1–2 on legibility and the cadastral map. The single most important reference for this section.
- ★★★ **Bernard S. Cohn (1996)**, _Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge: The British in India_, Princeton University Press. Read the introduction on "investigative modalities" — survey, enumerative, observational. This pairs perfectly with Scott.
- ★★★ **Michel Foucault (1991)**, "Governmentality" in _The Foucault Effect: Studies in Governmentality_, eds. Burchell, Gordon and Miller, University of Chicago Press. For the move from sovereignty to government — useful for explaining why DILRMP is not just better tax records.

**The point to drive home:** The Mughal _zabt-dahsala_ system established by Todar Mal in 1580 was a sophisticated revenue-assessment system, but it was a _fiscal_ system, not a _property rights_ system in the modern sense. For each pargana, the cultivated area of each crop, the yield in maunds per bigha, and the prevailing market prices over the years from 1570–71 to 1579–80 were averaged. From the resulting average yield, the state demanded one-third in kind, valued at the ten-year average price, and converted into a cash rate per bigha that was then entered into the dastur-ul-amal. This distinction is crucial — your section needs to make it visible.

## §2.5 Genesis of DILRMP

The argumentative move: DILRMP did not arise from a vacuum — it emerged from the convergence of (a) decades of unresolved post-reform record obsolescence, (b) the World Bank/de Soto global titling consensus of the 1990s–2000s, and (c) India's broader Digital India agenda. Tracing this genealogy sets up Chapter 3.

### The lineage scheme by scheme

- ★★★ **Department of Land Resources (various years)**, official documents on:
    
    - Computerisation of Land Records (CLR), 1988-89
    - Strengthening of Revenue Administration and Updating of Land Records (SRA&ULR), 1987-88
    - National Land Records Modernization Programme (NLRMP), 2008
    - DILRMP, 2016 (Central Sector Scheme conversion)
    - 2021–22 to 2025–26 extension
- ★★★ **Solomon Benjamin, R. Bhuvaneswari, P. Rajan, Manjunatha (2007)**, _"Bhoomi: 'E-Governance', Or, an Anti-Politics Machine Necessary to Globalize Bangalore?"_, CASUMM Working Paper. Foundational critical assessment of the _first_ major land records digitisation in India. The Bhoomi program on the digitization of twenty million land records is considered a 'best practice' model of 'e-governance' to be replicated in other parts of India and elsewhere in poor countries. The main findings, at two levels, contrast conventional wisdom. First the digitization of land records led to increased corruption, much more bribes and substantially increased time taken for land transactions. At another level, it facilitated very large players in the land markets to capture vast quantities of land.

## A practical workflow note

You don't need to read all of these. Here's how to approach this list:

First, do a **two-day mapping exercise** before reading anything. Take this list and tag each entry against your sections (you can literally annotate this document). For each section, identify the 3 readings you absolutely must read and the 5–10 you'll use for citation density. That gives you a 25-paper "core" and a 50-paper "support" set — totally manageable.

Second, **build the bibliography first**. Get all the full citations into your reference manager (Zotero/Mendeley) before you start writing. This sounds clerical but it saves you weeks at the end.

Third, **don't read linearly**. For each section, read your one most important book _first_, write the section's outline based on that, then plug in supporting works as you draft. So Habib first (then Mughal section), then Guha first (then Permanent Settlement section), then Appu first (then post-independence section). This protects you from the trap of reading 30 papers before writing a word.

Fourth, **use Bhattacharya (2018), Wahi/Bhatia (2018), and the NCAER Synthesis Report (2020) as your three "wrapper" texts**. Read them first across your whole chapter — they cover the entire arc and will help you decide which deeper readings to invest time in.

Fifth, **the Odisha-specific work is best done after you've drafted §2.1–2.4 in draft form**. Otherwise you risk getting lost in Odisha details that belong in Chapter 4.
