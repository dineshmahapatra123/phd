The desirability of state land titling has been the subject of heated debates in weak states grappling with legacies of conflict and colonisation. 

Proponents of state land titling argue that replacing such informal or customary property rights with individual titles will increase credit access, investment, and productivity by boosting tenure security (de Soto 2000; Feder and Nishio 1998).


Indeed, secure property rights are essential to long-term economic development( Acemoglu,Johnson,andRobinson 2001; North and Thomas 1973). However, others question whether titling will affect property rights security in contexts with weak state capacity.

Thus for fragile states, land titling has been described as premature load bearing for the state (Andrews, Pritchett, Woolcock 2000, 57).

Existing contributions to this debate generally compare written property rights governed by state authority (land titles) with oral, unwritten property rights governed by a customary authority(customary rights).

This approach conflates the authority enforcing land rights and the legibility of those rights. Legibility, or the visibility of land rights to those both outside of and within one’s own community, may be a valuable attribute of property rights on its own, independent of the authority governing these rights. The danger of confounding these attributes of land rights is not merely academic.

This gap in trust between state and customary authorities raises doubt about the efficacy of titling in weak state contexts.

We employ a survey experiment to disentangle how authority and legibility shape Malawian perceptions about land security.

We presented participants with a description of a plot of land in which the buyer, community, and titling characteristics vary and then asked them to evaluate the desirability and security of land tenure. These are meaningful outcomes of interest because they shape actual investment behaviors.

Our findings show that legibility matters even if it is generated by documents that are outside of the state’s direct authority. Research design: Gemini

The full research design of the paper **"Land and Legibility: When Do Citizens Expect Secure Property Rights in Weak States?"** is a carefully structured, large-scale **conjoint survey experiment**.

Because real-world land registries usually conflate _who_ has power (the state) with _how_ it is recorded (written documentation), the authors designed an experiment to systematically decouple **political authority** from **legibility** (documentation).

---

## 1. Sample & Logistical Execution

- **Target Population:** A representative, nationwide sample of **4,893 Malawian citizens** spanning 28 out of 29 districts.
    
- **Timing:** Fielded via phone surveys between **August 21 and October 7, 2020** (a design necessitated by COVID-19, literacy variations, and geographic reach).
    
- **Demographic Context:** The sample primarily consists of rural, low-income citizens (91% reporting financial difficulties; 53% with great difficulties) who face real-world tenure insecurity daily.
    

---

## 2. The Experimental Vignette (The Setup)

Enumerators read a short, hypothetical narrative aloud to respondents about a 35-year-old individual considering purchasing a hectare of land in a rural area similar to the respondent's own.

To isolate what truly drives perceptions of property security, the authors randomized **five distinct attributes** within the script:

Plaintext

```
"Imagine that a 35-year-old [married/single] [respondent's ethnic group] [man/woman] is considering buying a hectare of land. Here's a little bit of information about the land the [man/woman] can buy: [he/she] would be given permission to live on the land from the [Chief / Ministry] [and will be given written documents / blank]. The land is in an area where most people [are / are not] [respondent's ethnic group], and where most people [help / do not help] each other in times of need."
```

### The 5 Randomized Attributes:

1. **The Political Authority (Core Variable):** Permission is granted by either a **Customary Actor** (The Traditional Chief) or a **State Actor** (The Ministry of Lands).
    
2. **The Level of Legibility (Core Variable):** The purchase is either explicitly accompanied by **written documents**, or left completely unmentioned (**blank control**). _Note: Leaving it blank provides a conservative, lower-bound estimate, as respondents might naturally assume a state official includes paperwork by default._
    
3. **Gender & Marital Status:** The buyer is varied to be a single man, married man, single woman, or married woman to account for marginalized demographic vulnerabilities.
    
4. **Social/Ethnic Alignment:** The destination neighborhood is either predominantly **coethnic** or **non-coethnic** with the buyer.
    
5. **Community Cohesion:** The local community is described as a high-social-capital environment (where people help each other) or a low-social-capital environment (where they do not).
    

---

## 3. Measuring the Outcomes (Dependent Variables)

After hearing the narrative profile, respondents were asked to evaluate the situation across three primary post-treatment questions, using a standard scale:

- **Expropriation Risk (Attempt):** What is the likelihood that someone else might try to claim or take the land away from the buyer?
    
- **Institutional Protection (Success):** If someone _does_ try to take the land, what is the likelihood that this expropriation attempt will actually succeed?
    
- **Market Desirability (Recommendation):** Based on the circumstances, would the respondent recommend that the buyer go ahead and purchase this land?
    

---

## 4. Estimation Strategy

The authors analyzed the data using **Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression** to calculate the **Average Marginal Component Effects (AMCE)** and **marginal means** for each attribute.

This statistical approach allowed them to measure exactly how many percentage points an individual attribute (like adding a physical piece of paper) shifts a citizen's perception of land security, independent of all other factors.

### Key Design Strength

By shifting the focus to a hypothetical "buyer" scenario (even though 69% of Malawian land is accessed via inheritance), the authors established a standardized baseline. It forced respondents to judge the _rules of the game_—the interplay of paper trails and power dynamics—rather than relying on their own personal family histories or local status.

# Research Design and Results — Expanded Explanation

## 1. What the Paper Is Trying to Test

The paper asks a very specific question: when people think land rights are secure, are they responding to the **authority** behind the land right, or to the **legibility** of the land right?

Here, **authority** means who grants or recognises the right. In the experiment, this is either a **chief** or the **Ministry of Lands**. **Legibility** means whether the right is written down and therefore visible to others. In the experiment, this means whether the land purchase is accompanied by **written documents**.

The authors argue that land-titling debates usually mix these two things together. A state title is both state-backed and written. Customary land rights are often treated as both non-state and oral. Because of this, earlier research often cannot tell whether citizens value land titles because they trust the state, or because written documentation itself gives them a stronger claim.

The central hypothesis is that **written documentation has value in itself**. It may make land rights feel more secure even when the document is not issued by the state. In other words, citizens may value legibility as a practical resource for defending claims, not only as a sign of state power.

## 2. Why Malawi Is a Good Case

Malawi is useful for this study because it has both state and customary systems of land authority. Most smallholder farmers rely on customary land rights, and chiefs remain important in allocating land, recognising claims, and resolving disputes. At the same time, state land titling exists and is actively debated.

This creates a setting where citizens can compare different kinds of property rights:

- oral customary rights;
- written documents from chiefs or local authorities;
- state-issued titles or statutory documentation.

The paper notes an important trust gap. Citizens in Malawi report much higher trust in customary authorities than in national political institutions. This matters because, if people value state titles only because they trust the state, then state-backed documentation should be much more attractive. But if people value written evidence itself, then documents from chiefs should also matter.

The authors also show that written customary documents already exist in Malawi. In an earlier LGPI survey, some landholders reported having documents allocated by chiefs. These are not full state titles, but they are still useful pieces of evidence in local land relations.

## 3. The Research Design

The authors use a **single-profile conjoint survey experiment**. This means respondents are given one hypothetical land-purchase scenario in which several features are randomly varied. The purpose is to isolate the effect of each feature on how secure or desirable the land seems.

The experiment was embedded in phone surveys with **4,893 adult Malawians**, conducted between **21 August 2020 and 7 October 2020**. The survey covered **28 of Malawi's 29 districts**, so it had broad geographic coverage. Because it was a phone survey, the sample is limited to people with phone access and is somewhat more connected, urban, and educated than the average Malawian population. The authors acknowledge this limitation.

Each respondent heard a short vignette about a person considering buying **one hectare of rural land**. The hypothetical buyer was described as thirty-five years old and from the same ethnic group as the respondent. The scenario then varied several attributes.

## 4. The Randomised Attributes

The experiment varied six main features of the hypothetical land purchase.

**First, authority granting permission.** The buyer would be given permission either by a **chief** or by the **Ministry of Lands**. This tests whether people think land is safer when backed by customary authority or state authority.

**Second, written documents.** The buyer either would be given **written documents**, or the vignette would say nothing about documents. This is the paper's main legibility treatment. The authors treat this as a conservative test because respondents may assume that Ministry of Lands permission normally includes paperwork. If so, the measured effect of written documents is likely an underestimate.

**Third, gender.** The buyer was randomly described as a man or a woman. This lets the authors see whether respondents think women purchasers face greater land insecurity, and whether documents matter more for women.

**Fourth, marital status.** The buyer was either married or single. This matters because marriage and family status can shape land claims, inheritance, and social recognition.

**Fifth, ethnic composition of the area.** The land was either in an area where most people were coethnic with the buyer, or where most people were not coethnic. This tests whether social belonging affects perceived land security.

**Sixth, social capital.** The community was described either as one where most people help each other, or one where most people do not help each other. This tests whether written documents matter more when social support is weak.

## 5. The Outcome Questions

After hearing the vignette, respondents answered three questions.

**Outcome 1: attempted expropriation.** Would someone be likely to try to take the land from the buyer?

**Outcome 2: successful expropriation.** If someone did try to take the land, would they be likely to succeed?

**Outcome 3: purchase recommendation.** Would the respondent recommend that the buyer purchase the land?

The first two outcomes measure perceived tenure security. If respondents think others are less likely to try to take the land, or less likely to succeed if they try, then the land is perceived as more secure. The third outcome measures broader desirability. A respondent might recommend the purchase not only because the land is secure, but also because it seems socially acceptable, administratively safe, or economically worthwhile.

## 6. How to Read Table 4

Table 4 reports **Average Marginal Component Effects**. In simple terms, each coefficient shows how much that attribute changes the probability of a respondent giving a particular answer.

The three columns correspond to the three outcomes:

- column 1: whether someone would try to take the land;
- column 2: whether they would succeed in taking it;
- column 3: whether the respondent would recommend buying the land.

Negative coefficients in the first two columns are good for tenure security because they mean respondents think expropriation is less likely. Positive coefficients in the third column are good for desirability because they mean respondents are more likely to recommend the purchase.

The stars show statistical significance:

- one star means p < 0.05;
- two stars mean p < 0.01;
- three stars mean p < 0.001.

## 7. Main Finding: Written Documents Strongly Improve Perceived Security

The most important result is the row for **Written documents**.

Written documents reduce the perceived chance that someone will try to take the land by **4.47 percentage points**. This effect is highly statistically significant.

Written documents also reduce the perceived chance that a land grab would succeed by **4.32 percentage points**. This is also highly statistically significant.

Written documents increase the probability that respondents would recommend the land purchase by **2.94 percentage points**. This effect is smaller but still statistically significant.

The authors explain the substantive size clearly. Without documents, the expected probability that someone would try to take the land is around 0.15. With documents, this falls to around 0.10. That is a reduction of nearly one-third. Similarly, the expected probability that an expropriation attempt would succeed falls from about 0.13 to about 0.09.

So the finding is not only statistically significant; it is meaningful in practical terms. Written documents make land look safer and more worth buying.

## 9. Authority: Chief vs. Ministry of Lands

The result for **Chief** is more limited.

In column 1, chief permission does not significantly increase the perceived chance that someone will try to take the land. In column 2, chief permission increases the perceived chance that a land grab would succeed by about **2.39 percentage points**, and this is statistically significant. In column 3, chief permission does not significantly affect whether respondents recommend the purchase.

This means respondents see some weakness in chief-backed rights if a dispute actually occurs, but they do not simply reject chief authority. The more important result is that written documents work under both kinds of authority.

The later interaction analysis directly tests whether written documents matter only when they come from the state. The answer is no. The authors find **no evidence that the effect of written documents depends on whether the authority is the chief or the Ministry of Lands**.

This is the paper's key theoretical move. Citizens are not valuing documents only because they are state documents. They are valuing written evidence itself.

## 10. Local Demographics and Coethnicity

The row **Majority are coethnic** is not statistically significant in Table 4. This means that, in the full sample, respondents did not strongly change their assessment simply because the buyer would live in an area where most people shared their ethnicity.

This does not mean ethnicity is irrelevant to land politics in general. It means that, in this specific experiment and with this specific hypothetical land-purchase scenario, coethnicity did not have a clear independent effect on perceived land security or desirability.

The interaction analysis also finds no meaningful interaction between documents and coethnicity. Written documents did not matter only for ethnic outsiders or only for ethnic insiders. Their value was more general.

## 11. Social Capital: Communities Where People Help Each Other

The variable **Help** captures whether the land is in a place where most people help each other.

In Table 4, Help does not significantly reduce the perceived chance of attempted expropriation or successful expropriation. But it does increase the likelihood that respondents would recommend the land purchase by **2.94 percentage points**.

The more important finding appears in the interaction analysis. Written documents matter more in communities where people **do not** help each other. In low-social-capital settings, the effect of documents on reducing attempted expropriation is about **five percentage points larger**, and the effect on reducing successful expropriation is about **four percentage points larger**.

This is theoretically important. It suggests that documents can substitute for weak social support. Where neighbours and community members are not expected to help, a written document becomes more valuable as a defensive resource.

For your thesis, this is directly useful. In village settings, the value of records may depend on whether a household has strong local networks, caste support, kinship backing, or political connections. Those with weaker social support may rely more heavily on documents.

## 12. Gender and Marital Status

In Table 4, the gender and marital-status variables do not have strong direct effects. Respondents did not generally think that women buyers or married buyers were much more or less secure than men or single buyers.

However, the interaction results show that written documents matter more for women on one important outcome. The effect of written documents on reducing successful land grabs was about **four percentage points larger for women buyers than for men buyers**.

The authors interpret this as evidence that respondents see documents as especially useful for less empowered or more vulnerable claimants. Women may not be seen as universally insecure in the vignette, but when a woman's claim is written down, that document provides an additional bulwark against successful dispossession.

Marital status does not strongly condition the effect of written documents. This may be because the scenario describes a land buyer rather than an inheritor. Gendered and marital vulnerabilities may be stronger in inheritance disputes than in purchase scenarios.

## 13. Education of Respondents

The paper also examines whether respondent characteristics shape how people interpret written documents.

Education matters. Respondents with at least some secondary education responded more strongly to written documents. For these respondents, written documents reduced the expected probability that someone would try to take the land by about **ten percentage points**, from around 0.20 to around 0.10. For respondents with less education, the reduction was smaller, about **three percentage points**.

The authors suggest several possible reasons:

- more educated respondents may be more able to read and interpret documents;
- they may place greater symbolic value on writing and paperwork;
- they may be more familiar with statutory systems;
- they may be less dependent on purely customary land arrangements.

But the authors are careful: even less educated respondents still value documents. Education strengthens the effect; it does not create the effect from nothing.

## 14. What the Paper Finds Overall

The paper finds strong support for the first two hypotheses:

1. land with written documents is perceived as more secure;
2. land with written documents is perceived as more desirable.

The paper does **not** find support for the third hypothesis, which expected written documents to matter mainly when permission came from the state. Instead, written documents matter whether the authority is state or customary.

The most important conclusion is that **legibility can be valuable to citizens independently of state authority**. People do not value documents only because the state issues them. They value documents because documents make claims visible, portable, and usable in disputes.

## 15. Theoretical Interpretation

The paper challenges a state-centred reading of legibility.

In James Scott's account, legibility is often a technique through which the state makes society visible for taxation, control, planning, and intervention. Ferree et al. do not reject this. But they add another side: citizens may also want legibility because it helps them defend their claims.

Written documents are useful because they can be shown to others. A document can be taken to a chief, a neighbour, a family member, a government office, a court, a bank, or another forum. It may not guarantee victory, but it gives the claimant evidence.

This is why the authors describe legibility as a **strategic resource**. It is one tool among many that citizens can use to make land claims stronger.

## 16. Policy Implication

The paper warns against assuming that low uptake of formal land titles means people do not want documentation. Citizens may reject state titles because they are costly, individualised, taxable, or dependent on weak state enforcement. But they may still want written evidence of their rights.

This supports policy attention to intermediate and plural forms of documentation:

- customary land certificates;
- community mapping;
- local registers;
- written agreements;
- documents that recognise complex customary tenure rather than replacing it with simple individual title.

But the authors also give a warning. Documentation strengthens the person whose name appears in the document. Those whose names or rights are omitted may become weaker. Therefore, the design of documentation systems matters. If only one owner is recorded, then tenants, co-heirs, women, sharecroppers, secondary rights holders, and common land users may become less visible.

## 17. Direct Relevance to Odisha and DILRMP

For the Odisha land-records thesis, the paper is important because it separates four things that are often collapsed:

1. **Documentation**: Is the claim written down?
2. **Authority**: Who issued or recognises the document?
3. **Recognition**: Which institutions accept the document?
4. **Ground reality**: Does the document match actual possession, boundaries, inheritance, and use?

A Bhulekh RoR printout may not be conclusive title. It may not match possession perfectly. It may exclude tenants or co-heirs. But it can still be valuable because villagers can use it as evidence in welfare claims, mutation, bank interactions, disputes, compensation claims, or family negotiations.

This means DILRMP should not be evaluated only by asking whether it creates perfect title. It should also be evaluated by asking whether it increases or distorts citizens' ability to make claims.

The key fieldwork question becomes: **who gains documentary power from the land record, and whose claims become weaker because they are not written into it?**
