### Upward Continuity

> **"A person who wishes to govern the state well must first learn how to govern himself..."**

- **The Concept:** To rule a nation, a ruler must first master self-control, and then master the management of their own family, wealth, and estate.
    
- **The Flow:** Individual Morality $\rightarrow$ Domestic Economy $\rightarrow$ National Politics.
    
- **Example:** This served as the basis for the education of princes at the time, where a heir would first study a treatise on personal morality, then a book on economics, and finally a political treatise on ruling the state.
    

### Downward Continuity

> **"...when a state is well-governed, the father knows how to govern his family... and the individuals behave as they should."**

- **The Concept:** When a state is run correctly, the central government's effective management filters all the way down to the individual citizen.
    
- **The Flow:** National Politics $\rightarrow$ Domestic Family Units $\rightarrow$ Individual Behavior.
    
- **Example:** The laws and economic directives of the state successfully reproduce good behavior and order within private households and individual lives.

### Why this matters

In traditional sovereignty (Machiavelli's view), the prince's power is completely disconnected and distinct from other types of power. In contrast, the emerging "art of government" argues that political rule is just a grander scale of the exact same skills used to manage a household or one's own self.


### 1. Rousseau’s Problem: Scaling the Family to the State

Foucault highlights a quote by Jean-Jacques Rousseau that captures the core dilemma of the time: **How do you introduce "economy" (which originally meant managing a family and household goods) into the general running of an entire state?** * To govern a state now means applying this meticulous, household-level care to the whole nation.

- It requires exercising a form of **"surveillance and control"** over the inhabitants, their wealth, and their behavior that is just as attentive as a father watching over his family and property.
    

### 2. The Birth of "Economic Government"

Foucault points to the eighteenth-century economist François Quesnay, who coined the term **"economic government."** * Foucault notes that today, this term sounds redundant (tautological) because we assume government and the economy are always linked.

- But back then, it was a breakthrough idea: it meant that the very essence and art of exercising power was to take up "economy" as its main objective.
    

### 3. The Shift in the Word "Economy"

The most important takeaway on this page is the historical shift of the word itself:

- **16th Century:** "Economy" was a _form of government_ (specifically, how to manage a household).
    
- **18th Century:** "Economy" becomes a _level of reality_—a specific, measurable field of intervention (what we today simply call **"the economy"**).
    
### The Core Paradox

As you can see in the text, when Rousseau writes about "Political Economy" in the 18th century, he is wrestling with a linguistic transition. He notes that the word's _original_ meaning is the management of a family, so the modern problem is: **how do we take that same level of meticulous, fatherly surveillance and introduce it into the general running of a state?**

By the time economists like Quesnay start talking about "economic government," the shift is complete. "Economy" is no longer just a metaphor for how a king should treat his subjects like children; it has become a concrete, data-driven field—what we today simply call **"the economy"**—and managing it becomes the primary purpose of the state.

### 1. Sovereignty vs. Government (Territory vs. People)

Foucault draws a sharp line between Machiavelli's style of rule and the new art of government:

- **Machiavellian Sovereignty:** The **territory** is everything. The land itself is the fundamental foundation of the principality. The actual people living on it (whether they are rich or poor, lazy or active) are just minor variables compared to the land.
    
- **The Art of Government:** Territory is _not_ the main focus. Instead, government is about ruling a **"complex composed of men and things."**
    

### 2. What are "Things"?

Foucault clarifies that "governing things" doesn't mean managing inanimate objects like tables or chairs. It means managing **human beings in relation to their environment**. Government must focus on:

- Men in relation to their **wealth, resources, and subsistence** (the economy).
    
- Men in relation to their **climate, irrigation, and the fertility of the land** (the geography).
    
- Men in relation to their **customs, habits, and ways of thinking** (the culture/demographics).
    
- Men in relation to **accidents and misfortunes** like famine, epidemics, and death (public health).
    

### 3. The Metaphor of the Ship

To make this crystal clear, Foucault points to a common metaphor used in these 16th–18th century texts: **governing a ship**.

- Governing a ship doesn't just mean locking down a piece of wood.
    
- It means taking charge of the **sailors**, looking after the **cargo**, and constantly reckoning with external forces like **winds, rocks, and storms**.

### 1. Sovereignty is Inseparable from the Law

Foucault notes that for traditional sovereignty, the law is its absolute and only real instrument.

- **The Goal:** To achieve its aim—which is simply getting subjects to obey the ruler.
    
- **The Loop:** The law demands obedience, and obedience upholds the law. Law and sovereignty are completely locked together.
    

### 2. Government Uses Laws as "Tactics"

In contrast, the art of government is not interested in simply imposing laws on men from above. Instead, it is about **"disposing things."**

- Rather than relying on a rigid "do this or face punishment" decree, a government employs **multiform tactics**.
    
- Interestingly, Foucault points out that a government might still use actual laws, but it treats those laws _as_ tactics. The law becomes just one tool among many used to arrange, incentivize, or nudge things so that specific societal ends (like wealth production or population growth) are achieved.
    

### 3. The Ultimate Turning Point

Foucault summarizes this transition as a massive historical shift in political power:

- **Sovereignty's instrument is internal:** It looks inward, relying strictly on its own intrinsic laws to maintain itself.
    
- **Government's instrument is external:** Its purpose resides entirely in the real-world processes it manages (the economy, health, resources). Therefore, its instruments are a flexible range of tactics rather than a rigid legal code.

# AI Primer

Michel Foucault’s core lecture titled **"Governmentality"** (Chapter 4 of _The Foucault Effect_) tracks a profound, macro-historical transformation in the nature of political power in the West between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. It outlines the shift from a classical model of **territorial sovereignty** to a modern modality of power that he terms **"governmentality"**—the data-driven, administrative management of populations and economic systems.

The following is a comprehensive summary and analytical review of the chapter’s structural sequence and core theses.

---

## 1. The Contextual Crossroads: The Sixteenth-Century Explosion of "Government"

Foucault opens by identifying a sudden historical explosion of interest in the concept of "government" starting in the middle of the sixteenth century. This problem emerged at the crossroads of two massive historical shifts:

- **Macrophysical Centralization:** The collapse of feudal structures and the initial establishment of great, centralized, administrative territorial states.
    
- **Microphysical Spiritual Dispersion:** The religious upheavals of the Reformation and Counter-Reformation, which intensely raised the question of how an individual must be spiritually led and ruled on earth to achieve salvation.
    

Consequently, "government" emerged not merely as a state concept, but as a general problem with diverse, multi-layered applications: the government of oneself (morality), the government of families (economy), the government of children (pedagogies), and finally, the government of the state by the prince (politics).

---

## 2. Defining the Target: The Anti-Machiavellian Literature

To isolate the exact definition of this new "art of government," Foucault analyzes a vast, historically persistent body of literature written between the 16th and 18th centuries that defined itself entirely through its **explicit or implicit rejection of Machiavelli’s _The Prince_**.

In this anti-Machiavellian critique, Foucault notes that Machiavelli's model of power is characterized by structural **discontinuity**:

- **Externality and Transcendence:** The Prince stands in a relation of singular externality to his principality. Whether acquired via inheritance or conquest, the link binding the ruler to his territory is purely synthetic, mechanical, and fragile.
    
- **The Loop of Self-Preservation:** Because the link is fragile, the primary objective of Machiavellian power is entirely circular: **to maintain, reinforce, and protect the prince's grip on his territory and his subjects**. The ensemble of the population is merely a secondary variable.
    

The emerging literature on the "art of government" explicitly sought to replace this model of brute self-preservation with a new form of political rationality.

---

## 3. The Structural Scaffold: Upward and Downward Continuities

In sharp contrast to the discontinuity of traditional sovereignty, the new art of government demanded a **seamless continuity** that bridged all layers of society. Foucault maps this in two directions:

### Upward Continuity

An ascending line stating that a person must first learn how to govern themselves correctly (their moral conduct), then learn how to properly manage their family, goods, and patrimony (economy), after which they will naturally succeed in the grander task of governing the state. This logic governed the pedagogical formation of young princes during this era.

### Downward Continuity

A descending line stating that when a state is well-administered, the central governing principles transmit down into individual behavior and the running of private households. This downward transmission of order to ensure individual compliance began to be called **"police"**.

### The Role of "Economy"

The essential hinge for this continuity was **economy**. In the sixteenth century, "economy" meant explicitly what its Greek roots implied: _the wise and meticulous management of individuals, goods, and wealth within the family by a good father_. The core breakthrough of the art of government was the radical attempt to scale this concept up: **to introduce "economy" into political practice**. To govern a state meant exercising the same level of micro-surveillance and financial control over the entire nation that a father exercised over his household.

---

## 4. The Object of Power: Shift from Territory to "Things"

Foucault references Guillaume de La Perriere’s phrase: _"Government is the right disposition of things, arranged so as to lead to a convenient end."_ This sentence introduces a profound epistemological shift regarding the true target of state power:

- **Sovereignty Rules Territory:** In traditional public law, sovereignty is anchored fundamentally to the **land**. The territory is the absolute base, and the inhabitants are merely its structural variables.
    
- **Government Rules "Things" (Men-in-Relations):** Modern government does not rule land; it manages a **complex composed of men and things**.
    

Foucault clarifies that "things" are actually **human beings in their intricate intersections with reality**. Specifically, government must actively manage:

- Men in relation to their wealth, resources, and subsistence.
    
- Men in relation to their climate, territory, irrigation, and soil fertility.
    
- Men in relation to their customs, habits, and ways of thinking.
    
- Men in relation to accidents, misfortunes, epidemics, famines, and death.
    

To illustrate this, Foucault invokes the historical metaphor of the **ship**: governing a vessel does not mean protecting a piece of wood; it means taking charge of the sailors, looking after the cargo, and constantly calculating external hazards like winds, reefs, and storms.

---

## 5. The Instruments of Power: Tactics over Laws

Because the objects of power changed, the _finality_ (purpose) and _instruments_ of power also radically mutated:

- **The Circular End of Law:** Under sovereignty, the goal of power is simply the "common good," which jurists defined as subjects blindly submitting to and obeying the law. The end of sovereignty is the exercise of sovereignty itself. Its primary weapon is **the Law**, which demands absolute obedience under the threat of punishment.
    
- ==**The Multiform Ends of Tactics:** Under government, the goal is to steer the population toward specific, convenient real-world outcomes: maximizing wealth production, ensuring subsistence, and stimulating population growth. To achieve these diverse aims, government downplays rigid laws in favor of **multiform tactics**==. It utilizes incentives, statistics, and administrative arrangements—treating even actual laws as mere tactics to nudge society toward its structural objectives.
    

---

## 6. Historical Blockages and the Catalyst of "Population"

Foucault notes that although this theory of governance was conceived in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, its real-world implementation remained completely **immobilized and blocked** until the early eighteenth century. It faced two primary obstacles:

1. **Material Crises:** The devastating tensions of the seventeenth century—the Thirty Years War, sweeping peasant and urban rebellions, and catastrophic fiscal revenue strains across European monarchies.
    
2. **The Gridlock of Mercantilism:** While mercantilism was the first concrete attempt to conduct politics as an "art of government" through state knowledge and statistics, it failed to break free. It remained trapped because its final objective was still traditional—increasing the personal power and wealth of the sovereign—and its primary tools remained traditional sovereign instruments (decrees, laws, and rigid regulations).
    

### The Unblocking via Population

==The art of government was finally liberated in the eighteenth century by a series of material shifts: massive demographic expansion, an abundance of money, and an explosion in agricultural production.==

These shifts birthed a new, scientifically measurable reality: **the problem of population**.

The isolation of the "population" completely transformed the field of power in three ways:

- ==**The Death of the Family Model:** Statistics suddenly revealed that a population possesses its own distinct aggregate regularities (irreducible birth and death rates, endemic disease cycles, and economic spirals) that could not be mapped onto the tiny model of the family household==. The family ceased to be the _model_ of government and instead became a privileged, internal _instrument_ used to gather demographic data and run public health campaigns (such as vaccinations).
    
- **The Birth of "The Economy":** ==The science of government shifted the meaning of "economy" off the plane of domestic household management and instead isolated it as an autonomous, measurable sector of national reality: **the economy**==.
    
- **The Shift to Political Science:** Armed with statistics as its primary technical factor, the state moved decisively past the juridical framework of sovereignty. Power transitioned from an "art of government" into a formal **political science**, driven by the birth of **political economy**.
    

---

## Critical Review & Synthesis

Foucault concludes by clarifying that modern political reality does not mean that sovereignty or discipline vanished. Rather, history produced a **deeply complex, interlocking triangle: Sovereignty-Discipline-Government**.

Sovereignty became more acute than ever because the state now had to figure out what juridical and constitutional forms could legitimize a government that was constantly intervening in the lives of its citizens (as seen in Rousseau's _The Social Contract_). Concurrently, **discipline** became deeply valued and intensified because managing a population mass effectively required managing human beings in their absolute depths, details, and micro-behaviors.

Ultimately, this chapter serves as a seminal text because it exposes the exact historical mutation of the modern state. It demonstrates that the contemporary state is not a monolithic, static apparatus, but a historically contingent phenomenon that has been systematically **"governmentalized"**—shifting its core reason for being from the preservation of borders to the calculated optimization of biological life, resources, and economic systems.

## Occasion, Genre, and Opening Frame

This text is a lecture delivered at the Collège de France in February 1978. It was first published in Italian, transcribed and edited by Pasquale Pasquino, before appearing in English in _The Foucault Effect_ (1991). These facts of occasion matter. The text is exploratory and programmatic — not a finished treatise but a thinking-in-public, and Foucault is explicit about this. He opens by acknowledging that in a previous lecture on "apparatuses of security" he had identified a cluster of problems specific to the issue of population, and that those problems required him to take up the question of government. The announced subject is an attempt to begin making "an inventory of this question of government" — a deliberate understatement for a lecture that would become one of the most cited in the entire Foucauldian corpus.

The inventory mode is important for how you read the text. There are passages where Foucault is genuinely sketching, not arguing — where he says things like "I will try to show" or "I believe" or "to put it very schematically." These are not rhetorical hedges but accurate descriptions of a lecture that is generating hypotheses rather than demonstrating conclusions. The reader should treat the text as an opening of conceptual territory rather than a closed theoretical system.

Foucault frames his inquiry with the series: _security–population–government_. He has come to government through population, and he has come to population through apparatuses of security. This genealogical sequence — from security concerns, to the problem of managing populations, to the rationality of government — is the trajectory that the lecture will unpack in reverse, going back to the sixteenth century to show how the modern governmental rationality that operates through these three elements came to be.

## The Explosion of Government as a Problem

Foucault's first substantive observation is historical: the problem of government as a general intellectual and political question _explodes_ in the sixteenth century. Before this, from classical antiquity through the Middle Ages, there was a well-established genre of "advice to the prince" literature concerned with the prince's proper conduct, the means of securing the acceptance and respect of his subjects, obedience to God, and the application of divine law. This genre persists. But from the middle of the sixteenth century to the end of the eighteenth, a new and different literature proliferates — treatises on the _art of government_ — that is neither "advice to the prince" in the old sense nor yet the political science of modernity.

What explains this eruption? Foucault identifies a double historical movement. On one side, the shattering of feudal structures and the emergence of large territorial, administrative, and _colonial_ states. On the other, the Reformation and Counter-Reformation, which raise the urgent question of how one must be spiritually ruled and led on this earth. The problem of government emerges at the intersection of these two currents — at the crossroads of state centralisation and religious dispersion. It is precisely because these two movements are simultaneously intensifying and pulling in opposite directions that the question "how to be ruled, how strictly, by whom, to what end, by what methods" acquires the peculiar intensity it has in the sixteenth century.

What makes this eruption analytically interesting is the _multiplicity_ of domains in which the problem surfaces. Government as a general problem in the sixteenth century involves: the government of oneself (the Stoic revival and the ritualization of personal conduct); the government of souls and lives (Catholic and Protestant pastoral doctrine); the government of children (the problematic of pedagogy); and — almost as an afterthought, the last to be taken up — the government of the state by the prince. "How to govern oneself, how to be governed, how to govern others, by whom the people will accept being governed, how to become the best possible governor" — these questions, in their multiplicity and intensity, are characteristic of the sixteenth century.

**Dissertation Relevance — Chapter 2 (§2.1)**The colonialism reference in Foucault's account of the double movement — "great territorial, administrative and _colonial_ states" — is buried almost as an aside but is theoretically significant. The emergence of governmental rationality and the emergence of colonial administration are, for Foucault, _simultaneous_ historical processes. This complicates any attempt to treat colonial land administration as an aberrant deviation from European governmentality: it is co-constitutive with it. § 3

## Machiavelli as Foil: Sovereignty vs. the Art of Government

Foucault's analytical strategy for defining the new art of government is fundamentally contrastive: he uses Machiavelli's _The Prince_ — or more precisely, the _anti-Machiavellian_ literature that positioned itself against it — as a foil against which the new governmental rationality defines itself. He makes an important preliminary point: Machiavelli was not immediately execrated. He was honoured by contemporaries and successors, and re-emerged again at the beginning of the nineteenth century in the context of Napoleonic politics, Clausewitz's theory of strategy, and the problems of Italian and German territorial unity. It is the centuries _between_ these moments of honour — from the late sixteenth to the end of the eighteenth century — that constitute the "affair" around Machiavelli's work.

The anti-Machiavellian literature takes several forms: explicit praise (Naudé, Machon), frontal attacks from Catholic sources (Ambrozio Politi) and Protestant ones (Innocent Gentillet's _Discours_, 1576), and more implicit critiques (La Perrière's _Miroir Politique_, 1567; Elyott's _The Governor_, 1580; Paruta's _Della Perfezione della Vita politica_, 1579). What unites them across their differences is a shared concern to define an art of government distinct from what they take to be Machiavelli's conception.

What is that Machiavellian conception, as read by these critics? The key principle is what Foucault calls the _relation of singularity and externality_ — of transcendence — between the prince and his principality. The prince acquires his principality by inheritance or conquest, but in no case does he form part of it. He remains external to it. The link binding him to the principality — established through violence, family heritage, treaty, alliance — is in every case a purely synthetic one. There is no fundamental, essential, natural, or juridical connection between prince and principality.

From this external and synthetic relation follow two corollaries. First: the link is fragile and perpetually under threat — from enemies without who seek to conquer the principality, and from subjects within who have no _a priori_ reason to accept the prince's rule. Second: the objective of exercising power is therefore to reinforce, strengthen, and protect the principality — but not understood as "the objective ensemble of its subjects and the territory," but rather as the prince's relation with what he owns, with his inherited or acquired territory, and with his subjects. The _art of being prince_ that Machiavelli espouses has as its object precisely this fragile synthetic link. Its mode of analysis is therefore twofold: identify dangers (their source, nature, severity), and develop the art of manipulating relations of force to ensure the link's protection.

> Foucault's characterisation of Machiavellian analysis: the prince's task is to identify dangers and develop the art of manipulating relations of force in order to ensure the protection of his principality — understood as the link binding him to territory and subjects.— Reconstructed from pp. 89–90

Schematically, then, _The Prince_ as profiled in the anti-Machiavellian literature is essentially a treatise on the prince's ability to keep his principality. This _savoir-faire_ — knowing how to retain one's principality — is precisely what the art of government wants to replace with something different. Foucault is careful to note that whether this reading of Machiavelli is accurate or not is not the essential question. What matters is that the anti-Machiavellian authors were attempting to articulate a different rationality — one intrinsic to the art of government, without subordinating it to the problem of the prince and his relation to the principality of which he is lord and master.

**Dissertation Relevance — Chapters 2 & 3**The Machiavellian sovereign-territory relation maps precisely onto the colonial relationship to Indian land. Colonial sovereignty was exercised over territory as an object of conquest, with subjects as variables on a territorial surface. The colonial settlement apparatus was Machiavellian in Foucault's sense: it sought to reinforce the sovereign's relation to territory and extract surplus from it. § 4

## The Plurality and Immanence of Government

Foucault now develops the contrast through a close reading of Guillaume de La Perrière's _Miroir Politique_ (1567), which he acknowledges is "disappointingly thin in comparison with Machiavelli" but which nonetheless prefigures several important ideas. His first observation concerns vocabulary. La Perrière defines "governor" to include "monarch, emperor, king, prince, lord, magistrate, prelate, judge and the like." And throughout this literature, he notes, one speaks also of "governing" a household, souls, children, a province, a convent, a religious order, a family.

This apparently minor lexical observation carries heavy political implications. Machiavelli's prince — at least as read by these authors — is by definition unique in his principality and occupies a position of externality and transcendence. The new art of government, by contrast, involves practices that are _multifarious and concern many kinds of people_: the head of a family, the superior of a convent, the teacher or tutor of a child or pupil. There are therefore several forms of government, among which the prince's relation to his state is only one particular mode. And crucially, all these other kinds of government are _internal_ to the state or society — it is within the state that the father rules the family, the superior the convent, etc.

Thus, against the Machiavellian model of transcendent singularity, the art of government establishes a principle of _immanent plurality_: multiple forms of government, all internal to the social body. Foucault summarises: "The multiplicity and immanence of these activities distinguishes them radically from the transcendent singularity of Machiavelli's prince."

The typology that La Mothe Le Vayer would develop in the following century — writing educational texts for the French Dauphin — gives this three-part structure its clearest form: the art of self-government (connected with morality); the art of properly governing a family (belonging to economy); and the science of ruling the state (concerning politics). What matters across these three types, Foucault argues, is not their distinctness but their _essential continuity_ — one type flows into the other, in both an upward and a downward direction.

Key Conceptual Move — The Continuity of Government

- **Upward continuity:** Whoever wishes to govern the state well must first learn to govern himself, his goods, and his patrimony. The pedagogical formation of the prince ensures this ascending continuity. This is why La Mothe Le Vayer wrote for the Dauphin first a treatise on morality, then economics, then politics.
- **Downward continuity:** When a state is well run, the head of the family will know how to look after his family, goods, and patrimony — and individuals will in turn behave as they should. This descending transmission from state-government to individual behaviour is beginning at this moment to be called _police_ (in its original, broad sense — the regulation of collective life).
- **The central term:** The government of the family, termed _economy_, is the pivot of this continuity — the model through which governmental logic moves both upward and downward.

§ 5

## Economy, the Family, and the Introduction into Political Practice

Foucault now identifies what he considers "the essential issue in the establishment of the art of government": the introduction of economy into political practice. The art of government is essentially concerned with answering the question of how to introduce into the management of the state the kind of meticulous attention that the good father exercises over his family — how to manage individuals, goods, and wealth within a household, and how to make family fortunes prosper.

This is not a sixteenth-century concern only. Foucault points to Rousseau's Encyclopedia article on "Political economy" as evidence that the same framework persists in the eighteenth century. Rousseau's argument, rendered in paraphrase: the word "economy" can only properly be used to signify the wise government of the family for the common welfare of all — that is its actual original use. The problem is how to introduce it, _mutatis mutandis_, and with all the discontinuities that will need to be observed, into the general running of the state. To govern a state means therefore to apply economy to the level of the entire state — exercising toward its inhabitants, and toward the wealth and behaviour of each and all, a form of surveillance and control as attentive as that of the head of a family over his household and his goods.

Quesnay's phrase "economic government" captures this perfectly, and Foucault notes that it becomes almost tautological: the art of government is the art of exercising power in the form and according to the model of the economy. What makes Quesnay speak of "economic government" at all is that the word "economy" is, during this period, in the process of acquiring its modern meaning — ceasing to signify a form of government (household management) and beginning to designate a level of reality, a field of intervention. Foucault calls this shift "absolutely fundamental to our history." The word economy: from a mode of governing in the sixteenth century, to a domain of reality in the eighteenth.

§ 6

## Governing Things, Not Territory

The second major point Foucault draws from La Perrière is his definition of government: "government is the right disposition of things, arranged so as to lead to a convenient end." The key word is _things_ (_choses_). What does it mean that government concerns things rather than territory?

To answer this, Foucault contrasts the object of Machiavellian/juridical sovereignty with the object of governmental reason. For Machiavelli, and for juridical sovereignty in the tradition from the Middle Ages to the sixteenth century, the objects of power are _territory_ and _its inhabitants_. Sovereignty is not exercised on things but above all on a territory and consequently on the subjects who inhabit it. Territory is the fundamental element — everything else (fertility, population density, wealth) is a variable by comparison with territory itself, which is the very foundation of principality and sovereignty.

In La Perrière's text, by contrast, the definition of government makes no reference to territory. Government concerns things — but not in the sense of opposing things to men. Rather, it is a matter of showing that what government has to do with is a _complex of men and things_. Foucault renders this complex in remarkable detail:

> Government is to be concerned with men in their relations, their links, their imbrication with those other things which are wealth, resources, means of subsistence, the territory with its specific qualities (climate, irrigation, fertility); men in their relation to customs, habits, ways of acting and thinking; and men in their relation to accidents and misfortunes such as famine, epidemics, death.— Reconstructed from p. 93

Foucault illustrates this through the metaphor that these treatises on government consistently invoke: the ship. To govern a ship means clearly to take charge of the sailors, but also of the boat and its cargo; to take care of a ship means also to reckon with winds, rocks, and storms; and it consists in that activity of establishing a relation between the sailors who are to be taken care of and the ship which is to be taken care of, and the cargo which is to be brought safely to port, and all those eventualities like winds, rocks, storms. This is what characterises the government of a ship.

The same applies to governing a household. Governing a family does not essentially mean safeguarding family property. What concerns it is the individuals who compose the family, their wealth and prosperity. It means to reckon with births and deaths, possible alliances with other families, all the things that can be done — this general form of management is what is characteristic of government. By comparison, landed property for the family, and territorial acquisition for the prince, are "only relatively secondary matters." What counts essentially is this complex of men and things; property and territory are merely one of its variables.

Frederick the Great's _Anti-Machiavel_ provides a later example of the same logic. His comparison of Holland with Russia — Russia's vast territory largely uninhabited and unproductive, Holland's tiny marshland producing population, wealth, commercial activity, and a fleet — demonstrates that the wealth of a state is not a function of its territory but of its _population-things complex_. Territory matters only as one component of that complex.

§ 7

## Finality, Tactics, and the Relation to Law

Government has a finality of its own, and it is here that Foucault distinguishes it most sharply from sovereignty. Sovereignty, as defined in philosophical and juridical texts, must aim at the "common good" and the "salvation of all." Pufendorf, a late seventeenth-century example Foucault cites, argues that sovereign authority is conferred on rulers only to allow them to use it to attain or conserve what is of public utility. But when Foucault asks what this "common good" actually consists of in the real content jurists and theologians give to it, he finds something telling: "the common good" means essentially obedience to the law — either the law of the earthly sovereign or that of God, the absolute sovereign.

The logic is therefore circular: the end of sovereignty is the exercise of sovereignty. The good is obedience to the law; hence the good for sovereignty is that people should obey it. This circularity — whatever its theoretical structure or moral justification — comes very close to what Machiavelli said when he stated that the primary aim of the prince was to retain his principality. Sovereignty always comes back to this self-referring circularity of principality.

Against this, the art of government introduces a genuinely new kind of finality. Government is defined as the right manner of disposing things so as to lead not to "the common good" but to an end that is _convenient_ for each of the things being governed. This implies a plurality of specific aims: government must ensure that the greatest possible quantity of wealth is produced; that the people are provided with sufficient means of subsistence; that the population is enabled to multiply. These are specific finalities that become the objective of government as such — they are not a circular return to the form of sovereignty but a set of determinate, observable, measurable outcomes.

To achieve these various finalities, things must be _disposed_. And this leads to one of the most theoretically important sentences in the lecture:

> With sovereignty the instrument that allowed it to achieve its aim — obedience to the laws — was the law itself; law and sovereignty were absolutely inseparable. On the contrary, with government it is a question not of imposing law on men, but of disposing things: that is to say, of employing tactics rather than laws, and even of using laws themselves as tactics — to arrange things in such a way that, through a certain number of means, such and such ends may be achieved.— Reconstructed from p. 95

Foucault marks this as "an important turning point." While the end of sovereignty is internal to itself — sovereignty possesses its own intrinsic instruments in its laws — the finality of government resides in the things it manages and in the pursuit of the perfection and intensification of the processes it directs. The instruments of government, instead of being laws, are a range of multiform tactics. Within the perspective of government, law is not what is important. This is a frequent theme in the seventeenth century; it is made explicit in eighteenth-century Physiocrat texts, which explain that it is not through law but through management of processes that governmental aims are reached.

Finally, Foucault draws one more virtue from La Perrière: the good ruler must have patience, wisdom, and diligence. Patience is illustrated by the king of bees — the bumble-bee — who rules without needing a sting, without a weapon of killing. The good governor does not need the right to kill in order to exercise power. Wisdom is redefined: not knowledge of divine and human laws, of justice and equality, but rather knowledge of things, of the objectives that can and should be attained, and the disposition of things required to reach them. Diligence is the principle that a governor should only govern in such a way that he thinks and acts as though he were in the service of those who are governed — like the head of a family who rises first in the morning and goes to bed last, concerning himself with everything in the household because he considers himself as being in its service.

§ 8

## The Seventeenth-Century Blockage: Sovereignty and Mercantilism

Having established the conceptual contours of the art of government as articulated in the sixteenth century, Foucault turns to why this art of government remained largely theoretical — "blocked and immobilised" — throughout most of the seventeenth century. This is one of the more sophisticated passages in the lecture and is often under-read. The art of government, once conceptually articulated, nonetheless could not develop in a specific and autonomous manner because of two interlocking obstacles: the overwhelming institutional and theoretical weight of sovereignty, and the inadequacy of the family-model as a basis for managing large territorial and fiscal problems.

The art of government finds its first form of crystallisation around the theme of _reason of state_ — understood not in the negative, pejorative sense (the state doing whatever it likes), but in a full and positive sense: the state is governed according to rational principles intrinsic to it, which cannot be derived solely from natural or divine laws or from wisdom and prudence. The state, like nature, has its own proper form of rationality. The art of government must find the principles of its rationality in what constitutes the specific reality of the state. But this "reason of state" formation, right until the early eighteenth century, "acted as a sort of obstacle to the development of the art of government."

Historical factors: the Thirty Years War and its ruin and devastation; peasant and urban rebellions in the mid-century; the financial crisis and crisis of revenues affecting all Western monarchies at the century's end. The art of government could only spread and develop in subtlety in an age of expansion, free from the great military, political, and economic tensions which afflicted the seventeenth century from beginning to end.

Structural factors: so long as sovereignty remained the central question — both as a theoretical problem and as a principle of political organisation — the art of government could not develop in a specific and autonomous manner. The pre-eminence of the problem of the exercise of sovereignty was the fundamental obstacle here.

**Mercantilism** is the key case study. It might be described as the first sanctioned effort to apply the art of government at the level of political practice and knowledge of the state. Mercantilism represents a first threshold of rationality in the art of government — the first attempt to develop a _savoir_ of state that can be used as a tactic of government. For the first time with mercantilism, there is the development of knowledge of the state as a technique of government.

But mercantilism was blocked precisely because it took as its essential objective the might of the sovereign. Its aim was not to increase the wealth of the country per se but to allow the ruler to accumulate wealth, build up his treasury, and create the army for his policies. And the instruments it used were laws, decrees, and regulations — the traditional weapons of sovereignty. The objective was sovereign's might; the instruments were those of sovereignty. Mercantilism sought to reinsert the possibilities opened up by a consciously conceived art of government within a mental and institutional structure — that of sovereignty — which by its very nature stifled them.

The art of government also tried to reconcile itself with the theory of sovereignty by deriving the ruling principles of an art of government from a renewed version of sovereignty theory — and this is where seventeenth-century jurists who formalise or ritualise the theory of the _contract_ come in. Contract theory enables the founding contract — the mutual pledge of ruler and subjects — to function as a theoretical matrix for deriving the general principles of an art of government. But despite playing a very important role in theories of public law — Hobbes is the example — contract theory in practice remained at the stage of formulating general principles of public law. It could not generate the specific, concrete, administrative techniques that a genuine art of government required.

On one side, the framework of sovereignty too large, abstract, and rigid. On the other, the model of the family too thin, too weak, too insubstantial: an economy of enrichment still based on the family model was unlikely to respond adequately to the importance of territorial possessions and royal finance. The art of government was caught between a model that was too large and one that was too small.

§ 9

## Population and the Unlocking of Government

The concept of _population_ is the key that unlocks governmental reason from its seventeenth-century impasse. Foucault argues that there occurred, beginning in the eighteenth century, a subtle process — "which we must seek to reconstruct in its particulars" — through which the science of government, the recentring of the theme of economy on a different plane from that of the family, and the problem of population became interconnected. The three are not simply parallel developments; they are mutually constitutive.

Statistics — which in the mercantilist tradition had worked only within and for the benefit of a monarchical administration functioning according to the form of sovereignty — now gradually reveals that population has its own regularities, its own rate of deaths and diseases, its own cycles of scarcity. Statistics shows also that the domain of population involves a range of intrinsic, aggregate effects — phenomena irreducible to those of the family — such as epidemics, endemic levels of mortality, ascending spirals of labour and wealth. And lastly, it shows that through its shifts, customs, and activities, population has specific economic effects. Statistics, by making it possible to quantify these phenomena of population, demonstrates that this specificity is irreducible to the dimension of the family.

From this follows a fundamental shift in the place of the family within governmental logic. The family disappears as the _model_ of government. What emerges instead is the family considered as an _element internal to population_ and as a fundamental instrument in the government of population. This shift from the level of model to that of instrument is, in Foucault's judgment, "absolutely fundamental." From the middle of the eighteenth century, the family appears in this dimension of instrumentality relative to the population — through campaigns to reduce mortality, to promote marriages, through vaccination programmes. The family becomes the privileged instrument through which information about the population is gathered and through which governmental interventions are transmitted to individual behaviour.

> Prior to the emergence of population, it was impossible to conceive the art of government except on the model of the family — in terms of economy conceived as the management of a family. From the moment when population appears as absolutely irreducible to the family, the latter becomes of secondary importance compared to population, as an element internal to population.— Reconstructed from p. 99

Population now comes to appear as the ultimate end of government. In contrast to sovereignty, whose end is circular (the exercise of sovereignty), government has as its purpose the welfare of the population, the improvement of its condition, the increase of its wealth, longevity, and health. The means government uses are themselves immanent to the population — government will act either directly through large-scale campaigns or indirectly through techniques that make possible, without the full awareness of the people, the stimulation of birth rates, the directing of population flows into certain regions or activities.

Population is simultaneously the subject of needs and aspirations, and the object in the hands of the government. It is aware, vis-à-vis the government, of what it wants but ignorant of what is being done to it. Interest at the level of the consciousness of each individual who goes to make up a population, and interest considered as the interest of the population regardless of the particular interests and aspirations of the individuals who compose it — this double register of interest is the new target and fundamental instrument of the government of population.

Finally, population is the point around which is organised what Foucault calls "the patience of the sovereign" — the population is the object that government must take into account in all its observations and _savoir_, in order to govern effectively in a rational and conscious manner. The constitution of a _savoir_ of government is absolutely inseparable from the constitution of a knowledge of all the processes related to population in its larger sense — which is to say, what we now call the economy. It was through the development of the science of government that the notion of economy came to be recentred onto the different plane of reality which we characterise today as the "economic." And it was through this science that it became possible to identify problems specific to the population.

§ 10

## The Triangle: Sovereignty–Discipline–Government

Before arriving at his formal definition of governmentality, Foucault makes what is perhaps his most important structural argument about the relationship among sovereign power, disciplinary power, and governmental power. He explicitly resists any narrative of simple succession — the replacement of a society of sovereignty by a disciplinary society and the subsequent replacement of that by a society of government. This is a tempting narrative but, he insists, wrong:

> In reality one has a triangle, sovereignty–discipline–government, which has as its primary target the population and as its essential mechanism the apparatuses of security.— Reconstructed from p. 102

The governmental mode does not eliminate sovereignty. On the contrary, the problem of sovereignty was never posed with greater force than at the moment when the art of government became a genuine political science, because it was no longer a matter (as in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries) of trying to derive an art of government from a theory of sovereignty, but instead, given that such an art now existed and was spreading, of trying to see what juridical and institutional form, what foundation in the law, could be given to the sovereignty that characterises a state. Rousseau's two texts illustrate this: his Encyclopaedia article on "Political economy" sets up the problem of the art of government by acknowledging that the old family model can no longer work; his _Social Contract_ then attempts to provide a general principle of government which allows room both for a juridical principle of sovereignty and for the elements through which an art of government can be defined.

Discipline is not eliminated either. Schools, manufactories, armies — all of this can only be understood on the basis of the development of the great administrative monarchies. But discipline was never more important or more valorised than at the moment when it became important to manage a population. Managing a population not only concerns the collective mass of phenomena and the level of its aggregate effects; it also implies the management of population in its depths and its details. The notion of a government of population renders "all the more acute the problem of the foundation of sovereignty (consider Rousseau) and all the more acute equally the necessity for the development of discipline (consider all the history of the disciplines)."

The three modes — sovereignty, discipline, government — are therefore not successive but co-present and mutually reinforcing in a triangle. The question is one of dominance, not substitution: what changes from the eighteenth century onwards is not the disappearance of sovereignty or discipline but the increasing pre-eminence of governmental rationality as the dominant mode within which the other two are exercised.

§ 11

## The Three Definitions of Governmentality

In the lecture's conclusion Foucault proposes that his course of lectures for the year would more exactly have been titled "a history of governmentality" rather than the title he chose ("Security, territory and population"). He then gives what has become the canonical tripartite definition:

Foucault's Three Definitions of Governmentality

- **Definition 1 — The ensemble of power:** The ensemble formed by the institutions, procedures, analyses and reflections, the calculations and tactics that allow the exercise of this very specific albeit complex form of power, which has as its target _population_, as its principal form of knowledge _political economy_, and as its essential technical means _apparatuses of security_.
- **Definition 2 — The long-run tendency:** The tendency which, over a long period and throughout the West, has steadily led towards the pre-eminence over all other forms (sovereignty, discipline, etc.) of this type of power termed government — resulting in the formation of a whole series of specific governmental apparatuses and the development of a whole complex of _savoirs_.
- **Definition 3 — The historical process:** The process, or rather the result of the process, through which the state of justice of the Middle Ages, transformed into the administrative state during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, has gradually become "governmentalised."

These three definitions operate at different levels of abstraction. The first is synchronic — a description of what governmentality is at any given moment. The second is diachronic — a claim about the long historical tendency of Western political development. The third is genealogical — a periodisation of state forms. Taken together, they describe both the mechanics and the history of the governmental mode of power.

§ 12

## The Governmentalisation of the State

Foucault closes with a characteristically provocative inversion. The problem of the state — its genesis, history, advance, power, and abuses — has been attributed excessive value in modern thought, expressed in two ways. The first, immediate and affective, is the lyricism of the _monstre froid_ — the cold monster we see confronting us. The second, paradoxically reductionist, is the form of analysis that reduces the state to a certain number of functions — the development of productive forces and the reproduction of relations of production (an obvious reference to Marxist state theory) — yet this reductionist vision nevertheless invariably renders the state absolutely essential as a target to be attacked and a privileged position to be occupied.

Against both forms, Foucault offers a deflation: the state, no more probably today than at any other time in its history, has the unity, individuality, rigorous functionality, or importance attributed to it. "Maybe, after all, the state is no more than a composite reality and a mythicised abstraction, whose importance is a lot more limited than many of us think." What is really important for our modernity is not the étatisation of society but the _governmentalisation of the state_.

This is the final move of the lecture, and it is theoretically rich. The governmentalisation of the state is a singularly paradoxical phenomenon: the problems of governmentality and the techniques of government have become the only political issue, the only real space for political struggle and contestation — and this is because the governmentalisation of the state is at the same time what has permitted the state to survive. The state is what it is today precisely thanks to this governmentality, which is at once internal and external to the state. It is the tactics of government that make possible the continual definition and redefinition of what is within the competence of the state and what is not, the public versus the private. The state can only be understood in its survival and its limits on the basis of the general tactics of governmentality.

Foucault then sketches a rough typological history of the Western state in three forms: the state of justice, born in the feudal type of territorial regime, corresponding to a society of laws — customs or written laws — involving a whole reciprocal play of obligation and litigation; the administrative state, born in the territoriality of national boundaries in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, corresponding to a society of regulation and discipline; and finally the governmental state, essentially defined no longer in terms of territoriality or surface area but in terms of the mass of its population — its volume and density — with territory figuring only as one among its component elements. This governmental state, bearing essentially on population and making use of the instrumentation of economic _savoir_, corresponds to a type of society controlled by apparatuses of security.

The lecture ends by naming three elements that made possible the production of the fundamental phenomenon of Western history — the governmentalisation of the state: the pastoral model (archaic Christian pastoral care), the new diplomatic-military technique (perfected on a European scale with the Treaty of Westphalia), and, lastly, police (in its old seventeenth- and eighteenth-century sense of the regulation of collective life).

## Critical Assessment

### Strengths

The lecture's principal achievement is the sovereignty/government distinction and the rigorous reconstruction of how governmental reason comes to displace, without replacing, sovereignty as the dominant mode of political rationality in the modern West. The genealogy of "economy" — from domestic management to political economy to the modern economy as an autonomous domain of reality — is accomplished with great parsimony in a very small space. The argument that population is the pivot on which this entire transformation turns is compelling and has generated an enormous subsequent literature.

The tactics/laws distinction is analytically sharp and practically fertile. The claim that government operates by "disposing things" rather than "imposing law" — and that laws themselves become tactics within governmental logic — captures something genuine about how modern administrative states work that neither sovereignty theory nor classical Marxist state theory can easily accommodate.

The triangle of sovereignty–discipline–government is theoretically important precisely because it refuses the tempting succession narrative. Foucault does not claim that we have moved beyond sovereignty or beyond discipline. He claims that governmental rationality has become dominant within a triangle that still contains all three modes. This is a significantly more nuanced, and more empirically defensible, position than any simple "we are beyond sovereignty now" reading.

### Blind Spots

**Colonial difference.** The lecture's entire genealogy is Eurocentric. The colonial world appears only in passing — "great territorial, administrative and colonial states" — in the initial framing. The question of whether colonial governmentality constitutes a fundamentally different rationality (governing colonised populations through mechanisms unavailable for governing metropolitan ones) is not addressed. Partha Chatterjee's work — particularly _The Politics of the Governed_ (2004) and his critique in _The Nation and Its Fragments_ (1993) of what he calls the "rule of colonial difference" — is the essential corrective. For your Odisha fieldwork, where the men-things complex has been managed through a specifically colonial cadastral apparatus, this Eurocentrism is not a minor limitation but a structural one.

**Agency and resistance.** Population in this lecture appears primarily as the object-subject of governmental action — aware of what it wants but ignorant of what is being done to it. There is almost no theory of how populations contest, deflect, rework, or tactically appropriate governmental rationality. Your Chapter 5 household ethnography will generate material that requires theoretical resources beyond what this text provides. Scott's _Weapons of the Weak_ and de Certeau's _Practice of Everyday Life_ are the necessary supplements.

**Land and property.** Foucault treats land as one element within the men-things complex without according it any particular analytical weight. The specific dynamics of land as politically charged, symbolically dense, socially reproductive, and the object of competing claims across caste, gender, and community lines — what your dissertation addresses through the palimpsest argument and through the Odisha-specific histories of OSATIP, PESA, and FRA — require frameworks that Foucault does not provide. The agrarian political economy tradition (Guha, Bhattacharya) and the property rights literature (de Soto, Deininger, Bromley) address this terrain in ways this text cannot.

**Gender.** The family as instrument of governmental rationality is analysed entirely through the figure of the _pater familias_ who rises first and retires last. The gendered construction of both household economy and the governmental subject passes without comment. For Odisha, where women's names remain systematically absent from RoR records and where land titling programmes frequently reproduce male-headed household units, this is not a peripheral omission.

**The state as composite reality.** Foucault's deflation of the state — "a composite reality and a mythicised abstraction, whose importance is a lot more limited than many of us think" — is intellectually provocative but risks underweighting the specific, material, violence-backed operations of the state in contexts where land dispossession is backed by armed force. The Indian state's role in land acquisition for infrastructure, mining, and SEZs in Odisha's adivasi districts cannot be adequately theorised through a framework that demotes the state to a "composite reality."

# Review 2

## Michel Foucault, "Governmentality" (1978) — Scholarly Review

### Overview and Occasion

This text is a lecture delivered at the Collège de France in February 1978, later published in _The Foucault Effect: Studies in Governmentality_ (Burchell, Gordon, and Miller, eds., 1991). It is not a fully worked-out theoretical treatise but a lecture-in-progress — exploratory, programmatic, and occasionally schematic. Foucault himself acknowledges this: he describes the argument as proceeding from a prior lecture on "apparatuses of security" and announces that subsequent lectures will fill out what he only sketches here. The reader must therefore receive it as a theoretical opening rather than a closed argument. That caveat does not diminish its importance — this lecture is one of the most cited texts in the entire Foucauldian corpus — but it matters for how you deploy it analytically.

---

### Section-by-Section Analysis

#### 1. The Problem of Government as a Historical Eruption

Foucault opens by identifying the sixteenth century as the moment when "government" becomes a general _problematic_ — not just the government of a state, but the government of oneself, of children, of souls, of families, of a province, of a convent. He is tracking a discursive explosion: from the mid-sixteenth to the end of the eighteenth century, a vast literature on the "art of government" proliferates that is neither the old "advice to the prince" genre nor yet modern political science.

The historical context he gives for this explosion is a double movement: the consolidation of large territorial-administrative-colonial states on one hand, and the religious dispersions of the Reformation and Counter-Reformation on the other. At the intersection of centralisation and dissidence, the question _how should one be governed?_ acquires unprecedented intensity. This framing is historically important for your Chapter 2 — it situates the emergence of governmental reason in the same period as early colonial territorial expansion, linking the art of government structurally to the problem of administering distant territories and populations.

#### 2. Machiavelli as Foil

The bulk of the first movement of the argument uses Machiavelli's _The Prince_ — or rather, the _anti-Machiavellian_ literature that positioned itself against it — as a device to define what the new art of government is _not_. Foucault is less interested in what Machiavelli actually argued than in how he functioned as a theoretical target.

The Machiavellian prince, as these critics read him, stands in a relation of _singularity and externality_ — even _transcendence_ — to his principality. The prince acquired the principality through inheritance or conquest; he does not form part of it. The link between prince and principality is therefore synthetic, fragile, and perpetually under threat from without and within. The entire logic of Machiavellian statecraft follows from this: identify dangers, manipulate relations of force, reinforce the link. The end of sovereignty, Foucault argues, is circular — sovereignty exists to preserve sovereignty.

Against this, the anti-Machiavellian authors propose that governing is not the exercise of a singular, transcendent power over a territory. Drawing on Guillaume de La Perrière's _Miroir Politique_ (1567), Foucault shows that "governor" in this new literature signifies something immanent and multiple — the head of a family, a magistrate, a prelate, a teacher. Government is _plural and internal_ to the social body, not singular and external to it. This move from transcendence to immanence is theoretically foundational for the entire concept of governmentality.

For your dissertation, the Machiavellian-versus-governmental contrast maps productively onto the difference between a colonial sovereign relation to land and a governmental one. The colonial land record as an instrument of sovereignty — extractive, externally imposed, relating to territory as an object of conquest — is precisely the Machiavellian mode. DILRMP's rhetoric of "service delivery," "welfare," and population management gestures toward the governmental mode, even while its underlying power geometry often reproduces the sovereign one. This is a productive tension worth exploiting.

#### 3. The Economy of the Family and the Transition to Population

Foucault introduces what he calls "the essential issue in the establishment of the art of government": the transposition of _economy_ from the household to the state. The art of government is defined as the introduction of the meticulous management practices of the good _pater familias_ into the running of the state as a whole. La Mothe Le Vayer's pedagogical sequence for the Dauphin — morality (governing oneself), then economics (governing the family), then politics (governing the state) — illustrates the upward continuity that characterises the art of government. The downward continuity, through which the well-governed state produces well-governed families and individuals, begins at this moment to be called _police_ in its original, broad sense (the regulation of collective life).

This family-as-model of government then encounters its own limits. Foucault's argument pivots: the emergence of _population_ as an irreducible datum — possessing its own regularities, mortality rates, disease cycles, and economic effects — shatters the family-model. Statistics (originally _Staatswissenschaft_, the science of the state) gradually reveals that population cannot be reduced to an aggregation of families. It has emergent properties. From this moment, the family is demoted from _model_ to _instrument_ — a privileged instrument through which information about the population is gathered and through which governmental campaigns (on mortality, marriage, vaccination) are transmitted. This is one of Foucault's most elegant analytical moves: the family does not disappear, it is _repositioned_ from the macro-logic of government to its micro-instrumentality.

The genealogy running from domestic economy → political economy → the "economy" as an autonomous domain of reality is traced here with great parsimony. Quesnay's phrase "economic government" captures the moment of near-tautology: to govern is to manage an economy; governing _just is_ an economic practice. And from this moment, "the economy" begins to crystallise as a distinct level of reality with its own laws — the field into which liberal government will subsequently intervene.

For Chapter 2, this genealogy matters because it shows _how land records fit into governmental rationality_: land is not merely a territorial datum (Machiavellian sovereignty) but a component of the men-things complex that governmental reason seeks to manage. Agricultural productivity, tenancy relations, fertility, irrigation — these are precisely the objects of settlement surveys, ryotwari systems, and their digital successors. DILRMP's aspiration to make every parcel legible is continuous with the art of government's fundamental orientation toward "disposing things" rather than ruling territories.

#### 4. Governing Things, Not Territory

One of the most analytically powerful passages in the text is Foucault's reading of La Perrière's definition: _"government is the right disposition of things, arranged so as to lead to a convenient end."_ The key word is _things_. Foucault contrasts the Machiavellian-juridical concept of sovereignty — which takes _territory_ as its primary object — with governmental rationality, which takes a _complex of men and things_ as its object. Things here means: men in their relations to wealth and resources; men in their relations to customs and habits; men in their relations to accidents and misfortunes (famine, epidemics, death).

The ship metaphor is illuminating: to govern a ship is not to own it but to manage the relation between sailors, cargo, weather, and destination. To govern a household is not to safeguard its property but to reckon with births and deaths, possible alliances, the prosperity of those who compose it. Property and territory are, in both cases, variables rather than foundations.

This has direct force for your cadastral work. The Bhulekh/Bhunaksha apparatus of DILRMP does not merely seek to catalogue territory. It seeks to produce a knowable, manageable relation between population and land — to render legible the men-things complex at the parcel level. Scott's argument about cadastral maps creating (rather than describing) property systems is the field-level specification of precisely this Foucauldian point. The cadastral map is the governmental instrument par excellence — it "disposes things" so that specific ends (credit access, welfare targeting, taxation, land market development) may be achieved.

#### 5. The Tactics/Laws Distinction

A theoretically important passage concerns the distinction between sovereignty's instrument (law) and government's instruments (tactics). Sovereignty operates through law — its instrument and its end are unified. Government operates through _tactics_: specific arrangements of things designed to achieve specific ends. Laws, in the governmental mode, become one tactic among others rather than the constitutive form of power.

Foucault argues that government seeks to achieve its ends "not by imposing law on men, but by disposing things: that is to say, employing tactics rather than laws, and even using laws themselves as tactics." This is a theoretically sharp formulation. For your dissertation, it illuminates how DILRMP operates: the legal infrastructure (Record of Rights, mutation laws, land ceiling acts) is increasingly deployed _as a tactic_ in the service of broader governmental aims (financial inclusion, welfare delivery, investment facilitation) rather than as the constitutive framework of property rights. The Digital India apparatus converts law into interface, into database protocol, into algorithmic eligibility trigger — exactly the tactical mode Foucault describes.

#### 6. The Blockage: Sovereignty, Mercantilism, and the Seventeenth-Century Impasse

Foucault argues that the art of government, though conceptually articulated in the sixteenth century, remained _blocked_ for most of the seventeenth. Two forces impeded it: first, the overwhelming weight of sovereignty as the dominant political framework (reinforced by the Thirty Years War, peasant rebellions, and fiscal crises); second, the inadequacy of the family-model as a basis for managing large territorial and fiscal problems.

Mercantilism is the key case study in this blockage. It represents the first attempt to apply governmental rationality at the level of the state, developing a _savoir_ of state as a tactic of government. But it was ultimately trapped by sovereignty's framework: its objective was the sovereign's power and treasury, its instruments were laws and decrees (the traditional weapons of sovereignty). Mercantilism sought to apply governmental logic through sovereign instruments — and this contradiction stifled it. Contract theory (Hobbes) provided another attempted reconciliation that remained stuck at the level of public law formulation.

This analysis has comparative value for your Chapter 3 on the political economy of land records modernisation in India. Colonial revenue settlements (especially the ryotwari and zamindari systems) exhibit precisely this mercantile structure: they developed sophisticated _savoir_ of land and population (surveys, settlement reports, revenue statistics) but ultimately bent this knowledge toward sovereign extraction. The colonial cadastral apparatus was governmental in its instruments (surveys, statistics, the disposal of things) but mercantilist in its objectives (maximising revenue for the colonial treasury). Post-colonial Indian land administration inherits this ambiguity, and DILRMP — with its welfare-delivery and financial-inclusion rhetoric — represents a new attempt at the governmental mode that may face analogous internal contradictions.

#### 7. The Emergence of Population and the Unlocking of Government

Population is the key that unlocks governmental reason from its seventeenth-century impasse. Foucault argues that population's emergence as a datum with its own regularities (death rates, disease cycles, scarcity patterns, demographic rhythms) does three things simultaneously: it displaces the family as the _model_ of government (replacing it with population as both the _object_ and the _subject_ of governmental action); it enables the recentring of "economy" onto a new plane of reality (the economy in the modern sense, no longer household management); and it allows the problem of government to be thought _outside_ the juridical framework of sovereignty.

The governmental triangle that Foucault extracts from this analysis — sovereignty / discipline / government — is his final theoretical formulation. He resists any simple succession narrative (sovereignty replaced by discipline, discipline replaced by government). Instead, the three coexist as a triangle, with government becoming increasingly dominant, having population as its primary target and apparatuses of security as its essential mechanism. The three-part typological scheme — state of justice (feudal, law), administrative state (fifteenth-sixteenth centuries, regulation and discipline), governmental state (eighteenth century onwards, population and economic _savoir_) — provides a rough historical cartography of this shift.

#### 8. The Definition of Governmentality

The lecture concludes with Foucault's tripartite definition of governmentality:

First, the _ensemble_ of institutions, procedures, analyses, reflections, calculations, and tactics that allow the exercise of the specific form of power whose target is population, whose principal form of knowledge is political economy, and whose essential technical means are apparatuses of security.

Second, the _tendency_ over a long period throughout the West toward the pre-eminence of this type of power (government) over all others (sovereignty, discipline), resulting in the formation of specific governmental apparatuses and the development of complex _savoirs_.

Third, the _process_ through which the medieval state of justice, transformed into the administrative state in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, has gradually become "governmentalised."

The lecture's final provocation is characteristic of Foucault at his best: what matters about modernity is not the _étatisation_ of society (the absorption of social life by the state) but the _governmentalisation_ of the state — the way the state has survived and transformed precisely through the diffusion of governmental rationality, internal and external to itself, which continually redraws the boundary between public and private.

The text refuses both state-phobia and state-centrism, arguing that what matters is not the state as such but the _governmentalisation_ of the state — a formulation that allows analysis of power that moves through and beyond formal state institutions.

# Review 3
Foucault's chapter is a compact genealogy of modern government. Its core move is to show that "government" is not identical with sovereignty, law, administration, or the state. Government is a historically formed art of arranging people, things, wealth, territory, habits, resources, circulation, risks, and welfare toward particular ends.

The chapter begins with the "art of government" literature that developed from the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries. This literature, Foucault argues, was not merely advice to princes. It marked the emergence of a new political problem: how to govern oneself, souls, children, households, families, and finally the state. The "government of the state by the prince" appears as only one instance within a wider problem of conduct and management.

The decisive shift is away from the prince's fragile external relation to territory and subjects. In the Machiavellian model as Foucault reconstructs it, the prince must preserve the principality. In the governmentality model, the problem is different: how to dispose a complex set of people and things so that multiple ends can be achieved. The target is not simply obedience to law or retention of territory. The target becomes prosperity, subsistence, wealth, health, longevity, circulation, and population.

The chapter culminates in Foucault's famous definition of governmentality: a form of power whose target is population, whose knowledge is political economy, and whose technical means are apparatuses of security. This does not mean sovereignty disappears. Nor does discipline disappear. Instead, Foucault gives a triangle: sovereignty, discipline, government. Modern power is their arrangement, not a simple sequence.

## Conceptual Architecture

### 1. Art of Government

The chapter is built around the emergence of an "art of government" from the sixteenth century onward. Foucault treats this as a distinctive rationality, not a timeless feature of rule.

Quote anchor: "art of government" (pp. 87-90).

The art of government is important because it breaks the monopoly of sovereignty as the central way of imagining political power. Instead of asking only how a ruler keeps a territory, it asks how conduct is directed across multiple scales: self, family, institution, population, and state.

### 2. Anti-Machiavellian Problem

Foucault uses Machiavelli as a foil. The Prince, in his reconstruction, is about the prince's relation of externality to the principality and the need to preserve that relation against threats. Governmental literature distances itself from this.

The point is not whether Foucault gives the "true" Machiavelli. The point is that early modern writers defined government against a model of rule centred on possession, conquest, territory, and the prince's survival.

Conceptually, this lets Foucault move from sovereign retention to administrative disposition.

### 3. Continuity of Government

Foucault contrasts sovereignty's discontinuity with governmentality's continuities. Sovereignty draws a line between the prince's power and other powers. Government establishes continuities upward and downward.

Upward continuity: one who governs the state must first govern self, family, goods, and patrimony.  
Downward continuity: when the state is well run, the family and individuals conduct themselves properly.

This is where "police" appears: not merely crime control, but the downward transmission of good government into the conduct of individuals and households.

### 4. Economy

Economy is the hinge concept of the chapter. In the sixteenth century, economy means the proper management of the household. By the eighteenth century, it becomes a distinct field of reality and intervention.

Quote anchor: "introduction of economy into political practice" (p. 92).

This transformation matters because it marks the movement from family-as-model to economy-as-domain. Government becomes the art of exercising power according to the model of economy, and later over "the economy" as an object.

### 5. Things

Foucault's discussion of La Perriere's phrase - government as the right disposition of things - is the chapter's conceptual centre. "Things" does not mean objects instead of people. It means people in relation to resources, territory, climate, fertility, customs, habits, ways of acting, accidents, famine, epidemics, and death.

This is the crucial expansion of the object of government. Government does not simply command subjects. It manages relations among persons, material conditions, events, risks, and processes.

For land governance, this is the line that matters most: land is not just territory. It is a variable in a wider field of relations among people, subsistence, wealth, inheritance, cultivation, welfare, credit, acquisition, and dispute.

### 6. Sovereignty's Circularity

Foucault argues that sovereignty's end is circular: the good of sovereignty is obedience to law, and law is the instrument of sovereignty. Government has a different finality. It seeks multiple specific ends internal to the things governed: wealth, subsistence, multiplication of population, and improvement of condition.

Quote anchor: "obedience to the law" (p. 95).

The point is not that law disappears. Government can use law, but law becomes one tactic among others.

### 7. Tactics Rather Than Laws

Government differs from sovereignty because it works through arrangement, instruments, and tactics. Laws become usable means rather than the whole essence of rule.

Quote anchor: "employing tactics rather than laws" (p. 95).

This is a major concept for modern administration: government can operate through campaigns, statistics, incentives, categories, registers, classifications, eligibility rules, standards, and interfaces. These need not look like sovereign command, but they direct conduct.

### 8. Statistics

Statistics appears first as "the science of the state" and later as a technical factor of government. It reveals population as an aggregate reality with its own regularities: mortality, disease, scarcity, labour, wealth, customs, activities, and economic effects.

Quote anchor: "statistics" as "science of the state" (p. 96).

Statistics is not merely measurement. It helps produce population as a knowable and governable object.

### 9. Population

Population is the chapter's key object. It unblocks the art of government because it displaces the family as the model of government. The family becomes an instrument within population, not the model for governing.

Quote anchor: "ultimate end of government" (p. 99).

Population is both subject and object: it has needs and aspirations, but it is also acted upon by government. This double character is central to welfare, planning, public health, and development administration.

### 10. Political Economy

Political economy arises when population, territory, and wealth are perceived as a network of continuous relations. It is the knowledge form proper to government.

Political economy is not just economics. In Foucault's chapter, it is the intellectual form through which government sees population, wealth, and territory as governable relations.

### 11. The Triangle

Foucault rejects a simple sequence: sovereignty replaced by discipline, discipline replaced by government. Instead, modernity is a triangle: sovereignty, discipline, government.

Quote anchor: "sovereignty-discipline-government" (p. 102).

This is the best corrective against over-simple readings. Governmentality does not mean the end of law or coercion. It means law, discipline, and security are rearranged around population and economy.

### 12. Governmentality

Foucault defines governmentality in three ways:

1. A complex ensemble of institutions, procedures, analyses, reflections, calculations, and tactics.
2. A historical tendency toward the pre-eminence of government over sovereignty and discipline.
3. The process through which the administrative state becomes governmentalized.

The key elements are target, knowledge, and means: population, political economy, and apparatuses of security.

### 13. Governmentalization of the State

Foucault warns against overvaluing "the state." The state is not a unified essence. It survives by being governmentalized - constantly redefined through tactics that determine what falls inside or outside state competence, public versus private, intervention versus non-intervention.

Quote anchor: "governmentalization of the state" (p. 103).

This is the chapter's most powerful political insight: the modern state is not merely stronger because it commands more; it survives because governmentality continually redefines its domains of action.

# Review 5

Michel Foucault’s "Governmentality" (originally delivered as a lecture at the Collège de France in February 1978 and published in *The Foucault Effect* in 1991) is a seminal genealogical study of modern political power. Foucault's central thesis is that the modern state is not a monolithic, pre-existing container of power that commands subjects through law alone. Instead, the state survives, expands, and defines its limits through the historical emergence of a distinct rationality: **governmentality**.

Foucault traces the emergence of the "art of government" from the sixteenth to the eighteenth centuries, showing how political thought shifted away from the medieval and classical model of sovereignty (typified by Machiavelli’s *The Prince*) toward a model centered on the disposition of people, resources, and processes. Where the prince's primary concern was maintaining a fragile, external relation of possession over a territory and its subjects, the art of government introduces "economy" into political practice—managing the state with the same meticulous care that a father exercises over his household.

The core argument develops through a series of conceptual moves:
1. **The Shift from Sovereign Territory to the "Government of Things"**: Government is defined as the "right disposition of things, arranged so as to lead to a convenient end" (La Perriere, quoted on p. 93). Foucault shows that these "things" are actually a complex web of men in their relations, links, and intersections with resources, customs, habits, territory, climate, and accidents (such as famine, disease, and death).
2. **The "Unblocking" of the Art of Government by Population**: Early modern attempts to apply an art of government were blocked by the rigid framework of sovereignty and the conceptual weakness of using the family as a model. The demographic and agricultural expansion of the eighteenth century, coupled with the emergence of **statistics**, isolated a new reality: the **population**. Population possesses its own regularities, mortality cycles, and economic aggregate effects that cannot be reduced to the household. The family shifts from being the *model* of government to a mere *instrument* within it.
3. **The Sovereignty-Discipline-Government Triangle**: Foucault rejects a linear historical narrative where sovereignty is replaced by discipline, which in turn is replaced by government. Modern political reality is a dynamic triangle: *"sovereignty-discipline-government"* (p. 102), where security apparatuses target population using political economy as its knowledge form, while law and disciplinary systems are repositioned within this wider field.
4. **The Governmentalization of the State**: The state is not a unified, static entity but a "composite reality" (p. 103). It is the historical process of "governmentalization" that has allowed the state to survive, by providing the general tactics through which the boundary between state competence and private life, public intervention and market freedom, is constantly defined and redefined.

## 2. Conceptual Breakdown

### 2.1 The Art of Government vs. Machiavellian Sovereignty
Foucault uses the anti-Machiavellian literature of the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries as a foil to define the art of government. The Machiavellian prince stands in a relation of *"singularity and externality, and thus of transcendence, to his principality"* (p. 89). Because the prince's link to his territory is synthetic, fragile, and constantly threatened, the objective of sovereign power is circular: to reinforce, protect, and retain the principality itself. 
In contrast, the "art of government" focuses on immanent, plural, and distributed practices. Government is not unique to the prince; it is practiced by the father of a family, the teacher of a child, the superior of a convent, and the magistrate of a province.

### 2.2 Upward and Downward Continuity
The art of government is characterized by a double continuity:
* **Upward Continuity**: A ruler must first learn to govern himself, his goods, and his patrimony (morality and economy) before he can govern the state (politics). This is established through the pedagogy of the prince.
* **Downward Continuity**: A well-run state ensures that individuals and families conduct themselves as they should. This downward transmission is what early modern writers termed **police** (p. 92)—the detailed administrative regulation of the behavior, occupations, and security of the population.

### 2.3 The Metamorphosis of "Economy"
A crucial conceptual pivot is the transformation of the word "economy." Originally meaning the wise management of the family household (as in Rousseau's *Encyclopaedia* article on "Political Economy"), it was translated to the state level as "economic government" (Quesnay). By the late eighteenth century, the family model is discarded, and "the economy" emerges as a distinct, autonomous field of reality and scientific calculation, giving birth to *political economy* as the science of managing the relations between population, territory, and wealth.

### 2.4 The Government of "Things"
By analyzing Guillaume de La Perriere’s definition of government as the "right disposition of things," Foucault redefines the very target of power:
> *"What government has to do with is not territory but rather a sort of complex composed of men and things... men in their relations, their links, their imbrication with those other things which are wealth, resources, means of subsistence, the territory... customs, habits... accidents and misfortunes such as famine, epidemics, death, etc."* (p. 93)
This concept is illustrated by the **ship metaphor** (p. 93): governing a ship does not mean safeguarding the vessel's structure; it means taking charge of the sailors, the cargo, the winds, the storms, and the reefs, and bringing the ship safely to port.

### 2.5 Tactics Rather Than Laws
Sovereignty operates through **the law**, and its end is circular: obedience to the law is the ultimate good of sovereignty. Government, however, has a finality of its own—convenient ends for the things governed (wealth production, health, population growth). To achieve these, government relies on **tactics**:
> *"With government it is a question not of imposing law on men, but of disposing things: that is to say, of employing tactics rather than laws, and even of using laws themselves as tactics—to arrange things in such a way that, through a certain number of means, such and such ends may be achieved."* (p. 95)
This does not mean laws disappear; rather, they are demoted from the essence of authority to tools used strategically alongside statistics, administrative codes, and security mechanisms.

### 2.6 Statistics and the Emergence of Population
Statistics was originally the "science of the state" (p. 96), operating within the administrative frame of sovereignty. However, the demographic and economic expansions of the eighteenth century transformed statistics. It began to reveal that **population** has its own regularities, aggregate dynamics, mortality cycles, and wealth spirals that cannot be reduced to the household. Population becomes the ultimate end of government—its welfare, longevity, and wealth are the targets. The family ceases to be the model and becomes an instrument to gather information and implement campaigns (such as vaccinations or marriage promotions).

### 2.7 The Sovereignty-Discipline-Government Triangle
Foucault warns against a historicist reading that posits a sequential transition of power:
> *"We must not understand the transition from a state of justice to an administrative state and then to a state of government in terms of the replacement of one by the other... we have a triangle: sovereignty-discipline-government, which has as its primary target the population and as its essential mechanism the apparatuses of security."* (p. 102)
Modernity is characterized by this layered arrangement. Disciplinary systems (schools, barracks, offices) and legal-sovereign acts are reorganized and intensified to manage the aggregate population.

## 3. Thesis Relevance: Land Records Modernisation

Although Foucault’s analysis is historically and geographically rooted in early modern Europe, his conceptual architecture provides a powerful theoretical lens to analyze the **Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme (DILRMP)** and the digital governance of land in Odisha.

### 3.1 DILRMP as a Governmental Technology (Ch. 1.1 & Ch. 3.2)
NE-property rights (NIE) frameworks view land-record computerisation as a neutral, transaction-cost-reducing project. Foucault's framework helps deconstruct this: DILRMP is a **governmental technology**. It is an "investigative modality" that seeks to make the complex, overlapping, and customary relations of rural land visible and manageable. By mapping cadastral parcels and digitising the Record of Rights (RoR), the state is not merely "reflecting" ground reality; it is constructing a legible, standardized, and calculable domain of intervention.

### 3.2 The Relational Character of Land: "Men and Things" (Ch. 2.1)
Foucault’s definition of the "government of things" as the "imbrication of men and things" (p. 93) directly maps onto the institutional function of the RoR. An RoR is not merely a record of physical territory (cadastral plot). It is a record of **relations**:
* Peasant claimants in relation to partition, inheritance, and sale (customs and habits).
* Cultivators in relation to agricultural credit, crop insurance, and markets (wealth and subsistence).
* Households in relation to drought compensation, land acquisition, and state disputes (accidents and misfortunes).
By viewing land records through this lens, the thesis can argue that land administration is the management of a relational complex, where physical territory is only one variable among others.

### 3.3 Portals and Dashboards as "Tactics Rather Than Laws" (Ch. 3.4 & Ch. 4.3)
A key thesis question is whether digitisation resolves ground-level conflicts or merely restates them digitally. Foucault’s "tactics rather than laws" (p. 95) concept is critical here. The postcolonial Indian state has failed to execute formal statutory land reforms (such as moving from presumptive to conclusive title). Instead, it employs **governmental tactics**:
* The creation of digital databases and portals like **Bhulekh** and **Bhunaksha**.
* The introduction of the **ULPIN** (Unique Land Parcel Identification Number) as a spatial-textual anchor.
* The development of real-time monitoring dashboards tracking mutation rates and pendency.
These platforms do not change the underlying legal status of the land (which remains presumptive), but they reshape the tactics of administrative access, dispute, and verification, steering citizen and bureaucratic conduct.

### 3.4 RoRs as Welfare Filters: The Population as Target (Ch. 3.3 & Ch. 5.8)
Under modern cash-transfer schemes like **PM-Kisan**, **CM-Kisan**, and Odisha's **KALIA**, the RoR has been repurposed. It is no longer just a revenue-liability ledger (its colonial design) or a presumptive title document. It is now a **welfare eligibility gate**. 
This is the ultimate expression of Foucault's claim that the population is the ultimate end of government. The state uses the family/household as an instrument of information, sorting the rural population through the digital RoR database to distribute direct benefit transfers (DBT), thereby managing the welfare, subsistence, and security of the agrarian population.

## 4. Critical Academic Appraisal

### 4.1 The Limits of Eurocentrism
Foucault’s genealogy is explicitly Western, tracing the development of power from the Christian pastoral model, through the Treaty of Westphalia, to mercantilism and the European science of police. It completely ignores the colonial path-dependence of power. 
In the Indian context, the "governmentalization of the state" was not a purely domestic, evolutionary process. It was a colonial imposition. As Bernard Cohn’s work shows, the British constructed their investigative modalities (censuses, surveys, settlements) as tools of conquest and extraction. The postcolonial Indian state inherited this colonial administrative architecture, which remains structurally designed as a revenue-extraction machine rather than a title-protection agency. The thesis must, therefore, mediate Foucault through Cohn and Scott to account for this colonial difference.

### 4.2 The Role of the Intermediary
Foucault's account of statistics and economic savoir can feel overly clean, as if the state acts directly and transparently on the population. In reality, as the Odisha village studies demonstrate, digital systems are mediated by local revenue officials (Tehsildars, Revenue Inspectors, Amins) and informal brokers. The "digital gaze" is constantly disrupted by corruption, rent-seeking, and local power dynamics. Foucault’s framework must be supplemented by an institutional ethnography of the local bureaucracy to show how these digital "tactics" are negotiated on the ground.

*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ2** (Outcomes and uses of RoRs) | **Debate 8** (Ownership vs. possession).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Government is not merely the state apparatus; it is a general problem of conduct. The thesis can use this to explain how land documents shape how citizens govern their inheritances and represent themselves to the state.

### 3. The Centralisation-Dispersion Intersection
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"There is a double movement, then, of state centralization on the one hand and of dispersion and religious dissidence on the other: it is... at the intersection of these two tendencies that the problem comes to pose itself with this peculiar intensity, of how to be ruled, how strictly, by whom, to what end, by what methods..."* (Page 88)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.4 (Legal and revenue infrastructure) & Chapter 4.2.1 (Revenue hierarchy in Odisha).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ1** (Implementation of DILRMP) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic reforms vs. state capacity).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The problem of rule arises at the friction point between centralised state administrative authority and localized, dispersed practices on the ground.

### 4. The Singularity and Externality of the Prince
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...for Machiavelli, it was alleged, the prince stood in a relation of singularity and externality, and thus of transcendence, to his principality. The prince acquires his principality by inheritance or conquest, but in any case he does not form part of it, he remains external to it."* (Page 89)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.1 (Functions of land records) & Chapter 2.3.2 (Colonial settlements).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ4** (Source of disjuncture) | **Debate 4** (Colonial revenue record $\rightarrow$ postcolonial title).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Sovereign power rests on an external relationship to territory. The British East India Company entered India as a sovereign tax collector, maintaining an external, extractive relation that prioritised revenue collection over local land rights.

### 5. The Fragile Synthetic Sovereign Link
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...the link in any event remains a purely synthetic one and there is no fundamental, essential, natural and juridical connection between the prince and his principality. As a corollary of this, given that this link is external, it will be fragile and continually under threat..."* (Page 89)

*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ3** (Tenancy and history) | **Debate 1** (Presumptive vs. conclusive title).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The external link of sovereignty is fragile, forcing the state to continually defend its claim. In colonial Bengal and Odisha, the state created presumptive land records to artificially stabilize its synthetic revenue claim.

### 6. The Prince's Object of Power
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...the objective of the exercise of power is to reinforce, strengthen and protect the principality, but with this last understood to mean not the objective ensemble of its subjects and the territory, but rather the prince's relation with what he owns, with the territory he has inherited or acquired, and with his subjects."* (Page 90)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.1 (Functions of land records) & Chapter 2.3.2 (Colonial settlements).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ1** (Motivation of land records) | **Debate 4** (Colonial revenue record $\rightarrow$ postcolonial title).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The sovereign's concern is his own relation of possession. Colonial surveys were not executed for public welfare, but to secure the Company's tax extraction rights.

### 7. Immanent and Plural Government
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...practices of government are, on the one hand, multifarious and concern many kinds of people: the head of a family, the superior of a convent, the teacher or tutor... so that there are several forms of government... while, on the other hand, all these other kinds of government are internal to the state or society."* (Page 91)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 1.1 (Analytical framework) & Chapter 5.7.2 (Revenue office interactions).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ2** (How society uses records) | **Debate 7** (NIE efficiency vs. agrarian relations).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Government is internal to society and highly distributed. Land governance is not just the work of the Department of Revenue; it is negotiated in the daily conduct of village-level partition, sharecropping, and inheritance.

### 8. Discontinuity of Sovereignty vs. Continuity of Government
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...whereas the doctrine of the prince and the juridical theory of sovereignty are constantly attempting to draw the line between the power of the prince and any other form of power... in the art of government the task is to establish a continuity, in both an upwards and a downwards direction."* (Page 91)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 1.1 (Analytical framework) & Chapter 4.2.1 (Odisha revenue hierarchy).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ1** (Implementation architecture) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic reforms vs. state capacity).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Sovereignty separates the ruler from the ruled, while governmentality links them. Land records act as the continuity grid, transmitting state rules downward to individual claimants and folding their property claims upward into the state database.

### 9. Upward and Downward Continuity Defined
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"The prince's pedagogical formation ensures the upwards continuity of the forms of government, and police the downwards one."* (Page 92)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.4 (Legal and revenue infrastructure) & Chapter 4.2.1 (Tehsildar-RI-Amin hierarchy).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ5** (Technocratic digitisation vs. ground conflict) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic reforms vs. state capacity).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: "Police" is the downward transmission of order. In Odisha land governance, the Revenue Inspector and Amin are the local agents of this downward police, enforcing the rules of the state database onto the village landscape.

### 10. The Hinge of "Economy"
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"The art of government, as becomes apparent in this literature, is essentially concerned with answering the question of how to introduce economy—that is to say, the correct manner of managing individuals, goods and wealth within the family... into the management of the state."* (Page 92)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 1.2 (Review of literature) & Chapter 3.1.1 (Transition to market reforms).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ1** (Motivation of modernization) | **Debate 7** (NIE efficiency vs. agrarian relations).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Economy moves from household management to state practice. Land record modernisation is the modern mechanism for introducing this economic management into the state's database, tracking family property at a national scale.

## Theme 2: The Government of Things & Relational Power

### 11. The Government of Things Definition
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"Government is the right disposition of things, arranged so as to lead to a convenient end."* (Page 93)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 1.1 (Analytical framework) & Chapter 2.1 (Functions of land records).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ4** (Source of disjuncture) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The fundamental shift: government does not rule territory; it disposes "things." DILRMP’s goal is to dispose land records to secure credit, DBT eligibility, and transaction transparency.

### 12. Sovereignty over Territory vs. Government of Things
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...sovereignty is not exercised on things, but above all on a territory and consequently on the subjects who inhabit it... the territory is the fundamental element both in Machiavellian principality and in juridical sovereignty... On the contrary, in La Perriere's text... the definition of government in no way refers to territory."* (Page 93)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.1 (Functions of land records) & Chapter 2.2.2 (Colonial approaches).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ4** (Source of disjuncture) | **Debate 4** (Colonial revenue record $\rightarrow$ postcolonial title).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Sovereign power maps territory to claim authority. Governmentality focuses on the relations among people and resources. The disjuncture between paper records and physical possession in Odisha villages arises because the state tries to impose a static sovereign-territorial map onto a dynamic set of social relations.

### 13. The Relational Imbrication: Men and Things
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...the things with which in this sense government is to be concerned are in fact men, but men in their relations, their links, their imbrication with those other things which are wealth, resources, means of subsistence, the territory with its specific qualities, climate, irrigation, fertility, etc.; men in their relation to that other kind of things, customs, habits, ways of acting and thinking..."* (Page 93)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.1 (Functions of land records) & Chapter 5.5 (Ownership vs. possession).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ8** (Ownership vs. possession) | **Debate 7** (NIE efficiency vs. agrarian relations).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Land is not just soil. It is a relational web. An RoR must be read as a record of these relations: claimants in relation to irrigation, inheritance customs, and livelihoods. Modern digitisation fails because it strips out these relationships to record a simple ownership name.

### 14. The Ship Metaphor
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"What does it mean to govern a ship? It means clearly to take charge of the sailors, but also of the boat and its cargo; to take care of a ship means also to reckon with winds, rocks and storms; and it consists in that activity of establishing a relation between the sailors... and the ship... and all those eventualities..."* (Page 93)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.1 (Functions of land records) & Chapter 6.2 (Parcel-level findings).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ6** (Parity of alternative methods) | **Debate 5** (Spatial-textual disjuncture).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Governing a ship requires managing relationships and risks. Similarly, land administration cannot just draw a cadastral map; it must reckon with the "winds and storms" of family partitions, crop failures, and bureaucratic gatekeeping.

### 15. Property as a Secondary Variable
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...by comparison, the question of landed property for the family, and the question of the acquisition of sovereignty over a territory for a prince, are only relatively secondary matters. What counts essentially is this complex of men and things; property and territory are merely one of its variables."* (Page 94)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 1.2 (Review of literature) & Chapter 3.2.3 (Conclusive titling challenges).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ2** (Harmony of design and outcomes) | **Debate 7** (NIE efficiency vs. agrarian relations).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: A direct challenge to the de Soto/World Bank titling model. Private property title is not the primary determinant of development; it is merely one variable in a wider social complex of customs, state capacity, and rural relations.

### 16. Territory as Variable: Russia vs. Holland
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"Russia may have the largest territory... but it is mostly made up of swamps... Holland, on the other hand, with its tiny territory... possesses such a population, such wealth, such commercial activity... To govern, then, means to govern things." (Frederick the Great's Anti-Machiavel)* (Page 94)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.2.4 (Contemporary titling approaches) & Chapter 3.1.2 (Role of international institutions).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ1** (Motivation of modernization) | **Debate 9** (World Bank land policies).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Demonstrates that territorial scale is secondary to the management of human activity and wealth. The target of modernization is not merely mapping hectares, but organizing the commercial activities attached to them.

## Theme 3: Economy, Statistics, and Population

### 17. The Circularity of the Common Good
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...the common good refers to a state of affairs where all the subjects without exception obey the laws... the common good means essentially obedience to the law... in every case, what characterizes the end of sovereignty, this common and general good, is in sum nothing other than submission to sovereignty."* (Page 94-95)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.4 (Legal and revenue infrastructure) & Chapter 3.4 (Critical reflections).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ4** (Source of disjuncture) | **Debate 4** (Colonial revenue record $\rightarrow$ postcolonial title).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The end of sovereignty is obedience to the law. Colonial land laws were designed to enforce tax submission. The postcolonial state still operates within this legal loop, demanding obedience to record-correction acts that ignore ground possession.

### 18. The Circularity of Sovereign Power
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...the end of sovereignty is circular: the end of sovereignty is the exercise of sovereignty. The good is obedience to the law, hence the good for sovereignty is that people should obey it... We always come back to this self-referring circularity of sovereignty or principality."* (Page 95)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.4 (Legal and revenue infrastructure) & Chapter 3.4 (Critical reflections).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ5** (Technocratic reforms vs. conflict) | **Debate 1** (Presumptive vs. conclusive title).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Sovereignty's self-referring nature explains why legal title reform remains stuck in litigation and appeals. The legal system prioritises its own internal procedural rules over the reality of possession.

### 19. Disposing Things: Tactics Rather Than Laws
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...with government it is a question not of imposing law on men, but of disposing things: that is to say, of employing tactics rather than laws, and even of using laws themselves as tactics—to arrange things in such a way that, through a certain number of means, such and such ends may be achieved."* (Page 95)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 3.2.1 (Design of DILRMP) & Chapter 4.3.1 (Digitisation of Bhulekh/Bhunaksha).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ6** (Parity of alternative methods) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The postcolonial state bypasses statutory title reform by using database integration and unique IDs (ULPIN) as administrative tactics to manage land relations without changing formal property laws.

### 20. The Multiplicity of Multi-Form Tactics
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...the finality of government resides in the things it manages and in the pursuit of the perfection and intensification of the processes which it directs; and the instruments of government, instead of being laws, now come to be a range of multiform tactics."* (Page 95)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 3.2.1 (Design of DILRMP) & Chapter 3.4 (Critical reflections).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ5** (Technocratic reforms vs. conflict) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Modern state power operates by optimizing administrative processes. Digitisation speed, mutation rates, and database synchronisation are the multiform tactics used to intensify state control over land.

### 21. Wisdom as the Knowledge of Things
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"Wisdom, understood no longer in the traditional sense as knowledge of divine and human laws... but rather as the knowledge of things, of the objectives that can and should be attained, and the disposition of things required to reach them..."* (Page 96)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 3.2.1 (Design of DILRMP) & Chapter 4.3.2 (Implementation gaps in Odisha).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ1** (Motivation/design of DILRMP) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Modern state wisdom is technical and administrative: knowing how to design databases and structure workflows to achieve specific policy objectives, rather than understanding local customary justice.

### 22. Diligence as Service to the Governed
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"As for his diligence, this is the principle that a governor should only govern in such a way that he thinks and acts as though he were in the service of those who are governed."* (Page 96)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 4.3.1 (Digitisation of Bhulekh) & Chapter 5.6.1 (Access to Bhulekh).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ2** (Harmony of design and outcomes) | **Debate 6** (RoR as welfare inclusion vs. exclusion).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The state presents digital land portals (Bhulekh) in the language of "diligence" and public service. However, this online availability often creates new barriers for rural, illiterate landholders.

### 23. Statistics as the Science of the State
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...statistics, meaning the science of the state... mercantilism and the Cameralists' science of police... mercantilism represents a first threshold of rationality in this art of government..."* (Page 96)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.4 (Legal and revenue infrastructure) & Chapter 4.5 (Progress of DILRMP and challenges).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ1** (Implementation progress) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic reforms vs. political economy).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Statistics began as state-science. Modern land records rely on dashboards, mutation numbers, and digitisation percentages to construct a statistical picture of state progress.

### 24. Mercantilism Trapped in Sovereignty
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...mercantilism was blocked and arrested, I believe, precisely by the fact that it took as its essential objective the might of the sovereign... and the instruments mercantilism used were laws, decrees, regulations: that is to say, the traditional weapons of sovereignty."* (Page 97-98)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 3.1.1 (Transition to market reforms) & Chapter 3.2.3 (Conclusive titling challenges).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ5** (Technocratic digitisation vs. ground conflict) | **Debate 4** (Colonial revenue record $\rightarrow$ postcolonial title).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Early modern reforms failed because they tried to fit economic management inside the rigid legal tools of sovereignty. In the same way, DILRMP remains blocked because it attempts to run a digital property market on top of a colonial legal framework of presumptive title.

### 25. The Breakthrough of Contract Theory
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"Contract theory enables the founding contract... to function as a sort of theoretical matrix for deriving the general principles of an art of government... but... it remained at the stage of the formulation of general principles of public law."* (Page 98)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 1.2 (Review of literature) & Chapter 2.4 (Legal and revenue infrastructure).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ4** (Source of disjuncture) | **Debate 1** (Presumptive vs. conclusive title).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Legal contract theory provides abstract principles of rights but fails to address the practical, everyday tactics of land administration and record correction.

### 26. Statistics Reveals Population regularities
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...statistics... now gradually reveals that population has its own regularities, its own rate of deaths and diseases, its cycles of scarcity, etc.; statistics shows also that the domain of population involves a range of intrinsic, aggregate effects, phenomena that are irreducible to those of the family..."* (Page 99)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 3.3 (State welfare and DBT) & Chapter 5.8 (Welfare linkages to land).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ2** (Outcome/misuse of records) | **Debate 6** (RoR as welfare inclusion vs. exclusion).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Statistics constructs the population as an aggregate field of regularities. The state uses land records to count, classify, and sort rural landholders to manage the agricultural economy.

### 27. The Family as Instrument, Not Model
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...prior to the emergence of population, it was impossible to conceive the art of government except on the model of the family... from the moment when... population appears absolutely irreducible to the family, the latter becomes of secondary importance... the family becomes an instrument rather than a model: the privileged instrument for the government of the population and not the chimerical model of good government."* (Page 99)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 3.3 (State welfare & DBT) & Chapter 5.8 (Welfare linkages).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ3** (Tenancy/inheritance history) | **Debate 6** (RoR as welfare inclusion vs. exclusion).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The family shifts from a model of rule to a tool of administration. In DBT systems, the state uses the household unit as a tool to verify claims, targeting welfare to the family through the father's digitised RoR.

### 28. Population as the End of Government
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...government has as its purpose not the act of government itself, but the welfare of the population, the improvement of its condition, the increase of its wealth, longevity, health, etc.; and the means that the government uses to attain these ends are themselves all in some sense immanent to the population..."* (Page 100)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 3.3 (State welfare and DBT) & Chapter 5.8 (Welfare linkages).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ2** (Harmony of design and outcomes) | **Debate 6** (RoR as welfare inclusion vs. exclusion).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Government aims to improve the population's welfare. Cash-transfer schemes like PM-Kisan and KALIA are classic biopolitical tools that use the RoR database to govern rural welfare.

### 29. Population as Subject and Object
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...the population is the subject of needs, of aspirations, but it is also the object in the hands of the government, aware, vis-a-vis the government, of what it wants, but ignorant of what is being done to it. Interest at the level of the consciousness of each individual... this is the new target..."* (Page 100)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 5.6.2 (Experiences of mutation/partition) & Chapter 5.8.2 (Exclusion in cash transfers).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ2** (Uses of RoRs) | **Debate 6** (RoR as welfare inclusion vs. exclusion).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Peasant landholders are active subjects seeking records to prove their claims, but they are also objects sorted by state databases, often unaware of how algorithm filters exclude them.

### 30. The Birth of Political Economy
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"The new science called political economy arises out of the perception of new networks of continuous and multiple relations between population, territory and wealth; and this is accompanied by the formation of a type of intervention characteristic of government, namely intervention in the field of economy and population."* (Page 100)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 1.2 (Review of literature) & Chapter 3.1.2 (Role of international institutions).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ1** (Motivation of modernization) | **Debate 7** (NIE efficiency vs. agrarian relations).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Political economy connects population, territory, and wealth. Land records are the core documents that link the physical territory to population livelihood and market wealth.

## Theme 4: The Triangle of Power & The Governmentalized State

### 31. The Triangle of Sovereignty, Discipline, and Government
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...we have a triangle: sovereignty-discipline-government, which has as its primary target the population and as its essential mechanism the apparatuses of security."* (Page 102)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 1.1 (Analytical framework) & Chapter 3.4 (Critical reflections).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ5** (Technocratic digitisation vs. ground conflict) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Modern land governance is a hybrid triangle: sovereign legal acts (presumptive title), disciplinary land survey rules (Gunter's chain, RI verification), and digital-governmental systems (Bhulekh database, e-mutation).

### 32. The State as Composite Reality
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"The state is a composite reality and a mythicized abstraction, whose importance is a lot more limited than many of us think. Maybe what is really important for our modernity... is not so much the state-isation of society, as 'governmentalization' of the state."* (Page 103)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 1.1 (Analytical framework) & Chapter 3.4 (Critical reflections).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ5** (Technocratic digitisation vs. ground conflict) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The state is not a monolithic monster. The thesis should analyze DILRMP as a distributed network of local record offices, web portals, data standards, and local brokers, rather than a single state actor.

### 33. The Redefinition of State Competence
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...it is the tactics of government which make possible the continual definition and redefinition of what is within the competence of the state and what is not, the public versus the private, and so on; thus the state can only be understood in its survival and its limits on the basis of the general tactics of governmentality."* (Page 103)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 2.4 (Legal and revenue infrastructure) & Chapter 4.3.2 (Implementation gaps in Odisha).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ1** (Implementation architecture) | **Debate 3** (Technocratic reforms vs. state capacity).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: Governmentality redefines the boundary between the public and private. Under DILRMP, the state shifts the burden of record correction onto the citizen (via online self-service) while outsourcing database management to private vendors.

### 34. The Governmental State and Security
*   **Quote**: 
    > *"...finally a governmental state, essentially defined no longer in terms of its territoriality, of its surface area, but in terms of the mass of its population with its volume and density... This state of government... could be seen as corresponding to a type of society controlled by apparatuses of security."* (Page 104)
*   **Thesis Placement**: Chapter 3.2.1 (Design of DILRMP) & Chapter 6.2 (Parcel-level findings).
*   **RQ & Debate**: **RQ6** (Parity of alternative methods) | **Debate 5** (Spatial-textual disjuncture).
*   **Analytical Gloss**: The governmental state focuses on population rather than mere territory. In land governance, the state prioritises database links (linking land records to Aadhaar and bank accounts) to secure market transactions and welfare flows, even as the physical survey remains incomplete.
