Before I develop an analytical approach for engaging social dynamics of the production of authority through property and citizenship, it is necessary to specify more precisely what is meant by these concepts, and by their shared characteristic: recognition.


Byproperty we have to understand more than ‘private property’. Property is often —quite perfunctorily — equated with absolute, unfettered ownership. However, the idea of ownership as ‘total exclusion of the right of any other individual in the Universe’ is indeed an idea (Rose, 1998: 601). Ownership is always circumscribed by others’ rights, which limit the exercise of an abstract total right to property. I therefore understand property as a legitimized claim to something of value sanctioned by some form of political authority (Godelier, 1986; Le Roy et al., 1996; MacPherson, 1978; Rose, 1994; Sikor and Lund, 2009). Struggles over property — very often in the form of land — can therefore be seen as struggles for the recognition of a wide variety of rights to access resources in various ways. These range from rights to reside and settle, through rights to use and extract, and extend to the right to transact those rights. Land conflicts are sometimes about more than property, however.

Territorialization and the spatial ordering of people combine different political and legal techniques of classification, registration and mapping. The processes do not merely structure physical space; they also organize the social and political perception of it (Rasmussen and Lund, forthcoming; Vandergeest and Peluso, 1995). Territorializing strategies allow and disallow certain forms of land use and access; they regulate certain forms of mobility and transactions; and, by differentiating rights to natural resources, territorialization contributes to the structuring of citizenship.

Citizenship can be defined as meaningful membership of an organized political body. Struggles for citizenship are, generally, struggles for the recognition of the very right to have rights in a body politic (Arendt, 1948/1979; Lund, 2011; Somers, 2008). Formal national citizenship is just one of several configurations of socially constructed collective subjectivities.

Projects of rule, taxation, conscription, confiscation, eviction and so on have made people very suspicious of public authority, with good reason (Scott, 2009). However, for many people, the grim reality is —to paraphrase OscarWilde—that there is only one thing worse than being seen by political authority, and that is not being seen. (My example: how people line up to make Aadhaar).

## Christian Lund, "Rule and Rupture: State Formation through the Production of Property and Citizenship" (2016) — Comprehensive Scholarly Review

### I. Framing and Theoretical Ambition

Lund opens with a provocation that sets the register for everything that follows: =="Treating the 'state' as a finished product gets in the way of understanding it. The state is always in the making."== This is not merely a rhetorical opening. It is a methodological commitment that places the essay in a lineage running from Weber through Tilly and Bourdieu to the post-structural critiques of Mitchell and Das — all of whom, in different ways, insisted on treating state authority as an effect of practice rather than a pre-given institutional fact. What Lund adds to this tradition is a specific analytical architecture centred on two concepts — property and citizenship — and the dynamic through which struggles over both simultaneously produce and contest political authority.

The essay's ambition is stated cleanly: "investigating the social production of property and citizenship enables concrete understanding of the dynamics of authority or state formation." This is not a claim that property and citizenship are the only axes of state formation. Lund acknowledges that "not all questions of state formation can be reduced to property and citizenship" and that "government forms around the control over central resources, and in some historical periods, and in some places, key resources may be trading points and routes; they may be 'knowledge' or 'security.'" But he argues that property — especially in land — and citizenship are "increasingly such central resources in most societies," and that engaging with their production gives the analyst access to a remarkably wide array of state-formation dynamics: "how notions of ownership are conceptualized, how political identities are constructed, how taxes are recovered, how conflicts are adjudicated, how violence and other sanctions are exercised and legitimated."

This is an important framing move for anyone working on land governance. It means that a programme like DILRMP is not merely an administrative or technical initiative — it is, on Lund's terms, an attempt to remake the contracts through which the Indian state recognises land rights and is in turn recognised as the authoritative adjudicator of those rights.

---

### II. The Mutual Constitution of Rights and Authority

The theoretical heart of the essay is the claim that rights and political authority are not sequentially related — authority producing rights or rights preceding authority — but mutually constitutive. "Rights and political authority are contemporaneous, and the control exercised by institutions over resources and political subjectivities does not represent a pre-existing authority. It produces authority. Conversely, effective rights do not represent pre-existing natural rights. They are political constructions and achievements."

The concept Lund uses to hold this mutual constitution together is _recognition_. Drawing loosely on a Hegelian tradition — and specifically on Honneth's "simple legal recognition" — he defines it as "effective recognition of rights by an institution and a reciprocal recognition of its authority by their subjects." The key word is _reciprocal_. When a revenue official certifies a landholding, she is not merely recording an existing fact. She is performing an act of authorisation that simultaneously invests the revenue department with the authority to perform such acts. "The act of authorizing recursively authorizes the authorizer." This recursive loop is the engine of state formation.

The concept of a "contract of recognition" follows from this: "In exchange for recognized valuable property rights in land and other entitlements, people recognize the political power of the institution by payment of tax in the form of money, tribute, labour, allegiance, or other resources." Lund is careful to flag the limits of the contract metaphor — these arrangements are "not exactly voluntary, not always consensual, often contentious, and always under renegotiation." But the vocabulary of contract is useful precisely because it captures the bilateral structure of recognition: institutions need subjects who will acknowledge their authority just as much as subjects need institutions that will recognise their claims. "The ability to entitle and disenfranchise people with regard to property, to establish the conditions under which they hold that property — together with the ability to define who belongs and who does not, and to establish and uphold rank, privilege and social servitude in its many forms — is constitutive of state power."

A crucial implication follows: "as rights and public authority are co-produced, the erosion of one also means the dissipation of the other." This is analytically powerful. An authority that fails to deliver effective recognition of rights loses its claim to state quality. Equally, rights that are not institutionally sustained — because the supporting authority is weakened or displaced — evaporate. "Rights held under one regime may evaporate under the next." For land rights analysis in transitional or post-reform contexts, this is a fundamental insight.

---

### III. Reconceptualising Property and Citizenship

Before developing the analytical framework, Lund undertakes careful conceptual work on both property and citizenship — work that is worth sitting with because both concepts are routinely flattened in land governance debates.

On property: "the idea of ownership as 'total exclusion of the right of any other individual in the Universe' is indeed an idea." Lund refuses the liberal conflation of property with absolute, private, individual ownership. Instead he defines property as "a legitimized claim to something of value sanctioned by some form of political authority." This relational, politically contingent definition — drawing on Rose, Godelier, and his own earlier collaboration with Sikor — has radical implications. It means that property is always a bundle of claims of different strengths, recognised to different degrees by different institutions. "Struggles over property — very often in the form of land — can therefore be seen as struggles for the recognition of a wide variety of rights to access resources in various ways. These range from rights to reside and settle, through rights to use and extract, and extend to the right to transact those rights."

The discussion of territory is particularly important: "Territorialization and the spatial ordering of people combine different political and legal techniques of classification, registration and mapping. The processes do not merely structure physical space; they also organize the social and political perception of it." This resonates directly with Scott on cadastral maps and with Vandergeest and Peluso's work on internal territorialisation — the argument that mapping is a political act that creates, rather than simply describes, jurisdictional authority.

On citizenship: Lund offers a deliberately expansive definition — "meaningful membership of an organized political body" — that encompasses much more than formal nationality. "Formal national citizenship is just one of several configurations of socially constructed collective subjectivities." This is important for contexts of legal and institutional pluralism where local membership, community belonging, and customary inclusion may be more immediately operative than national citizen status. "In many places, land is a resource to which access is ensured not merely through the market or by membership of a national community: local citizenship and status are often just as important."

The Oscar Wilde paraphrase — "there is only one thing worse than being seen by political authority, and that is not being seen" — captures something that development and rights discourse often misses: that visibility to state institutions is simultaneously a resource and a risk, and that the calculus of visibility is a central strategic problem for marginalised populations. "To be recognized — and reckoned with — by society and its institutions of authority renders citizenship, at its most basic, a mechanism for inclusion and exclusion, and signifies the right to have rights."

---

### IV. Rupture as Epistemological Strategy

One of the essay's most distinctive moves is its treatment of _rupture_ — not as a sociologically unusual condition but as a methodological vantage point. "Both forms of change can give rise to profound reconfigurations of polities, institutions, norms and the prevailing social contract. Above all, the choice of rupture is epistemological."

Why rupture? Because it renders visible what in conditions of relative stability appears natural and given. Ruptures are "open moments when opportunities and risks multiply, when the scope of outcomes widens, and when new structural scaffolding is erected." In these moments, "it becomes more readily visible to whom people are beholden for their rights and, by implication, what institutions validate or ignore the plight of the disadvantaged." Lund draws here on the Manchester School's "trouble cases" and "situational analyses," on Sahlins's "revelatory crisis," and on what he had earlier called "diagnostic events" — a tradition of sociological inference that treats the breakdown of normality as the most productive site of structural analysis.

The discussion of colonialism as rupture is the centrepiece here, and it repays close reading. "Colonial agents — governments and private companies in various combinations — dispossessed colonial subjects and established new property regimes. By the same token, they established new subject categories and various degrees of (dis-)enfranchisement of people in their new dominions." The Domesday Book example is analytically elegant: "recording identity and property did more than that; it also established, with bureaucratic and regal ceremony, that the propertied classes were beholden to the new king, William the Conqueror, for their rights. The Domesday Book established the sovereign as much as it established rights." The cadastre, here in its earliest English form, is not a neutral record but a constitutive political act.

Lund is also careful about the limits of rupture as a category. Post-colonial, post-liberal, post-socialist, or post-authoritarian conditions rarely involve clean breaks: "most new orders combine with the institutional debris of the past." "Post is not necessarily past." This is a guard against teleological readings of independence, democratisation, or digital reform as transformative break — a caution that matters greatly for any analysis of DILRMP.

---

### V. Dynamics of Categorisation and Jurisdictional Competition

The essay's second major analytical section moves from the bilateral logic of recognition to the more complex dynamics of categorisation and jurisdictional competition. These two dynamics are related: institutions compete to control categorical distinctions because controlling categories means controlling who can make recognisable claims, and therefore controlling who validates the institution's authority.

On categorisation: "Who can acquire, hold and transact property? This involves questions of identity, since engagement with institutions is very often differentiated along ethnic, gender, occupational and age lines, as well as by class and wealth." The political production of categories — autochthone/stranger, citizen/non-citizen, tribal/non-tribal, encroacher/landowner — is not a descriptive but a constitutive exercise. Quoting Tilly: "'Durable inequality among categories arises because people who control access to value-producing resources solve pressing organizational problems by means of categorical distinctions. Inadvertently, or otherwise, those people set up systems of social closure, exclusion, and control.'" Categories, once institutionally embedded, can harden into habitual necessity for all involved — including those they disadvantage.

On jurisdiction: Lund proposes thinking of jurisdictions as "overlapping and interfering fields of authority in the making, rather than discrete realms of defined legal scope" — territorial, functional, and personal jurisdiction all potentially operating simultaneously and in competition. This produces the condition he calls "forum shopping," in which claimants can strategically invoke different institutional authorities depending on which is most likely to recognise their claim. "Neighbourhood associations, religious institutions, political parties, local strongmen, vigilantes and militias all compete to be able to define and enforce identity and property claims as rights."

The discussion of visibility strategies is one of the richest sections: "Consolidating rights is hard work. Strategies of visibility and obscurity depend on the context, on the authorities' ambitions and resources, and on people's available options." The examples Lund marshals — communities organising as CPAs before formal registration, residents structuring informal settlements to conform to municipal technical norms, water users facilitating the practical work of government agents — all illustrate a single point: that ordinary people are not simply subject to recognition or its denial, but actively labour to become recognisable on terms that the relevant institution can process. "By the same token, people breathe life into the institutions' claims to authority."

This insight — that the governed actively constitute the governing — complicates any simple top-down picture of state formation and is essential for understanding how digitisation programmes like DILRMP interact with the existing landscape of claims.

---

### VI. The Two Cases

The Ghana chieftaincy case demonstrates how competition over jurisdiction produces not just confrontation but collusion. The colonial rupture created chiefs who could simultaneously claim traditional legitimacy and statutory recognition — able to "edit and create customs and justify convenient practice as 'tradition' by the mere status of chieftaincy." The subsequent accommodation between state and chiefly authority is described bluntly: "The state recognized the rights of the chiefs to control land and revenue, and the chiefs consented to the state gaining a share of these revenues and actively participating in the management of stool revenues... This arrangement has served to undermine the rights in land of farmers and other land users." The contemporary layer — where scarcity drives retroactive weakening of "strangers'" land rights — illustrates that categorical disadvantage can intensify precisely as formal rights frameworks strengthen. "Their capacity to define citizenship and its ensuing rights was strengthened as it combined with increasing scarcity of land."

The Indonesia case is more complex. Violent entrepreneurs — _ormas_ — are shown to acquire state quality not through formal authorisation but through the functional delivery of protection and rights recognition. After 1998, "rather than being the regime's sub-contracted muscle, some of the ormas of the violent entrepreneur type began to define and protect rights to land and livelihood in poor urban areas, thus emerging as authorities able to negotiate concessions, privilege and impunity with institutional actors that needed their support." This demonstrates that the mutual constitution of rights and authority operates even — perhaps especially — at the institutional margins, through actors whose legitimacy is formally contested. State quality is not a monopoly of statutory institutions.

### VII. Critical Assessment: Strengths and Silences

The essay's greatest strength is its theoretical clarity and its refusal of both structural determinism and ethnographic particularism. The mutual constitution thesis is stated with precision, operationalised through tractable analytical concepts, and illustrated with cases that are historically specific enough to be instructive without collapsing into mere description.

The insistence on institutional pluralism as the _normal_ condition rather than a deviation from a Weberian ideal is methodologically liberating. It means that the analysis of land governance does not have to begin by lamenting the incompleteness of the state apparatus — it can simply ask which institutions are, in practice, performing recognitional authority and with what effects.

The treatment of categorisation as a constitutive political process — not merely a descriptive or administrative act — is particularly valuable for land governance analysis. And the concept of practical norms that develop when "official norms, rules and laws exist for bureaucrats and citizens as socio-legal markers" but "actual conditions often prohibit the observance of official norms" is a precise description of how formal property registration regimes actually function in most of rural India.

The silences, however, matter. _Gender_ appears repeatedly in lists of categorical distinctions but receives no sustained theoretical treatment. The gendered production of land rights — who can hold, inherit, mortgage, and transact — is among the most robust findings in agrarian political economy globally and across India specifically. Its relegation to a listing in Lund's framework is a gap.

_Class_, similarly, is gestured at but not theorised. Lund's framework accounts well for how categorical distinctions are produced and institutionalised, but it does not adequately engage the agrarian political economy tradition's insistence that class relations — the surplus-extraction nexus, the labour-land relation, the structure of indebtedness — are constitutive of rather than merely incidental to property regimes. For a critical Development Studies analysis, this is a significant absence.

_Resistance_ is thin. The framework models claims and counter-claims within institutional frameworks but does not have a robust vocabulary for sustained collective opposition that refuses the terms of recognition altogether — that refuses to make itself legible on the state's terms. This is where the _metis_ tradition from Scott, or the subaltern studies tradition from Guha, would need to supplement Lund.

Finally, while Lund draws on a global literature, the essay's empirical anchors are Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia. The South Asian agrarian context — with its specific colonial cadastral traditions, its complex jajmani and tenure relations, its dense legislative history of land reform — does not appear. Readers must do this translation work themselves.

## 1. Executive Summary & Core Thesis

Lund’s paper challenges the orthodox, institutionalist view of "the state" as a static, pre-existing apparatus that unilaterally enforces laws and distributes resources. Instead, he conceptualizes the state as an **ongoing, relational process that is constantly under construction**.

The central thesis is that **political authority (state quality) and substantive rights are contemporaneous and mutually constitutive**. This dynamic is driven by two fundamental axes of social struggle:

1. **Property:** The institutionalization and legitimization of claims over valuable resources (particularly land).
    
2. **Citizenship:** The categorization, inclusion, or disenfranchisement of political subjects (membership).
    

Rather than analyzing these dynamics during periods of relative socio-political equilibrium—where the illusion of an omnipotent, pre-existing state obscures the machinery of power—Lund argues that researchers must look to **moments of "rupture"** (regime shifts, colonial encounters, crises) to observe the raw manufacturing of authority in real-time.

---

## 2. Theoretical Scaffolding & Key Concepts

Lund constructs a dense, interdisciplinary framework bridging political anthropology, legal sociology, and agrarian studies. The architecture relies on three primary conceptual loops:

### The Dialectic of Recognition

==Lund dispenses with the linear, "chicken-and-egg" debate regarding whether authority or rights come first. He posits that they are born at the exact same moment through **contracts of recognition**:==

- A claim to a right (e.g., a land dispute or a demand for inclusion) forces an institution to exercise authority (to validate, deny, or punish).
    
- When an institution successfully arbitrates that claim and the subjects accept the ruling, the institution's legitimacy is cemented.
    
- Thus, the act of claiming rights _invokes_ public authority, and the act of enforcing rights _manufactures_ state quality.
    

### Property Beyond "Absolute Ownership"

Drawing on legal anthropology (such as MacPherson and Carol Rose), ==Lund deconstructs the myth of property as absolute, unfettered ownership. He defines property as a **legitimized claim to something of value sanctioned by a political authority**==.

- Property is a "bundle of rights" (to reside, extract, or transact) that is always circumscribed by the rights of others.
    
- Consequently, conflicts over land are never mere private real estate disputes; they are struggles over _which_ authority has the right to sanction the claim.
    

### Spatial Control: Property vs. Territory

Lund highlights a productive tension in how space is managed:

- **Space as Property (Ownership):** Economic or legal rights to specific, localized assets.
    
- **Space as Territory (Rule):** Political jurisdiction over a defined geographic boundary.
    
- Through **territorialization** (mapping, classification, and registration), institutions do not just organize dirt; they structure the social and political perception of space, deciding who is an "insider" or a "trespasser," thereby shaping citizenship.
    

---

## 3. Methodological Intervention: The Value of "Rupture"

A significant contribution of the paper is its methodological justification for focusing on historical and political breakdowns. Lund terms these **"open moments."**

```
[Normal Stability] ---> (Illusion of static, top-down state power)
       │
[Moment of Rupture] ---> (Mask is ripped away; structural scaffolding exposed)
       │
[Open Negotiation] ---> (Contemporaneous creation of new rights & new authority)
```

### Intellectual Lineage

Lund transparently grounds his methodology in classic anthropological field techniques:

- **Marshall Sahlins & Jacqueline Solway ("Revelatory Crisis"):** Crises act as a societal X-ray, activating hidden power structures, mobilizing conflicting interests, and rendering core systemic contradictions visible.
    
- **The Manchester School ("Trouble Cases" & "Diagnostic Events"):** Utilizing localized, highly specific conflicts (such as an acute agrarian land battle) to diagnose and extrapolate the hidden, macro-structural features of the wider society.
    

### The Analytical Advantage

Lund explicitly notes that sudden ruptures are not inherently more important than slow, incremental, day-to-day social transformations. However, they provide an unmatched **analytical window**. When the state apparatus fractures or shifts, the "glue" is exposed before it dries, allowing researchers to inspect the active construction of new contracts of recognition.

---

## 4. Empirical Application: The Fabrication of the Sovereign

To demonstrate the historical validity of this model, Lund bridges modern colonial dispossession with classic medieval European state formation, demonstrating that the underlying mechanics of power remain identical.

### The Colonial Enterprise

Colonialism is categorized as the most dramatic and violent reordering of property and political subjectivity in human history.

- Colonial agents did not just seize land; they established new **property regimes**.
    
- Concurrently, they invented new **subject categories** to determine who was entitled to resources and who was subjected to social servitude.
    
- The everyday bureaucratic power to categorize, regulate, and exclude was the primary mechanism used to manufacture colonial state authority.
    

### The Norman Conquest & The Domesday Book (1086)

Lund utilizes William the Conqueror's compilation of the _Domesday Book_ to elegantly summarize his thesis:

- **The Rupture:** The 1066 Norman invasion completely shattered the Anglo-Saxon legal and social order.
    
- **The Scaffold:** The _Domesday Book_ was a massive bureaucratic audit of England's land and property classes.
    
- **The Production of Authority:** The book did not merely record pre-existing assets. By recording property with immense regal ceremony, it forced the new Norman barons to acknowledge that their land rights existed _only_ because the King recognized them.
    

> **"The Domesday Book established the sovereign as much as it established rights."**

---

## 5. Critical Interventions & Conceptual Shifts

The paper successfully shifts the paradigm of state-formation studies in several ways:

|**Orthodox / Institutionalist View**|**Lund’s Relational Framework ("Rule and Rupture")**|
|---|---|
|**The State** is a fixed, physical set of statutory institutions (legislative, executive, judiciary) that holds a monopoly on power.|**"State Quality"** is an achieved status. Non-statutory institutions (local chiefs, rebel groups, committees) can acquire state quality by effectively governing.|
|**Rights** flow downward as a gift from a pre-established, consolidated government.|**Rights and Authority** are contemporaneous; demanding and granting rights is what _constructs_ authority.|
|**Land Disputes** are economic struggles over resource scarcity and private ownership.|**Land Disputes** are deeply political struggles over jurisdiction, citizenship, and a "claim to state."|

---

## 6. Limitations & Areas for Further Exploration

While theoretically brilliant, a holistic review must note the boundaries of Lund's intervention:

- **The Scope of "Resource":** As Lund himself prudently qualifies, not all state effects can be reduced to land and citizenship. In certain contexts, states form around abstract wealth, trade routes, technological data, or the monopoly of "security" and "knowledge" (referencing Rose & Miller's problematics of government).
    
- **Institutional Durability:** The framework is highly effective at explaining how authority is _born_ during a rupture, but it leaves room for deeper exploration into how these negotiated "contracts of recognition" solidify into rigid, durable, and path-dependent institutional bureaucracies that survive long after the rupture has faded.
    

---

## 7. Conclusion

Christian Lund’s _"Rule and Rupture"_ provides a profound, dynamic, and ethnographically sensitive toolkit for analyzing political power. By shifting the focus to the social production of **property** and **citizenship**, and utilizing **ruptures** as an analytical lens, he strips away the mystique of the "monolithic state." The paper remains a cornerstone text for researchers seeking to understand how authority is built, challenged, and legitimized from the ground up in volatile or transforming societies.

Lund argues that political authority is not prior to property and citizenship; authority is produced through the practical recognition of claims to resources and membership. ### 2. Conceptual Architecture

**State as process, not object**  
Lund treats the state as an ongoing effect of institutional practice. The point is not to ask whether the state is weak or strong in the abstract, but to ask which institutions successfully recognise and enforce claims. This concept functions as the frame for reading DILRMP as state formation through digital record infrastructure.

**Claims, rights, and authority**  
The article's most important mechanism is that "Claims to rights prompt the exercise of authority" (p. 1201). Rights are not simply distributed by a completed authority; they trigger, test, and constitute authority. This is directly portable to land-record disputes: a mutation application, RoR correction petition, or welfare claim forces the revenue state to recognise or deny a claimant.

**Contracts of recognition**  
Recognition is reciprocal. When a person or group seeks recognition of a land claim, they also recognise the institution as capable of validating it. When the institution successfully validates and enforces the claim, it acquires state quality. In the thesis, this explains why villagers line up before the RI, Amin, Tehsildar, Sub-Registrar, or court: they are not just seeking paperwork; they are seeking state recognition.

**Property as recognised claim**  
Lund defines property as a legitimised claim to something valuable sanctioned by authority. This shifts property away from the narrow idea of absolute ownership. It helps explain why RoR, possession, registered deed, inheritance, tenancy, and social recognition may all represent different layers of property rather than one settled legal truth.

**Citizenship as recognised membership**  
Citizenship is meaningful membership in a political body. In land governance, membership is not only formal national citizenship; it may be local belonging, caste/community status, settlement history, or recognition as a landholding farmer. For Odisha, this is central to PM-Kisan, CM-Kisan, KALIA, and other land-record-mediated benefits.

**Property-citizenship nexus**  
The paper links land rights and political membership. Who can hold property and who counts as a rights-bearing subject are not separate questions. This gives the thesis a language for RoR as both property record and welfare/citizenship gate.

**Rupture**  
Rupture is an open moment when old institutional arrangements are unsettled and the construction of authority becomes visible. Colonialism is Lund's key example, but the concept can also be used carefully for post-independence land reform and digital modernisation. DILRMP is not a violent rupture like colonial settlement, but it is a soft administrative rupture because it changes the infrastructure of recognition.

**Territorialization**  
Lund connects legal control over property with political control over territory. Classification, registration, and mapping do not merely describe space; they organise how space can be governed. This is directly relevant to Bhunaksha, cadastral maps, ULPIN/Bhu-Aadhaar, and the spatial-textual disjuncture in Odisha.

**Jurisdictional competition**  
Authority over land is often split among multiple institutions. Lund's account supports the thesis' focus on the fragmentation of revenue, registration, settlement, and judicial institutions. DILRMP can be read as an attempt to integrate competing jurisdictions, but also as a risk: it may harden one institutional version of reality without resolving plural claims.

This is a theoretical introductory article to a special issue, not a primary empirical study. It uses comparative examples to demonstrate the rights-authority mechanism.

**Colonialism and Domesday Book**  
Lund uses colonialism as the clearest historical rupture in property and subject formation. **Indonesia**

The later article summaries are less important for the thesis. They show the range of cases where property and citizenship form authority, but they should not be mined deeply unless a later chapter needs comparative language on rupture, post-conflict state formation, or territorialization.

# Review 4

## 1. Core Argument

In *Rule and Rupture*, Christian Lund argues that the state is not a static, pre-existing administrative container that distributes rights; rather, it is a relational process that is constantly "in the making." Political authority and rights (conceptualized through property and citizenship) are contemporaneous and mutually constitutive. When an institution successfully defines and enforces property rights or community membership (citizenship), it recursively establishes and consolidates its own authority, thereby acquiring "state quality." Moments of "rupture" (colonialism, regime shifts, conflict, or democratization) serve as "open moments" or revelatory crises that expose the raw construction of these contracts of recognition, showing that rights do not simply flow from authority but actively constitute it.

> "The core element of both rights is recognition. The processes of recognition of claims to land and other resources as property, and of political identity as citizenship with entitlements, simultaneously invest the institution that provides such recognition with recognition of its authority to do so. That is to say, the act of authorizing recursively authorizes the authorizer." (p. 1204)

---

## 2. Conceptual Architecture

Lund builds his framework on five load-bearing concepts, defined with verbatim precision:

* **The State-in-the-Making**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Treating the 'state' as a finished product gets in the way of understanding it. The state is always in the making. Political authority is (re-)produced through its successful exercise over an important issue in relation to the social actors concerned." (p. 1200)
  * *Gloss*: Statehood is not defined by static Weberian compliance but by the active, daily reproduction of authority through the regulation of vital resources.
* **Property**:
  * *Verbatim*: "I therefore understand property as a legitimized claim to something of value sanctioned by some form of political authority... Struggles over property... can therefore be seen as struggles for the recognition of a wide variety of rights to access resources..." (p. 1203)
  * *Gloss*: Property is not a thing or an absolute legal status, but a socially and politically sanctioned relation that requires a validating "authorizer."
* **Citizenship**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Citizenship can be defined as meaningful membership of an organized political body. Struggles for citizenship are, generally, struggles for the recognition of the very right to have rights in a body politic..." (p. 1203)
  * *Gloss*: Citizenship represents local or national political "visibility" and agency, acting as the mechanism that determines who can legitimately claim property.
* **Reciprocal Recognition & State Quality**:
  * *Verbatim*: "I am looking for an effective recognition of rights by an institution and a reciprocal recognition of its authority by their subjects... The effective recognition of claims as rights produces state quality in recognizing institutions." (p. 1204)
  * *Gloss*: A dual-directional loop where individuals accept an institution's authority (paying taxes, showing deference) in exchange for the enforcement of their claims as rights.
* **Rupture**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Ruptures are 'open moments' when opportunities and risks multiply, when the scope of outcomes widens, and when new structural scaffolding is erected." (p. 1201)
  * *Gloss*: Significant historical crises that shatter established social contracts, making the underlying machinery of state-making and rights-enforcement visible to observers.

## 3. Empirical and Evidence Terrain

Lund substantiates his processual framework through historical examples and two main post-rupture case studies:

* **Historical Case: The Domesday Book (1086)**:
  * ==Following the Norman Conquest of England in 1066 (a massive political rupture), William the Conqueror compiled the Domesday Book in 1086. It recorded landholdings and identities, establishing that the propertied classes owed their rights entirely to the new sovereign. Thus, "the Domesday Book established the sovereign as much as it established rights" (p. 1202).==
* **Case 1: Chieftaincy in Southern Ghana**:
  * *The Rupture*: Colonial indirect rule.
  * *Mechanism*: Colonial agents declared land as part of the "traditional" realm to keep it non-commoditized and cheap. They consolidated chiefs' power to allocate land to northern migrant cocoa farmers. Postcolonial state reforms (1979–1992) further entrenched chiefs by placing Customary Land Secretariats under their control.
  * *Citizenship Nexus*: In the 1990s, land scarcity prompted local youth to pressure chiefs to retroactively weaken the property rights of "strangers" (migrants) to favor "autochthons," showing how local citizenship categories dictate property security.
* **Case 2: Paramilitaries and Ormas in Indonesia**:
  * *The Rupture*: The fall of Suharto's New Order in 1998, democratization, and decentralization.
  * *Mechanism*: During the New Order, paramilitaries (*ormas*) operated as state-contracted muscle. The post-1998 democratic rupture fragmented central military control.
  * *State Quality*: In cities like Medan, *ormas* began controlling informal settlements and acquiring land. To mobilize voters for local politicians, they delivered protection (housing, services, dispute resolution) to poor squatters. By enforcing and protecting these informal rights, the *ormas* established themselves as local authorities, acquiring "state quality" from below.

### Theme B: The Dialectic of Rights and Authority
7. **"To grasp the dialectics of rights and authority, we need to dispense with simple assumptions that political authority exists prior to rights of property and citizenship. Rights and political authority are contemporaneous..."** (p. 1201)
   * *Gloss*: Rejects both the Hobbesian "state first" and lockean "natural rights first" models, proposing instead that rights and rulers are born together.
8. **"Conversely, effective rights do not represent pre-existing natural rights. They are political constructions and achievements."** (p. 1201)
   * *Gloss*: Rights do not exist in the abstract; they are only real when backed by an authority capable of enforcing them against challengers.
9. **"Claims to rights prompt the exercise of authority."** (p. 1200)
   * *Gloss*: The state does not act in a vacuum. It is the demands and petitions of citizens that force the administrative machinery of the state to mobilize.
10. **"Rights are not simply there, bestowed on people by a benevolent higher body; they are wrested from power."** (p. 1206)
    * *Gloss*: Focuses on agency and political economy; rights are the product of historic struggles and political negotiations.
11. **"Political subjectivity does not only concern relations to formal governments or singular institutions. It concerns relations to any institutional actor that recognizes claims..."** (p. 1207)
    * *Gloss*: If a local gang or customary chief secures your home when the police will not, your political subjectivity is bound to that gang or chief.
12. **"As rights and public authority are co-produced, the erosion of one also means the dissipation of the other."** (p. 1205)
    * *Gloss*: If the state loses the capacity to defend land titles, its own legitimacy and authority in the eyes of the landholders dissolve.

### Theme C: Ruptures as Epistemological Windows
13. **"Ruptures are ‘open moments’ when opportunities and risks multiply, when the scope of outcomes widens, and when new structural scaffolding is erected."** (p. 1201)
    * *Gloss*: Ruptures are periods of structural instability where the rules of resource distribution and political belonging are rewritten.
14. **"By looking at ruptures, however... we can inspect the very construction of new contracts of recognition."** (p. 1201)
    * *Gloss*: Explains the methodological choice of studying crises; it allows the researcher to see the state-making process in slow motion.
15. **"The Domesday Book established the sovereign as much as it established rights."** (p. 1202)
    * *Gloss*: A powerful historical example showing that state registration of property is primarily a tool to bind property owners to the sovereign.
16. **"Hence, when we use words like post-colonial, post-liberal, post-socialist, or post-authoritarian, it is to suggest a dramatic break... without implying that all colonial features have disappeared..."** (p. 1202-1203)
    * *Gloss*: Highlights institutional path dependency. New regimes are always built on the "institutional debris" and administrative categories of the past.
17. **"Indeed, post-colonial governments have in many instances preserved — sometimes even furthered — colonial patterns of exclusionary property and subject formation."** (p. 1203)
    * *Gloss*: Postcolonial continuity. The post-independence state frequently adopts the same divide-and-rule classification tactics as its colonial predecessor.

### Theme D: The Nexus of Property and Citizenship
18. **"I therefore understand property as a legitimized claim to something of value sanctioned by some form of political authority."** (p. 1203)
    * *Gloss*: Property is not an object but a social relation of exclusion and inclusion that requires institutional backing to exist.
19. **"Struggles over property — very often in the form of land — can therefore be seen as struggles for the recognition of a wide variety of rights to access resources..."** (p. 1203)
    * *Gloss*: Land conflicts are fundamentally legal-political conflicts over the recognition of claims.
20. **"Territorialization and the spatial ordering of people combine different political and legal techniques of classification, registration and mapping."** (p. 1203-1204)
    * *Gloss*: Mapping and registration are political strategies of territorialization used to control both physical space and social populations.
21. **"Citizenship can be defined as meaningful membership of an organized political body."** (p. 1203)
    * *Gloss*: Broadens citizenship beyond national passports, defining it as the recognized "right to have rights" within any functional polity.
22. **"There is only one thing worse than being seen by political authority, and that is not being seen."** (p. 1204)
    * *Gloss*: Highlights the desperation of the marginalized. Being mapped, registered, or taxed by the state is often preferred over total exclusion and invisibility.

### Theme E: Reciprocal Recognition and State Quality
23. **"The processes of recognition of claims... simultaneously invest the institution that provides such recognition with recognition of its authority to do so."** (p. 1204)
    * *Gloss*: The recursive loop: when you ask the state to register your land, you are validating the state's right to govern you.
    * *Citation*: Lund (2016: 1204)
24. **"The effective recognition of claims as rights produces state quality in recognizing institutions."** (p. 1204)
    * *Gloss*: Statehood is not a binary status; it is a quality acquired by any institution that successfully enforces property and membership rights.
25. **"In exchange for recognized valuable property rights in land... people recognize the political power of the institution by payment of tax in the form of money, tribute, labour, allegiance, or other resources."** (p. 1205)
    * *Gloss*: The fiscal-sociological contract: property rights are secured in exchange for resource extraction and political compliance.
26. **"The capacity to recognize rights is also the capacity to deny and expunge them."** (p. 1205)
    * *Gloss*: A warning on the absolute power of the state. The registry that gives you a title is the same registry that can write you out of existence.

### Theme F: Institutional Pluralism and Fragmented Jurisdictions
27. **"We can think of jurisdictions as overlapping and interfering fields of authority in the making, rather than discrete realms of defined legal scope."** (p. 1210)
    * *Gloss*: Rejects the neat legal-rational map of administration. In reality, multiple authorities compete to resolve the same disputes.
28. **"Clever collusion to reduce land users' rights."** (p. 1212)
    * *Gloss*: The state and customary elites often collaborate (e.g., in Ghana) to expropriate smallholders and share the revenues, rather than clashing.
29. **"Parallel, practical contracts of recognition emerge where authority and rights are functional and effective but have only faint connections to official norms and law."** (p. 1208)
    * *Gloss*: Explains the informal economy of land administration. People build informal structures to secure what they believe is legally theirs.
30. **"By forming health committees, market guilds... before there is a clinic, a marketplace... citizens enter the orbit of certain governing institutions and conjure up the exercise of authority..."** (p. 1208)
    * *Gloss*: The performative aspect of citizenship. Marginalized groups organize in anticipation of state structures to force the state to recognize them.

### Research Question Alignment
