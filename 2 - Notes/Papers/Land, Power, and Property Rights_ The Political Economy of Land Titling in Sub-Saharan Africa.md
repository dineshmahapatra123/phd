## Research Design

Ribar's research design is built around a clear empirical puzzle: formal land titles are widely available across much of Sub-Saharan Africa, and the literature expects titles to increase tenure security and investment, yet actual title uptake remains low and highly uneven. The paper asks why some households formalise land claims while others do not, and why uptake varies so sharply across and within countries.

==The key theoretical move is to treat land titling not only as a state-supplied institution but also as a household decision shaped by local politics. Households are expected to seek titles when the value of land, or the expected returns to long-term investment in land, make the benefits of formalisation greater than its costs. But this demand-side logic is moderated by customary authority.== Chiefs and other customary elites may lose authority when titling transfers control over land to a central state agency. Conversely, if the formalisation process is devolved to local institutions, customary elites may capture the process and use it to reinforce their authority. The paper therefore studies the interaction between three forces: household economic incentives, strength of customary institutions, and the national design of the land tenure regime.

The paper uses a mixed, nested design. The first part is a cross-national quantitative analysis across 22 African countries. The second part is an in-depth case study of Côte d'Ivoire, used to trace the mechanism through which customary elites capture titling in a devolved land regime.

### Cross-National Quantitative Design

The cross-national analysis combines Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) and Living Standards Measurement Study (LSMS) data. The usable sample contains 170,216 rural landholding household observations across 62 survey waves in 22 countries. The outcome variable is whether a household possesses a title or government-recognised ownership document for at least one agricultural parcel. Ribar restricts the analysis to rural landholding households because the theory concerns agricultural land titling, not urban tenure or landless households.

The main explanatory variable is not observed land price, because informal and poorly documented African land markets make direct price measurement unreliable. Instead, Ribar constructs a geospatial measure of attainable agricultural land value. He combines FAO Global Agro-Ecological Zones data with IMF commodity prices to estimate the maximum attainable value of agricultural production per hectare in each 10 km by 10 km grid cell. In simple terms, the measure asks: given this place's soil, climate, terrain, and crop suitability, what is the highest-value crop that could plausibly be produced here, and what would that production be worth at international commodity prices?

Ribar then creates two related measures of investment returns:

- **Returns to fertiliser**: the increase in attainable value from using fertiliser, capturing a short-term investment incentive.
- **Returns to tree crops**: the increase in attainable value from planting tree crops rather than non-tree crops, capturing a longer-term investment incentive because crops such as coffee, cocoa, rubber, tea, coconuts, and bananas require delayed returns.

To measure customary institutional strength, the paper uses georeferenced data from Murdock's Ethnographic Atlas, especially the level of precolonial political hierarchy within local communities. The assumption is not that precolonial authority is unchanged, but that areas with more hierarchical precolonial institutions tend, on average, to have stronger contemporary customary authority. This is an indirect measure, but it has the advantage of broad spatial coverage across Africa.

The third major variable is the national land regime. Ribar codes each country as having either a **centralised** or **devolved** land tenure regime. The distinction is based on where the decision to grant title is effectively made, not merely where the final certificate is recorded. For example, if a national agency formally certifies a title but local village committees decide whose claim can proceed, the regime is treated as devolved. This distinction is central because the theory predicts opposite effects of customary authority under centralised versus devolved regimes.

The statistical models are linear probability models with country-survey-wave fixed effects. These fixed effects absorb broad national and temporal differences. The models include household controls such as age, sex, education, and marital status of the household head, along with district-level geographic controls such as area, population density, terrain ruggedness, caloric yield, road density, and highway density. The core models use triple interactions between land value or investment returns, strength of customary institutions, and whether the land regime is devolved.

The logic of the triple interaction is crucial. Ribar is not simply asking whether land value increases titling. He is asking whether the relationship between land value and titling changes depending on the strength of customary institutions, and whether that moderation itself differs between centralised and devolved land regimes.

The paper also addresses several threats to inference. It recognises that the land-value measure resembles a weighted exposure-to-common-shocks design, because crop suitability is interacted with global commodity prices. Ribar controls for average exposure to reduce bias from crop suitability being correlated with other drivers of titling. He also discusses the possible endogeneity of land-regime design, the risk that collective ownership may affect titling differently, and the possibility that donor-funded formalisation programmes mediate the relationship between land value and titling. The paper does not claim perfect causal identification; instead, it presents a carefully controlled observational design that combines cross-national breadth with mechanism tracing.

### Côte d'Ivoire Case Study

The Côte d'Ivoire case study is used to examine the mechanism behind the cross-national findings. Côte d'Ivoire is a useful case because its land regime is devolved: village-level land management committees play an important role in determining who can obtain a land certificate, even though the national agency ultimately certifies titles. This allows Ribar to study how local customary power operates inside a formal titling system.

The case study uses an original 2024 survey of 801 household heads and 194 customary elites across 80 administrative villages in the Ivorian cocoa belt, specifically in the Haut-Sassandra and Indenie-Djuablin regions. These regions are suitable for studying land-value and investment dynamics because they are agriculturally valuable areas where coffee, cocoa, rubber, and oil palm are important.

The case design also exploits an important social cleavage: the distinction between autochthones and allochthones. Autochthones are locally recognised as original inhabitants, while allochthones are migrants or descendants of migrants, often with long-term residence and substantial land use but weaker customary legitimacy. This distinction allows Ribar to test whether chiefly capture of titling benefits all households equally or advantages the in-group.

Chiefly authority is measured through survey questions asking respondents how much of the village would obey the chief in different activities, such as giving land for a school, repairing a road, planting trees, participating in village cleanup, or supporting traditional activities. Ribar constructs indices of chiefly political authority and traditional authority. He then examines whether stronger chiefs are associated with higher titling rates and whether this effect differs between autochthones and allochthones.

The case study also investigates the mechanism of capture. Ribar examines the composition of village land management committees, whether chiefs and customary actors dominate the titling pathway, whether households expect to pay customary authorities to formalise title, and whether titles are perceived as useful in disputes or expropriation contexts.

## Research Findings

==The paper's central finding is that land titling is shaped by both economic demand and local political authority. Households are more likely to title when the value of land or the returns to agricultural investment are higher. But this relationship is not uniform. It depends on whether the national land regime is centralised or devolved, and on whether customary authorities are strong enough to influence household behaviour and local titling procedures.==

### 1. Higher Land Value Increases Title Uptake

The first finding supports the demand-side theory. Households in areas with more valuable agricultural land are more likely to possess a title. In the cross-national models using maximum attainable agricultural value, a one standard deviation increase in land value is associated with roughly a 27 to 31 per cent increase over the baseline probability of possessing a title. This supports the idea that households pursue titles when the asset is valuable enough to protect.

This finding matters because it shifts the interpretation of low titling rates. Low uptake does not necessarily mean households are ignorant of the benefits of title or that formalisation programmes are unavailable. It may mean that the expected benefit of titling is too low relative to its financial, administrative, and political costs.

### 2. Returns to Agricultural Investment Are Strongly Associated with Titling

The investment-return results are stronger than the raw land-value results. Where fertiliser would substantially increase attainable agricultural value, households are more likely to hold titles. A one standard deviation increase in returns to fertiliser is associated with roughly a 21.7 to 22.6 per cent increase over the mean titling rate.

The long-term investment measure is also important. Where tree crops would generate higher returns than non-tree crops, households are more likely to possess titles. A one standard deviation increase in returns to tree crops is associated with roughly a 23.3 to 24.6 per cent increase over the baseline likelihood of title possession.

This supports the argument that titling is connected to investment horizons. Tree crops require high up-front costs and delayed returns. Households have stronger reasons to formalise land claims when they expect to make investments that only pay off over time.

### 3. Customary Institutions Have Opposite Effects in Centralised and Devolved Regimes

The paper's most important finding is the interaction between customary authority and land-regime design. Strong customary institutions do not have a single effect on titling. Their effect depends on whether titling is centralised or devolved.

In **centralised land regimes**, strong customary institutions weaken the relationship between land value and title uptake. This supports Ribar's argument that chiefs have incentives to impede formalisation when titling moves land out of the customary system and into a central state system. In these contexts, titling threatens chiefs' authority because it reduces their role in land allocation, dispute resolution, and local recognition.

In **devolved land regimes**, the relationship reverses. Strong customary institutions strengthen the relationship between land value and titling. This is because chiefs and local customary authorities can capture or control the local formalisation process. If titling does not remove land from local authority, chiefs can facilitate titling while still maintaining practical power over land.

The conclusion gives the clearest substantive contrast. In devolved regimes, a $1,000 increase in returns to long-term agricultural investment is associated with a 14 per cent increase in titling rates where customary institutions are weakest, but a 55 per cent increase where customary institutions are strongest. In centralised regimes, the same increase is associated with a 63 per cent increase in title likelihood where customary institutions are weakest, but only a 34 per cent increase where customary institutions are strongest.

The broader implication is that land titling does not simply substitute formal state authority for customary authority. Depending on institutional design, formalisation can either displace customary power or become a tool through which customary elites preserve and extend that power.

### 4. The Wealth Alternative Is Not the Main Explanation

Ribar tests whether the results are simply driven by wealth. If richer households live in higher-value areas, perhaps they title more because they can better afford the process. To probe this, he uses dwelling titles as a placebo outcome. If wealth were the main mechanism, land value should also predict title possession for dwellings. The placebo regressions show null effects, suggesting that the agricultural land results are not simply a wealth effect.

The paper also reports that population density does not predict titling rates in the way the theory would require if it were merely capturing land scarcity or settlement intensity. The results are also robust across different specifications, including excluding Ethiopia and Rwanda, log-transforming geospatial variables, and restricting the analysis to the most recent survey waves.

### 5. In Côte d'Ivoire, Strong Chiefs Facilitate Titling for In-Groups and Disadvantage Out-Groups

The Côte d'Ivoire case study explains how customary capture works in practice. In a devolved regime, strong chiefs are associated with higher titling, but not equally for everyone. Chiefly political authority increases titling for autochthones, the local in-group. Among autochthones, a one standard deviation increase in chiefs' political authority is associated with a 34 per cent increase over the baseline titling rate.

For allochthones, the relationship goes in the opposite direction. A one standard deviation increase in chiefly political authority is associated with a 22 per cent decrease relative to the baseline titling rate. This suggests that chiefly capture of formalisation can increase titles for socially favoured groups while restricting title access for migrants or outsider groups.

This finding is important because it shows that higher titling rates are not necessarily equitable. A local elite may facilitate formalisation overall while using the process to discriminate among claimants.

### 6. Chiefs Capture the Titling Process Through Local Committees and Informal Payments

The mechanism of capture operates through control over village land management committees and informal authority over the titling process. In Côte d'Ivoire, village land management committees investigate claims and issue dossiers for potential land certificates, while the national agency records and certifies the title. Ribar shows that these committees tend to be dominated by autochthones even in villages that are mixed or mostly allochthonous.

The paper also finds that households expect customary actors to be involved financially even when they have no formal legal role. Seventy per cent of surveyed respondents expected to have to pay chiefs to formalise title, while only 57 per cent expected to pay the village land management committee, the body formally empowered in the process. On average, respondents expected to pay 3.8 customary actors with no de jure position in order to acquire title.

This demonstrates that formal land administration can be locally captured even when the official legal structure locates authority elsewhere. The decisive question is not only who issues the certificate, but who controls verification, social recognition, and passage through the process.

### 7. Titles Are Less Effective Where Chiefs Can Override Them

The case study also shows that titles may not constrain local power equally. Respondents were asked whether a land certificate would help them keep land if the government tried to take it, receive compensation if land were taken, or succeed in a dispute. Allochthones generally saw titles as useful, but stronger chiefly authority reduced the perceived usefulness of titles, especially for allochthones.

This suggests that titles do not automatically remove land from local politics. Where chiefs remain powerful, they may be able to shape whether a title is practically enforceable. Formal documentation exists, but its effectiveness depends on the local institutional environment.

### 8. The Paper's Main Contribution

==Ribar's main contribution is to connect household demand for property rights with the political economy of local authority.== Much of the property-rights literature asks why states or elites supply secure property rights. Ribar adds that households make strategic decisions about whether to seek titles, and those decisions are conditioned by the local authorities who can facilitate, block, capture, or selectively enforce formalisation.

The paper also explains why informal institutions can appear to complement the state in some cases and substitute for or obstruct it in others. The same strong customary institution can impede formalisation in a centralised system but facilitate it in a devolved system. The difference lies in whether formalisation threatens local authority or can be appropriated by it.

### PhD Relevance

For the Odisha thesis, the paper is useful as a comparative political-economy warning. It does not provide direct evidence about India, but it offers a mechanism for thinking about DILRMP, mutation, Bhulekh/Bhunaksha integration, and record correction. Formalisation should not be analysed only as a technical process of producing clearer records. ==It should also be analysed as a process mediated by local authority, land value, administrative cost, and unequal access to recognition.==

The closest Odisha analogue is not chiefly authority, but the practical authority of RIs, Amins, local brokers, panchayat actors, caste networks, dominant landholders, and revenue-office intermediaries. Ribar's framework suggests asking:

- When do households find it worthwhile to update, correct, or formalise land records?
- Who controls the local verification of possession, boundaries, inheritance, and partition?
- Does formalisation weaken local intermediaries, or give them new opportunities to control access?
- Are stronger claimants better able to convert ground possession into record recognition?
- Do women, tenants, co-sharers, informal possessors, encroachers, or marginal caste groups face higher risks when records are clarified?

The paper therefore strengthens two core arguments in the thesis: first, that land-record modernisation cannot be understood without political economy; second, that formal records may increase security for some while reorganising or weakening the claims of others.



# Literature Review: "Land, Power, and Property Rights" (Ribar, 2026)

## 1. Core Argument

In *Land, Power, and Property Rights*, Matthew K. Ribar resolves a major empirical anomaly in agrarian development: why do only 15% of African households possess a formal land title for their agricultural land despite widespread availability, international aid subsidies, and documented investment benefits? The paper argues that this uneven uptake is explained by the strategic interactions between **local politics** (specifically the strength of customary chiefs) and **national land regimes** (centralized vs. devolved). 

Households behave as rational actors, deciding to formalize their land only when the potential returns to agricultural investments outweigh the administrative costs and social risks of formalization. However, this demand-side calculus is heavily moderated by customary elites who act defensively to preserve their authority. In countries with **centralized land tenure regimes** (where titling is managed by distant national agencies), formalization removes land from customary arbitration, threatening the chiefs' role as development and dispute-resolution intermediaries. Consequently, strong customary institutions actively *impede* and *attenuate* the relationship between rising land values and titling. In contrast, in countries with **devolved land tenure regimes** (where village-level bodies make key titling decisions), chiefs can *capture* the process, staffing land committees with co-ethnics and extracting rents. In these devolved settings, strong customary institutions *reinforce* and *facilitate* the relationship between land values and titling, using the formalization process to entrench their authority and advance their political agendas by discriminating against out-groups.

> "In countries with centralized land tenure regimes, titling erodes the authority of intermediaries who then impede formalization when they can. In countries which devolve responsibilities for land tenure administration to local governments, customary elites facilitate land titling because they can capture the process and maintain their authority." (p. 399)

## 2. Conceptual Architecture

The paper establishes several core conceptual building blocks to analyze the political economy of demand-driven titling:

* **Centralized Land Tenure Regimes**:
  * *Verbatim*: "In countries with centralized land tenure regimes, decisions around land tenure formalization are made at the national level... Land administration and titling occur in Monrovia far from customary institutions... [or] is managed entirely by the state." (p. 403)
  * *Gloss*: Systems where state agencies directly map, adjudicate, and record titles without local customary intermediaries, making it harder for village chiefs to control the process or claim credit.
* **Devolved Land Tenure Regimes**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Other countries devolve their land tenure regimes to local authorities... where land tenure administration is devolved, chiefs are able to capture the land tenure formalization process." (p. 403)
  * *Gloss*: Systems where the statutory authority to adjudicate, issue, or recommend land certificates is nested within local municipal or village-level bodies, creating structural opportunities for chiefly capture.
* **Customary Institution Strength (Precolonial Hierarchy)**:
  * *Verbatim*: "To measure the extent to which chiefs are able to act on their incentives to impede or facilitate titling, I use georeferenced data from Murdock’s Ethnographic Atlas... the precolonial institution’s level of hierarchy, which Murdock measures [by the number of hierarchical layers]." (p. 405)
  * *Gloss*: The historical complexity and hierarchical depth of local traditional authorities, which determines their contemporary capacity to enforce decrees, sanction dissidents, or capture local state bodies.
* **Attainable Yield / Land Value**:
  * *Verbatim*: "I implement a novel measurement of 'attainable value': the value for which the maximum attainable yield (per hectare) could sell for on the international commodity market." (p. 405)
  * *Gloss*: A geospatial measure of potential agricultural land value calculated by multiplying GAEZ crop suitability indexes by international commodity prices, bypassing the illegibility of informal land markets.
* **Strategic Ambiguity**:
  * *Verbatim*: "In much of Africa, agricultural parcels are subject to overlapping ownerships which exist in a state of strategic ambiguity... Land titling forces the issue of hierarchy between these partial owners." (p. 401)
  * *Gloss*: The co-existence of multiple overlapping claims (usufruct, grazing, lineage ownership) on a single parcel, which is disrupted by the binary, zero-sum nature of formal titling.
* **Autochthone vs. Allochthone Divide**:
  * *Verbatim*: "Land tenure in Côte d’Ivoire pits autochthones, or sons of the soil, against allochthones, or relative newcomers... Village chiefs are almost always autochthones. Later arrivals are called 'allochthones,' or simply 'strangers.'" (p. 411)
  * *Gloss*: The deep social and political distinction between original settlers (who claim customary sovereignty and dominate local committees) and subsequent migrants (who hold usufruct rights but face exclusion from formal property titles).

## 3. Empirical and Evidence Terrain

Ribar employs a nested, mixed-method research design combining cross-national statistical analysis with subnational fieldwork:

* **Cross-National Dataset**:
  * **Sample**: 170,216 rural, land-owning household observations from 62 survey waves across 22 African countries (DHS and LSMS data from 2010–2021).
  * **Geospatial Control**: Integrates FAO's GAEZ model (v4) at a 10km-by-10km grid cell level to estimate land values and returns.
  * **Specification**: OLS linear probability models with country-survey wave fixed effects and triple interactions (Land Value $\times$ Precolonial Hierarchy $\times$ Devolved Land Regime).
  * **Placebo Test**: Evaluates the effect of land values on formal titles for *dwellings* (finding no relationship), ruling out household wealth as the primary causal driver.
* **Case Study: Côte d’Ivoire (Cocoa Belt)**:
  * **Sample**: Original survey of 801 household heads and 194 customary elites (village chiefs/headmen) across 80 villages in the Indénié-Djuablin and Haut-Sassandra regions.
  * **Key Findings**:
    * **CVGFR Staffing**: Autochthones overwhelmingly dominate Village Land Management Committees (CVGFRs) even in villages where they represent a minority or are equally split (e.g., in mostly allochthone villages, 15 out of 16 committees were mostly autochthonous).
    * **Inequal Uptake**: In Côte d'Ivoire (a devolved regime), a 1 SD increase in chiefs' political authority is associated with a 13-percentage-point *increase* in titling for autochthones (in-group) but a 9-percentage-point *decrease* for allochthones (out-group).
    * **Uneven Enforcement**: A 1 SD increase in chiefs' traditional authority decreases the perceived usefulness of titles (to keep land or resolve disputes) by 0.50–0.53 SDs for allochthones, indicating that powerful chiefs can overrule formal certificates to protect customary hierarchies.
    * **Rent Extraction**: 70% of respondents expected to pay chiefs to formalize a land title, compared to only 57% who expected to pay the legally mandated CVGFR.

If the local land administration (RI/Amin/Tehsildar) is susceptible to capture by dominant local castes, digital modernisation will entrench existing agrarian hierarchies rather than provide secure title.
* **RQ3 & RQ4 (Disjuncture between records and ground reality)**: The paper's concept of *strategic ambiguity* (overlapping customary and usufruct rights) matches the Indian reality of unregistered partitions, sharecropping, and commons usage. Formalization (digitisation) forces a zero-sum resolution that widens the gap between the official record and actual village possession.
* **RQ5 (Technocratic reforms vs. political economy)**: Ribar directly addresses whether computerisation resolves conflicts or restates them. Modernisation programs are technocratic, but their success is dictated by local political structures (chiefly authority in Africa, caste/revenue bureaucracies in India).
