# Highlights


Colonial conquest was not just the result of the power of superior arms, military organization, political power, or economic wealth—as important as these things were. Colonialism was made possible, and then sustained and strengthened, as much by cultural technologies of rule as it was by the more obvious and brutal modes of conquest that first established power on foreign shores. The cultural effects of colonialism have too often been ignored or displaced into the inevitable logic of modernization and world capitalism; but more than this, it has not been sufficiently recognized that colonialism was itself a cultural project of control. Colonial knowledge both enabled con quest and was produced by it; in certain important ways, knowledge was what colonialism was all about. Cultural forms in societies newly classi fied as “traditional” were reconstructed and transformed by and through this knowledge, whieh created new categories and oppositions between colonizers and colonized, European and Asian, modern and traditional, West and East.

Bernard Cohn has played a critical role in identifying and exploring these relations between colonialism and cultural transformation m the last three hundred years of South Asian history. Long before the power ful theoretical proposals of Michel Foucault made “knowledge" a term that seemed irrevocably linked to power, and before Edward Said so provocatively opened up discussion of the relations between power and knowledge in colonial discourses and Orientalist scholarship, Bernard Cohn had begun to apply an anthropological perspective to the history of colonialism and its forms of knowledge. In a series of essays written between the mid-1950s and the early 1980s, and now published in An Anthropologist among the Historians and Other Essays (Delhi: Oxford University Press, 1986), Cohn wrote about the history of British colonial knowledge regarding India, about the specific careers of terms like “vil lage,” “tribe,” and “caste,” about the anthropology of the colonizers as well as the colonized, and more generally about the anthropology of British rule itself.

Cohns anthropological agenda is firmly harnessed to a commitment to historicize the analytical schemas and institutional arenas of colonial knowledge and practice. And Cohns historical sensibility is demonstrated in rich ethnographic explorations of the varieties of colonial mentalities and ways of life. 

We read that the painstaking efforts by British Orientalists to study Indian languages was not part of a collaborative enterprise responsible for a new renaissance, but rather was an important part of the colonial proj ect of control and command. We read that the very Orientalist imagina tion that led to brilliant antiquarian collections, archaeological finds, and photographic forays were in fact forms of constructing an India that could be better packaged, subsumed, and ruled. We read that concerns about appropriate forms of dress expressed the colonial imagination of domination, and worked later to demonstrate the colonial domination of imagination in the arena of nationalist resistance and appropriation.

# Review 1

Cohn's central claim is that British colonial rule in India was not only a military, fiscal, or legal project. It was also a knowledge project. The British made India governable by converting complex social, legal, linguistic, spatial, and material worlds into state-readable forms: grammars, surveys, settlement reports, legal digests, maps, censuses, museum catalogues, ethnographies, and official proceedings.

For this PhD, the most important version of the argument is narrower: colonial land administration did not merely record land relations. It reorganised them. Through survey, settlement, revenue assessment, and legal codification, colonial rule translated layered agrarian relations into administrative facts. This helps explain why postcolonial land records are simultaneously powerful and unreliable: they inherit a state form of knowledge that was designed around revenue, classification, and control rather than around perfect correspondence with lived possession.

## Conceptual Architecture

### Colonial Knowledge-Power

Cohn treats colonial knowledge as constitutive of rule. The state did not simply collect neutral descriptions of India; it created usable categories through which India could be administered. This is most visible in the relationship between reports, statistical returns, legal codes, histories, surveys, and institutions of rule.

PhD use: this gives Chapter 1 a way to frame land records as state knowledge rather than mere administrative paperwork.

### Investigative Modalities

This is the book's master concept. An investigative modality is a state-oriented method for collecting, ordering, classifying, and converting information into usable administrative knowledge. Cohn lists historiographic, observational/travel, survey, enumerative, museological, and surveillance modalities.

PhD use: the survey and enumerative modalities are the only ones that should be heavily used. The others are useful as background but not central.

### Survey Modality

The survey modality is the strongest concept for this PhD. Cohn shows that survey in colonial India meant more than measuring land. It included inspecting, supervising, valuing, mapping, bounding, classifying, and documenting both natural and social features of territory. The survey produced a government-readable territory.

PhD use: direct relevance to cadastral maps, Bhunaksha, Survey and Settlement, ULPIN, parcel-level verification, and the spatial-textual mismatch.

### Enumerative Modality

Enumeration converts people and society into countable categories. Cohn's main example is the census, especially caste, religion, occupation, age, literacy, residence, and domestic organisation. The key point is that enumeration does not merely reflect categories; it hardens them.

PhD use: useful for caste and class patterns in land ownership, welfare eligibility, and RoR as an entitlement gate.

### Documentation Project

Cohn's colonial state works through documentation: reports, enquiries, commissions, records, maps, histories, and statistical returns. This produces a totalising and individualising form of rule: the state classifies the whole population while also locating individual subjects within administrative categories.

PhD use: DILRMP can be framed as a digital continuation of this documentation logic, but the analogy must remain precise.

### Colonial Legal Reconstitution of Property

Chapter 3 shows how the colonial state converted plural, locally mediated, and textually complex legal traditions into court-administered legal forms. Hastings, Jones, Halhed, Colebrooke, pandits, maulavis, and British judges all appear in a process that claims to preserve Indian law while converting it into something usable by British courts.

PhD use: this is central for inheritance, succession, joint khata, partition, presumptive title, and the legal basis of record-ground mismatch.

### Intermediaries and Translation

Cohn repeatedly shows that British official knowledge depended on Indian intermediaries: munshis, pandits, maulavis, scribes, translators, vakils, and local informants. The British relied on these intermediaries while also trying to subordinate or bypass their interpretive authority.

PhD use: this is useful for analysing RIs, Amins, data-entry operators, document writers, local brokers, and the mutation/correction economy around land records.

## Internal Tensions and Silences

### 1. Cohn is not a land-records author

The book is foundational for colonial knowledge and useful for land administration history, but it is not directly about RoR, cadastral record maintenance, mutation, Odisha, DILRMP, or village-level record-ground mismatch. Its value is conceptual and genealogical.

### 2. Odisha is absent

Cohn's strongest empirical terrain is Bengal, upper India, Banaras, Madras, Mysore, and imperial institutions. Odisha must be connected through Bengal Presidency, Madras Presidency, Orissa Survey and Settlement Act, and later Odisha-specific revenue administration. The extension must be done by the thesis, not assumed from Cohn.

### 3. The postcolonial transition is asserted more than traced

Cohn and Dirks indicate that colonial knowledge persists after colonialism, but the book does not analyse postcolonial land administration. This PhD's originality lies partly in tracing that afterlife through DILRMP and Odisha fieldwork.

### 4. The village subject is underdeveloped

Although Cohn is historically anthropological, this book mostly works through official archives, texts, elites, institutions, and representational practices. The peasant whose land is measured or whose possession is misrecorded remains largely off-stage.

### 5. The state is sometimes too coherent

Cohn shows contradictions among Orientalists, utilitarians, administrators, scholars, and Indian intermediaries. Still, the broader frame can make "the British" appear more unified than colonial administration often was.

### 6. Chapter 5 is theoretically rich but thesis-weak

The cloth chapter is excellent cultural history, but the link to land records is too indirect. It should not be allowed to distract from Chapters 1 and 3.

# Review 2
Bernard S. Cohn’s central thesis is that the British conquest of India was fundamentally a **conquest of knowledge**. Colonialism was not merely sustained by military superior arms, political power, or economic wealth, but was first and foremost a cultural project of control enabled by **cultural technologies of rule**. In coming to India, the British invaded and conquered an indigenous **epistemological space**, translating dynamic, fluid, and context-dependent local practices into static, standardized European administrative objects.

This epistemological conquest was operationalized through what Cohn defines as **investigative modalities**—historiography, observation/travel, survey, enumeration, museology, and surveillance. Through these modalities, the state collected, ordered, classified, and transformed native realities into usable administrative texts (gazetteers, census tables, settlement reports, and legal codes) to establish its governing capacity. 

In the critical domains of language and law (Chapters 2 and 3), Cohn demonstrates that linguistic mastery and legal codification were not neutral administrative improvements but active projects of command and reification. By taming Sanskrit, Persian, and regional vernaculars into standard grammars, the British created a "language of command." Concurrently, by translating and reconstructing dynamic Hindu and Muslim legal traditions into a rigid, precedent-based system of case law to govern property and succession, the British carried out a **colonial legal reconstitution of property**. This project sought to eliminate the flexible, discretionary power of local interpreters, establishing a presumptive, revenue-focused administrative database that permanently decoupled official records from lived agrarian realities.

### Defining Quote
> "Colonial conquest was not just the result of the power of superior arms, military organization, political power, or economic wealth—as important as these things were. Colonialism was made possible, and then sustained and strengthened, as much by cultural technologies of rule as it was by the more obvious and brutal modes of conquest that first established power on foreign shores... The cultural effects of colonialism have too often been ignored or displaced into the inevitable logic of modernization and world capitalism; but more than this, it has not been sufficiently recognized that colonialism was itself a cultural project of control. Colonial knowledge both enabled conquest and was produced by it; in certain important ways, knowledge was what colonialism was all about." (p. ix)

---

## 2. Conceptual Architecture

*   **Cultural Technologies of Rule**: The administrative, linguistic, taxonomic, and cartographic instruments through which the colonial state constructed a highly legible and controlled version of social reality, enabling the domination of both physical territory and the native imagination.
*   **Investigative Modalities**: The highly structured officializing procedures designed to define, collect, order, and classify information, transforming it into standardized, authoritative texts (published reports, statistical returns, historical narratives, and legal codes) that serve as the foundation of modern state capacity.
*   **Epistemological Space**: The pre-existing landscape of indigenous meanings, conceptual categories, linguistic structures, and customary arrangements which the British invaded and subsequently restructured by forcing it through translation and European legal classifications.
*   **The Documentation Project**: The totalizing and individualizing project of state-building that records, certifies, and categorizes subjects. It naturalizes social divisions (e.g., educated/uneducated, landlord/tenant, Hindu/Muslim) by embedding them into official databases, creating state-sanctioned identities that dictate access to resources and legal recognition.
*   **Colonial Legal Reconstitution of Property**: The process by which the British reconstructed dynamic, context-dependent customary land relations into a rigid, text-bound Whig property rights framework to secure revenue collection and market readability, permanently splitting legal "Record" from physical "Possession."
*   **Command of Language and Language of Command**: The translation, codification, and taming of Indian vernaculars into standardized grammars and dictionaries. This converted fluid, spoken communication into a standardized administrative apparatus for issuing orders, extracting revenue data, and supervising native subordinates.
*   **The "Despotic" vs. "Theocratic" Models**: The dual administrative paradigms of the colonial mind. The first assumed India was a lawless vacuum ruled by arbitrary despots, justifying "strong hand" patriarchal administration (the *ma-bap* revenue hierarchy). The second viewed India as an ancient, static theocracy governed by sacred texts, justifying the aggressive codification of religious scriptures to govern civil property.

---

## 3. Empirical & Evidence Terrain

*   **Warren Hastings' Judicial Plan of 1772**: The foundational administrative policy that structurally bifurcated civil justice in Bengal, decreeing that "suits regarding inheritance, marriage, caste and other religious usages" would be governed by the Shastras for Hindus and the Quran for Muslims. This linked property rights directly to textual religious codification.
*   **Halhed’s Code of Gentoo Laws (1776)**: The compilation by eleven Bengal pandits (*Vivadarnavasetu*) translated tortuously from Sanskrit to Bengali to Persian, and finally to English. This was the first major British attempt to objectify and fix Hindu civil law for district courts.
*   **Sir William Jones' Sanskrit Translation and Manu (1780s-1790s)**: Jones' systematic study of Sanskrit at Nadiya, driven by a Whiggish distrust of native interpreters (pandits). His translation of the *Institutes of Hindu Law, or the Ordinances of Menu* reified ancient Sanskrit scriptures as the absolute civil code governing property, contracts, and succession.
*   **Colebrooke's Digest of Hindu Law (1798)**: The completion of Jones' Whig-property project, which aggressively excised religious, philosophical, and ritual contexts from Sanskrit texts to isolate a dry civil code of contracts and successions, aligning indigenous law with British precedent-based case law.
*   **Colin Mackenzie’s Deccan Surveys (1799-1815)**: The pioneering topographical and historical mapping of southern India. Mackenzie triangulated boundaries while using Brahmin assistants to accumulate historical records, genealogies, and antiquities, illustrating how spatial surveys were deeply intertwined with the extraction of local histories.

## 4. The Quote Bank

### Theme A: Investigative Modalities and the Documentation Project

> [!NOTE]
> *Quotes in this theme examine how the modern state utilizes officializing procedures, surveys, and database compilation as active instruments of rule to categorize and control populations.*

1. **On the transition of state power to bureaucratic "officializing"**:
   "From the eighteenth century onward, European states increasingly made their power visible not only through ritual performance and dramatic display, but through the gradual extension of 'officializing' procedures that established and extended their capacity in many areas. They took control by defining and classifying space... by recording transactions such as the sale of property; by counting and classifying their populations... and by standardizing languages and scripts." (p. 3)
   *Gloss: Undergirds the transition from precolonial personalized authority to the modern technocratic state. Digitization programs like DILRMP represent the absolute peak of these "officializing" procedures, replacing customary local negotiations with centralized, digital algorithms.*
2. **On the state's reliance on historical and textual documentation**:
   "The establishment and maintenance of these nation states depended upon determining, codifying, controlling, and representing the past. The documentation that was involved created and normalized a vast amount of information that formed the basis of their capacity to govern." (p. 3)
   *Gloss: The state cannot govern a territory without first "normalizing" it into a database. The creation of Odisha's "Bhulekh" portal is a direct continuation of this colonial project—standardizing complex spatial-social relations into a uniform digital archive.*
3. **On the metropole and colony as a unitary analytical field**:
   "A guiding assumption in my research on the British conquest of India... has been that metropole and colony 'have to be seen in a unitary field of analysis.' In India the British entered a new world that they tried to comprehend using their own forms of knowing and thinking. There was widespread agreement that this society... could be known and represented as a series of facts." (p. 4)
   *Gloss: Critiques the technocratic hubris of contemporary land administration reforms (often funded and designed by the World Bank). These reforms treat property formalization as a "neutral science" rather than an imported, Eurocentric epistemological framework.*
4. **On the nature of the "epistemological space"**:
   "In coming to India, they unknowingly and unwittingly invaded and conquered not only a territory but an epistemological space as well. The 'facts' of this space did not exactly correspond to those of the invaders. Nevertheless, the British believed they could explore and conquer this space through translation..." (p. 4)
   *Gloss: Direct relevance to the disjuncture between official land records (RoR) and ground reality. The state "conquered" the agrarian space by translating multi-layered customary possession into the rigid language of "individual ownership" and "Khata numbers."*
5. **On the definition of an "investigative modality"**:
   "An investigative modality includes the definition of a body of information that is needed, the procedures by which appropriate knowledge is gathered, its ordering and classification, and then how it is transformed into usable forms such as published reports, statistical returns, histories, gazetteers, legal codes, and encyclopedias." (p. 5)
   *Gloss: Provides the precise theoretical framework to analyze the DILRMP. E-mutation, ULPIN, and Bhunaksha are not mere "IT tools" but components of a modern investigative modality designed to reconstruct agrarian society into a highly legible tax-and-transaction grid.*
6. **On the land-settlement process as a historical documentation project**:
   "Out of this grew the most extensive and continuous administrative activity of the British, which they termed the land-settlement process. Entailed in this enterprise was the collection of 'customs and local histories,' which in the British discourse related to land tenure. The process culminated in the production of settlement reports..." (p. 5)
   *Gloss: Explains why modern Indian land records remain fundamentally historical and path-dependent. Every digital "RoR" in Nayagarh today is structurally bound by the colonial Survey and Settlement reports that first froze local social dynamics into written revenue titles.*
7. **On the totalizing and individualizing nature of the state's database**:
   "Cohn now directs his interpretive gaze on the reports, investigations, commissions, and data of colonial regulation, which he sees as part of a 'documentation project' that is 'both totalizing and individualizing.' Not only are subjects ruled by the state; the position of the subjects are constituted by classifying and naturalizing such categories..." (p. xi / Foreword)
   *Gloss: Key analytical tool for Chapter 3.2. Digitization is "totalizing" because it aggregates all land parcels under a single central state database (Bhulekh), yet "individualizing" because it uses ULPIN to pin property responsibility to a single, atomized individual, erasing collective and customary rights.*

### Theme C: Law, Reification of Texts, and the Reconstruction of Property

> [!NOTE]

18. **On the revalorization of the "despotic model" of strong-hand rule**:

    *Gloss: A brilliant metaphor for the "IT translation" in modern land records. 23. **On Sir William Jones modeling his role on the Roman Justinian**:

24. **On the Whiggish Whig-property focus of the Sanskrit Digest**:
    "The object was to find and fix a Hindu civil law concerned with the topics that Jones, a Whig in political and legal philosophy, was centrally concerned with—those rights, public and private, that affected the ownership and transmission of property." (p. 71)
    *Gloss: Crucial for Chapter 2.4. Hindu and Islamic personal laws were codified not out of cultural respect, but to construct a stable Whig framework of private property rights, enabling secure land transactions and taxation.*
25. **On the contradiction of English common law vs. Indian codified law**:

But it appears that Jones believed that... the Hindus of India had usages that were fixed from time immemorial... and differences in interpretations offered by pandits must have arisen from ignorance or venality." (p. 71)
    *Gloss: The central contradiction of land administration in India. The British imposed a static, text-bound legal system on India while maintaining a dynamic case-law system at home. Today, the state demands absolute, rigid digital conformity, treating organic customary adjustments as "illegal errors."*
26. **On the reification and systematic refashioning of Indian legal traditions**:

    *Gloss: The "colonial legal reconstitution of property" created a hybrid monster: a system of presumptive titles that reified texts and maps while completely divorcing them from the dynamic, living practice of village land relations.*
27. **On the exclusion of Indian scholars from scientific capital**:

28. **On the structural misfit of colonial legal imports**:
    "Colonialism produced a level of institutional self-mimesis in India that was completely at odds with its own self-representation and rhetoric." (p. xv / Foreword)
    *Gloss: Captures the tragic irony of DILRMP: under the rhetoric of "modernization" and "efficiency," the state merely replicates and digitizes the archaic, presumptive revenue records inherited from the East India Company.*

## Conceptual Architecture

### 1. Investigative Modalities (Page 5)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"An investigative modality includes the definition of a body of information that is needed, the procedures by which appropriate knowledge is gathered, its ordering and classification, and then how it is transformed into usable forms such as published reports, statistical returns, histories, gazetteers, legal codes, and encyclopedias."*
*   **Significance**: Underpins the land-settlement and census operations as state tools to "fix, bound, and settle" the population.

### 2. The Historiographical Modality (Pages 5–6)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"The historiographic practices and narrative genres of these writers can obviously be subjected to critical analysis, but beyond this they can be seen to have begun the formation of a legitimizing discourse... Out of this grew the most extensive and continuous administrative activity of the British, which they termed the land-settlement process."*
*   **Significance**: In land administration, this is realized as the "land-settlement process" and the production of district settlement reports, converting local customs into histories of land tenure to justify and stabilize tax collection.

### 3. The Survey Modality (Pages 7–8)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"The survey as an investigative modality encompasses a wide range of practices, from the mapping of India to collecting botanical specimens, to the recording of architectural and archaeological sites of historic significance, or the most minute measuring of a peasant's fields."*
*   **Significance**: Represents the systematic exploration and geometric bounding of the landscape, turning local territories into legible, cartographic resources for administrative control.

### 4. The Enumerative Modality (Page 293)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"A number was, for the British, a particular form of certainty to be held on to in a strange world. But when they turned to early attempts to enumerate the population... they found that even the simplest of enumerative projects raised problems of classification."*
*   **Significance**: Unpacks how population counts and censuses reified caste, occupation, and religion into rigid, numerical categories for state planning and control.

### 5. Substantive Theory of Authority (Page 114 / 132)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: *"Hindus and Muslims operated with an unbounded substantive theory of objects and persons. The body of the ruler was literally his authority, the substance of which could be transmitted in what Europeans thought of as objects. Clothes, weapons, jewels, and paper were the means by which a ruler could transmit the substance of his authority to a chosen companion."*
*   **Significance**: Contrasts the Western "sign-based" contractual model of property with the indigenous concept of physical, relational incorporation.

### 6. Epistemic Translation and Violence (Page 4, Page 71)
*   **Verbatim Definition**: The process of forcing fluid, oral, and contextual indigenous knowledge into rigid, Western textual equivalents to serve the state's legal and revenue apparatus.
*   **Significance**: Strips local practices of their context (e.g., separating religious or ethical duties from property rules) to isolate clean, Whiggish categories of contract and inheritance.

## Empirical / Evidence Terrain

Cohn builds his historical anthropology of the colonial state through several rich archives:
*   **The Bengal Revenue Debates (1760–1790)**: Administrative records, letters from Robert Clive, and Warren Hastings's 1772 Plan for the Administration of Justice, tracking the shift of the East India Company from a commercial operator to a territorial sovereign after the 1765 Dewani of Bengal.
*   **Indological Translational Texts**: Halhed’s *A Code of Gentoo Laws* (1776), Sir William Jones's legal manuscripts, and H. T. Colebrooke's translation of *The Digest of Hindu Law on Contracts and Successions* (1798).
*   **Survey Archives**: The Mysore Survey (1800–1810) files, tracking Colin Mackenzie's use of Telugu Brahman assistants (C. V. Boria and Luchmiah) to collect "local tracts" and temple registers.
*   **The Breast-Cloth Controversy Files (Travancore, 1859)**: Resident General Cullen's reports and missionary records tracking caste riots, state decrees, and the reinterpretation of Queen Victoria's 1858 Proclamation.

## Best Working Thesis Paragraph

Cohn's value for this PhD is to show that colonial land administration was not merely a fiscal apparatus but a knowledge apparatus. Through survey, settlement, enumeration, legal codification, and district reporting, the colonial state converted land, custom, and social relations into official facts. Contemporary land records modernisation inherits this architecture: Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, e-mutation, and ULPIN pursue cleaner digital legibility, but they may reproduce the older disjuncture between administrative categories and lived possession.


# Review 6

Cohn argues that British colonialism in India was not merely a political or economic project but fundamentally an epistemological conquest — an invasion and restructuring of knowledge itself. The British could not govern India without first making it legible on their own terms: they had to learn its languages, survey its land, codify its laws, catalogue its objects, and classify its people. Each of these operations was an "investigative modality," a procedure for gathering, ordering, and transforming Indian social reality into a body of official colonial knowledge. The cumulative effect was a massive "documentation project" that generated an epistemological space — a set of facts, categories, and representations — which gradually displaced the lived realities it purported to describe. Colonial rule thus operated through and as knowledge: the power to define became the power to govern.

The argument is structured across five case studies (language, law, objects, cloth, and an introductory theoretical chapter), each showing how one domain of Indian life was converted into a European administrative category. The epistemological stance is broadly Foucauldian: knowledge is inseparable from power, and the production of colonial knowledge was simultaneously the production of colonial subjects and colonial property.

> "In coming to India, the English had to learn the languages of India in order to issue commands, to collect information, and to settle the land revenue... In coming to India, they unknowingly and unwittingly invaded and conquered not only a territory but an epistemological space as well." (p. 5)

### 2. Conceptual Architecture

**Investigative Modality**
Cohn's master concept. An investigative modality is a "culturally constituted mode of enquiry" — a procedure by which the colonial state gathered information, ordered it, and transformed it into knowledge that could underwrite administrative action. Cohn identifies five: historiographic, observational/travel, survey, enumerative, museological, and surveillance. Each modality had its characteristic institutions, instruments, and personnel; together they constitute the epistemological infrastructure of colonial rule. The term is a borrowing from Foucault's concept of "apparatus" but is given empirical flesh through archival cases.

> "I will use the term 'investigative modalities' to refer to the vast effort in terms of texts — the reports, the gazetteers, the statistical tables, the maps, the histories, the grammars, the dictionaries, the legal codes — produced by Europeans to make India knowable." (Introduction)

**Epistemological Space**
The domain of knowledge — categories, facts, representations, official truths — that the colonial project both occupied and restructured. Before the British, India had its own epistemological organisation (Persian revenue administration, Sanskrit legal commentary, local cadastral knowledge). The British did not encounter a blank slate; they displaced and overwrote an existing epistemological order while claiming to recover or merely describe an indigenous one.

**Documentation Project**
The aggregate of all colonial knowledge-making activities. Not a single programme but the cumulative effect of generations of surveys, censuses, gazetteers, dictionaries, law compilations, museum collections, and intelligence reports. The documentation project created the "official version" of India — what the state knew — which eventually became the basis for all administrative action, regardless of its correspondence to ground reality.

**Discursive Formation / Orientalism**
Cohn is explicitly in dialogue with Said's *Orientalism*, which he predates as an empirical researcher. He treats colonial knowledge as a discursive formation: a set of statements, practices, and institutions that produce their objects rather than merely represent them. The British did not "discover" Hindu law or Indian property rights; they constituted them through the act of codification.

**Objectification**
The process by which Indian social forms — living, contested, context-dependent — were converted into bounded, enumerable, portable "objects": a grammar, a law code, a museum artefact, a census category. Objectification is the epistemic violence underlying the documentation project.

**Languages of Command**
Cohn's term for the successive languages the British adopted as the medium of colonial administration: Persian (Mughal inheritance), Sanskrit (Orientalist moment), Hindustani/Urdu (utilitarian turn under Gilchrist). Each shift represented a restructuring of the epistemological space — a new set of terms, categories, and interlocutors. The history of colonial language policy is simultaneously a history of who could know what, and who could be known.

> "There were two views on what language or languages should be used to educate Indians... The Orientalists, led by H.H. Wilson, argued for the continuation of Sanskrit and Persian as the media of instruction... The Anglicists, whose position was codified in Macaulay's Minute on Education of 1835, argued for the exclusive use of English." (Ch. 2)

**Native Informant / Munshi**
The Indian interlocutor through whom colonial knowledge was actually produced. Grammars, dictionaries, law digests, and revenue settlements were all mediated by Indian scholars, scribes, and interpreters whose contributions were systematically erased in the official record. The "munshi" (language teacher/secretary) is Cohn's exemplary figure for this invisible mediation.

**Khilat and Substantive Authority**
Analysed in the cloth chapter. *Khilat* (robe of honour) was a South Asian system for constituting political relationships through the giving and wearing of cloth. Authority was not abstract but was literally embodied in cloth: to wear a king's robe was to be incorporated into his substance and political order. The British misread this as mere ceremony and gift-giving, thereby systematically misunderstanding and undermining the substantive political relations they were navigating.

**Despotic Model / Theocratic Model**
Two competing British frameworks for interpreting Indian society, deployed in the law chapter. The despotic model (associated with Halhed and early Hastings) held that Indian law was arbitrary and personal — the ruler's will. The theocratic model (Jones and the Orientalists) held that India had an ancient, textual, sacred law that needed to be recovered and codified. Both models were wrong in different ways; the theocratic model prevailed and produced the catastrophe Cohn documents in Chapter 3.

### 3. Evidence and Examples Used

Cohn's evidence is wholly archival and secondary. He draws on:

- **East India Company records**: Revenue correspondence, Presidency proceedings, Governor-General minutes — particularly for Chapter 3 (law and land revenue).
- **Orientalist published texts**: Jones's *Digest of Hindu Law*, Halhed's *Code of Gentoo Laws*, Colebrooke's legal digests, Gilchrist's Hindustani grammar — treated both as historical sources and as objects of analysis.
- **The Mackenzie Collection**: The archive of surveys, manuscripts, and objects gathered by Colin Mackenzie during his survey of Mysore (1800–1810). Analysed in Chapter 4 as the paradigmatic museological modality.
- **Amaravati marbles controversy**: The dispute over the removal of Buddhist sculpted panels from Amaravati to the British Museum, used to illustrate the logic of objectification and the subordination of Indian aesthetic and religious judgment to European categories.
- **James Fergusson's architectural history**: *History of Indian and Eastern Architecture* (1876), analysed as racial historiography — a system for ranking Indian architectural achievement against a European standard.
- **Rajendralal Mitra's counter-discourse**: Mitra's challenge to European art-historical categories in *The Antiquities of Orissa* (1875), treated as a case of Indian knowledge refusing objectification.
- **Travancore breast-cloth controversy**: The 1820s–1850s dispute over whether lower-caste women could wear upper-body cloth — analysed as a clash between cloth-as-substantive-authority and cloth-as-modesty/decency in European moral categories.
- **Gandhi's khadi programme**: Gandhi's deliberate construction of homespun cotton as nationalist uniform, read as a reversal of the colonial semiotic of cloth.

**Key empirical findings**: (1) Colonial language policy was not neutral instruction but the production of new epistemological hierarchies. (2) The Permanent Settlement was premised on a false theory of property (the search for a single "proprietor") that created systematic disjuncture between record and ground. (3) The codification of "Hindu law" produced a law that bore little resemblance to the diverse, locally interpreted normative orders it replaced. (4) Indian knowledge was systematically incorporated into colonial collections while Indian epistemological authority was systematically denied.

### 4. Theoretical Lineage

**Michel Foucault** — *Discipline and Punish*, *The Order of Things*, *Archaeology of Knowledge*
Cohn's "investigative modalities" are a direct extension of Foucault's concept of "apparatus" (dispositif) and his analysis of how modern states produce knowledge/subjects. Cohn applies the Foucauldian framework to a colonial and non-European context, anticipating much of what Bhabha, Spivak, and Said would later theorise.

**Edward Said** — *Orientalism* (1978)
Cohn's empirical work in many cases precedes Said's theoretical synthesis. Cohn accepts the basic Saidian claim that Orientalism produces its objects rather than merely describes them, but his case studies are more granular and more attentive to the internal heterogeneity of colonial knowledge-making. He does not cite Said as a framework; rather, both are drawing from a common Foucauldian well.

**Ranajit Guha and the Subaltern Studies Collective**
Cohn is a formative influence on Subaltern Studies and contributed directly to the early volumes. His insistence on colonial knowledge as power, and on the erasure of Indian agency in colonial archives, is foundational to the subalternist project. Guha's *A Rule of Property for Bengal* (1963) is a complementary text on the Permanent Settlement.

**Max Weber** — rationalisation, bureaucracy, legal-rational authority
The transition from personal/substantive to formal/procedural authority that Weber theorised as "rationalisation" is the deep structure of Cohn's account of colonial law-making. The codification of Hindu law is a Weberian rationalisation of a non-rational normative order — with devastating consequences.

**McKim Marriott and Ronald Inden** — ethnosociology, "dividual" personhood
Cohn draws on their work in Chapter 5 (cloth) to argue that South Asian personhood is not the bounded individual of European legal theory but a "dividual" whose identity is constituted through transactions with substances (food, cloth, bodily fluids). This makes the British misreading of *khilat* not merely ignorant but ontologically misconceived.

**Norbert Elias** — *The Civilizing Process*
Implicitly present in the cloth chapter: the European reduction of cloth to modesty/decency versus the South Asian understanding of cloth as substantive authority parallels Elias's account of how "civilisation" names European norms while rendering non-European practices as their negation.

### 6. Internal Tensions and Silences

**The intentionality problem** (visible throughout)
Cohn's account slides between the British "unknowingly and unwittingly" invading the epistemological space (p. 5) and a series of deliberate, policy-driven knowledge projects. Were the investigative modalities a conscious programme or a structural effect? Cohn never fully resolves this. The Introduction's "unwittingly" sits uneasily with Chapter 3's detailed account of Hastings's deliberate revenue policy.

**The subaltern voice is absent**
Cohn documents the production of colonial knowledge but rarely reaches the ground — the peasant who had his land measured, the woman forbidden to wear upper-body cloth before 1857, the village scribe whose local knowledge was extracted and then erased. This is acknowledged in the Foreword by Dirks but is a genuine limitation: what did the objects of the documentation project make of it?

**"The British" as a unified actor**
Cohn distinguishes Orientalists from Anglicists, Jones from Hastings, Mackenzie from Fergusson — but the book's framing often treats colonial knowledge production as a single coherent project. The internal conflicts, failures, reversals, and contradictions within the colonial administration are underweighted relative to the systemic argument.

**The Mughal epistemological order is underdeveloped**
Cohn acknowledges that the British encountered an existing Persian revenue and administrative apparatus, and that they initially worked within and through it. But the nature, scope, and limitations of Mughal epistemological ordering are not analysed. If the British inherited a documentation project, the pre-colonial baseline matters for assessing what was genuinely new.

**Class is underdeveloped**
The analysis of who benefited from and who was dispossessed by the documentation project is structurally present (the zamindars, the revenue farmers, the village scribes) but never developed into a class analysis. The Permanent Settlement created winners and losers, and the Indian intermediaries who facilitated the documentation project — the munshis, pandits, maulvis — had their own interests and strategies, which Cohn notes but does not systematically pursue.

**No periodisation**
The five chapters range across roughly 1765–1947, with the cloth chapter going to 1947 (Gandhi). But the argument treats colonial epistemological practice as relatively uniform across this period. The shift from Company raj to Crown raj (1858), the rise of the Indian National Congress, and the late-colonial statistical state all imply discontinuities that the "documentation project" framework papers over.

**The postcolonial state is not examined**
Cohn asserts that colonial forms of knowledge were inherited by the postcolonial state but provides no analysis of how, why, or with what modifications. This is the most consequential silence for the thesis — it is precisely the postcolonial inheritance that needs to be traced.

### 8. Ideas and Questions Sparked

**The RoR as an enumerative modality**
If Cohn's census converted "caste" from a local identity into a pan-Indian administrative category, the Record of Rights (RoR) converted land tenure from a bundle of overlapping, contextual claims into a bounded, enumerable, transferable property unit. This advances Ch. 2.3.2 because it gives the RoR's epistemological architecture a name and a genealogy — the enumerative modality — rather than treating it as a neutral administrative tool.

**DILRMP as a continuation, not a break, in the documentation project**
The thesis currently frames DILRMP as a modernisation of the record system. Cohn's framework suggests a more productive framing: DILRMP is the latest iteration of the documentation project, substituting GIS/blockchain/algorithm for Gunter's chain/survey theodolite/patwari register. This advances Ch. 3.4 because it reframes the digitisation question — the problem is not technological capacity but epistemological architecture: what the system can and cannot see.

**Pre-colonial customary tenure: a fieldwork question**
Cohn shows that colonial codification replaced living, locally interpreted normative orders with textual constructs. For the thesis, the key question is: what did land tenure in Odisha look like *before* the British settlement operations? This is a question for archival fieldwork in the Odisha State Archives (Bhubaneswar) — particularly the pre-settlement records and the gadjat files for the princely states. Cohn makes visible what needs to be excavated.

**The Gunter's chain as a survey modality instrument**
Cohn analyses how the survey modality used specific instruments (theodolite, triangulation, chain measurement) to impose the grid. For Ch. 6.1 (methodology), this is a useful comparative frame: the GPS/CORS network used in DILRMP's drone surveys is a new generation of the same modality — one that faces the same epistemological limits (it records what is visible on the surface, not what is practised on the ground).

**Colebrooke's taxonomy → Odisha's settlement regime categories**
Cohn shows that Colebrooke's digests of Hindu law created new legal categories. In Odisha, the settlement operations produced analogous taxonomic outputs: the distinction between *ryot*, *under-ryot*, *occupancy tenant*, and *non-occupancy tenant* in the Orissa Tenancy Act (1913). Tracing the genealogy of these categories back to the Cohn-documented codification process would strengthen Ch. 4.1's argument about the colonial constitution of property categories.

**The native informant problem in fieldwork design**
Cohn's analysis of how munshis and pandits mediated colonial knowledge while their contributions were erased has a direct methodological implication for the thesis's field research. The RI/patwari who mediates between the land record and the farmer is the contemporary native informant — their knowledge is essential to any account of the record-ground gap, but they have structural reasons to manage what they reveal and to whom. This is a question for Ch. 5.1 (research design) — how to account for the informant's position, not just their information.

**Path dependence as the unifying mechanism**
Cohn provides the historical genealogy; the thesis needs a mechanism that connects colonial origins to postcolonial outcomes. Path dependence (Mahoney 2000; Pierson 2004) is that mechanism: the investigative modalities created organisational forms, categories, and incentive structures that became self-reproducing. This advances Ch. 7.4 (contributions) by naming what the thesis adds to both the colonial history literature (a mechanism) and the path dependence literature (an empirical case from land administration).
