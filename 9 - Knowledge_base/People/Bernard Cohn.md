# Bernard Cohn

## Scholarly Lens
Bernard S. Cohn (1928–2003) was Professor of History and Anthropology at the University of Chicago. He is widely regarded as the founder of the historical anthropology of colonialism in South Asia. His intellectual project — developed across a series of essays from the 1950s through the 1990s — was to understand British colonialism in India as a *cultural project of control*, in which the production of knowledge was as constitutive of power as military conquest or administrative extraction.

Cohn's key intellectual move was to apply an anthropological sensibility to colonial history: he treated British administrators not as transparent recorders of Indian reality but as producers of culturally specific knowledge that reorganised Indian society in the act of describing it. Long before Foucault's vocabulary of knowledge-power entered South Asian studies, and before Said's *Orientalism* (1978), Cohn was making the same argument through archival and ethnographic research.

His framework arrived in the Indian land administration literature through Nicholas Dirks (who edited *Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge*), Partha Chatterjee, and Bernard Cohn's direct student David Ludden. All three explicitly apply Cohn's modalities framework to land administration, fiscal extraction, and agrarian property.

## Core Bibliography (PhD-Relevant)
- **Cohn, B. S. (1996). *Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge: The British in India*. Princeton University Press.** — Your primary Cohn source. The Introduction's taxonomy of investigative modalities is the theoretically generative section for Chapters 2 and 3.
- Cohn, B. S. (1987). *An Anthropologist among the Historians and Other Essays*. Oxford University Press. — Essays on the colonial construction of Indian society; includes early work on land tenure in Uttar Pradesh.
- Cohn, B. S. (1990). "The Census, Social Structure and Objectification in South Asia." In *An Anthropologist among the Historians*. — The enumeration modality in detail; directly relevant to how caste became the unit of land-rights classification.

## Key Concepts Introduced
- [[Investigative Modalities]] — the culturally specific social practices through which colonial states produce knowledge (survey, enumeration, historiographic, observation/travel, museological, surveillance)
- [[Colonial Legal Reconstitution of Property]] — the transformation of Indian property relations through codification and English case law

## Inter-Person Links
- **Precedes [[James C. Scott]]**: Cohn's survey modality is the historical mechanism that produces Scott's *legibility*. The settlement officer who maps and classifies tenure is Cohn's figure; the legible landscape that results is Scott's. The two frameworks are the historical and the structural sides of the same phenomenon.
- **Extends Said**: Cohn makes the same argument as Said's *Orientalism* (1978) for the administrative rather than literary domain. His investigative modalities framework applied to land governance is more analytically precise than Said for the purposes of this thesis — it specifies *which* knowledge practices produce *which* administrative outcomes.
- **With [[Henry Sumner Maine]]** (critically): Maine's theory of the village community and the evolution from status to contract provided the ideological framework within which colonial property law was designed. Cohn shows that Maine was reading a colonial documentary artefact as historical fact — circular reasoning with vast administrative consequences.
- **Extends Cohn → David Ludden**: Ludden's *Peasant History in South India* (1985) applies Cohn's modalities to the Madras Presidency's agrarian history. Particularly useful for understanding how the ryotwari settlement produced a specific form of individual, documented title that excluded women and lower castes.

## Methodological Bias
Cohn works through archival historical anthropology: deep reading of colonial administrative texts, Company records, settlement reports, and correspondence, combined with an anthropological sensibility to cultural practice and institutional context. He does not quantify; he does not use models. His analysis is interpretive and genealogical — tracing how specific practices of knowledge production shaped administrative categories that persist into the present.

**Limitation for this thesis**: Cohn's analysis centres on Bengal and the Madras Presidency. His direct material on Odisha is thin. The value to this PhD is theoretical, not empirical: his modalities framework provides the vocabulary to analyse the Odisha settlement tradition through the same lens. Chapter 2.4 should make this extension explicit.
