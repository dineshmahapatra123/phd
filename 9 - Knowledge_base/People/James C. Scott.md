# James C. Scott

## Scholarly Lens
James C. Scott (1936–2024) was Sterling Professor of Political Science and Anthropology at Yale University and founder of the Yale Agrarian Studies Programme. A political anthropologist and comparative historical sociologist, his intellectual project spans peasant resistance, state power, and the politics of knowledge. His central argument is that modern states systematically underestimate and destroy the complexity of the social worlds they govern — and that this destruction produces unforeseen, often catastrophic consequences.

His analytical lens combines three commitments:
- **Anti-high-modernist**: Sceptical of technocratic, top-down planning and its claim to scientific authority
- **Subaltern-sympathetic**: Attentive to how the poor, the marginal, and the mobile resist, evade, and subvert state power
- **Institutionalist but heterodox**: Unlike NIE institutionalists, Scott foregrounds power, violence, and knowledge asymmetry rather than transaction costs and efficiency

## Core Bibliography (PhD-Relevant)
- **Scott, J. C. (1998). *Seeing like a state: How certain schemes to improve the human condition have failed*. Yale University Press.** — The legibility framework; your primary Scott source for Chapter 2.
- Scott, J. C. (1985). *Weapons of the weak: Everyday forms of peasant resistance.* Yale University Press. — The micro-politics of resistance; relevant to Chapter 5 fieldwork on community responses to DILRMP.
- Scott, J. C. (1990). *Domination and the arts of resistance: Hidden transcripts.* Yale University Press. — The concept of the "hidden transcript" versus the "public transcript"; useful for interpreting what cultivators say to revenue officials versus what they do on the ground.
- Scott, J. C. (2009). *The art of not being governed.* Yale University Press. — The Zomia thesis: stateless highland peoples in Southeast Asia as a political choice, not a developmental lag. Relevant background for understanding illegibility as deliberate political strategy.

## Key Concepts Introduced
- [[State Legibility]] — the manufactured simplification of complex social reality for state management
- [[High Modernism]] — the technocratic ideology that drives destructive state simplification
- [[Metis]] — practical local knowledge that legibility destroys

## Inter-Person Links
- **Against [[Hernando de Soto]]**: Scott's legibility critique directly confronts the formalisation thesis — what de Soto calls "dead capital" is, for Scott, living local knowledge ([[Metis]]) that formalisation destroys.
- **Against [[Klaus Deininger]]**: Scott's *Waldsterben* parable challenges the NIE optimism of World Bank land titling research. Second-rotation consequences are invisible to cross-sectional impact evaluations.
- **With [[Elinor Ostrom]]**: Shared concern for locally evolved, community-managed institutions and the knowledge they encode. Both argue against universal, top-down rule imposition.
- **With [[Daniel Bromley]]**: Shared critique of formalisation as inevitably beneficial. Both argue for the social and political dimensions of property that economic formalisation ignores.
- **Precedes [[R. Ramakumar]]**: Ramakumar & Ramesh (2023) explicitly extend Scott's legibility concept to the LRUP in Telangana, coining [[Illegibly legible]].

## Methodological Bias
Scott works through comparative historical analysis and ethnographic case studies. *Seeing Like a State* builds an inductive argument from wide-ranging historical cases (German scientific forestry, Haussmann's Paris, Soviet collectivisation, the Stolypin Reform, Tanzania's villagisation). He does not use regression analysis or formal models. His method privileges the thick description of social complexity over measurable outcomes — which is both its strength (it captures what quantitative methods miss) and its limitation (the argument is difficult to falsify and can be stretched to cover almost any state failure).
