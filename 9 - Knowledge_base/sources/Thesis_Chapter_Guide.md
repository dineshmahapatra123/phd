## A note before you begin

This document consolidates an extended consultation on writing six chapters of a doctoral thesis. It is meant to be a working reference: you should be able to open it to any chapter and find, in one place, the argumentative spine for that chapter, the layered reading list, section-by-section writing guidance, and where relevant a deep dive into the primary government documents that anchor the argument empirically.

Six chapters are covered. Chapter 2 is the historical and conceptual scaffold — a global and Indian history of land records, read through three theoretical traditions in conversation. Chapter 3 moves into the political economy of contemporary land records modernisation in India, with particular attention to DILRMP, the post-1991 turn, and the welfare-electoral nexus that DBT has produced. Chapter 4 grounds all of this in Odisha specifically, treating the state's tenurial heterogeneity as a palimpsest. Chapters 5 and 6 are the empirical heart of the thesis: Chapter 5 at the village and household level, Chapter 6 at the parcel level, with the forensic comparison of digital, paper, and ground evidence that gives the thesis its methodological signature. Chapter 7 is the conclusion.

References are tagged throughout with one to three stars. Three stars mark essential readings — texts that should be read fully or near-fully and that anchor the chapter's argument. Two stars mark important readings to engage selectively: introduction, key chapter, conclusion. One star marks texts useful for citation coverage, where abstract-level reading is sufficient. A doctoral chapter cannot meaningfully engage with eighty texts at full depth, and the layered-reading discipline is what makes any large bibliography manageable. The approximate proportions you are aiming for: twelve to fifteen texts at Layer 1, twenty-five to thirty at Layer 2, thirty to forty at Layer 3.

The document has been edited for readability. Repetition has been trimmed, transitions tightened, and the spoken register of the original consultation smoothed into something closer to a finished reference. No analytical content has been removed, and the references themselves are preserved exactly. Where a compact briefing block was prepared (for Chapter 2), it is retained at the end of that chapter for use in future sessions.

## Chapter 2 — History and Evolution of Land Records: Global and Indian Contexts

### The opening question

“I am writing my PhD thesis in Development Studies. I am currently trying to write my Chapter 2. How to start, and what are the best papers I should refer to? Treat this as extremely important. (1) Papers I must refer to formulate the chapter. (2) How to assemble all the concepts and researches to write the chapter. (3) A plan of action to finish.”

### What this chapter has to do

Chapter 2 carries unusual weight. It is the historical-conceptual scaffold on which everything else stands: the political economy chapter, the Odisha chapter, and the two empirical chapters that follow all rely on this one having done its work properly. If the scaffold is weak, the upper floors collapse.

The chapter's section structure is fixed: §2.1 on land records and their functions; §2.2 on the global evolution of land records and titling systems, broken into pre-colonial, colonial, post-colonial, and contemporary or neoliberal phases; §2.3 on the history of land records in India, again across pre-colonial, colonial settlements, and post-independence; §2.4 on the legal and institutional infrastructure for land records in India; and §2.5 on the genesis of DILRMP itself.

Within this structure, the analytical work is done by holding three traditions in conversation across four historical regimes. The three traditions are: state legibility (Scott, Cohn, Foucault), which answers how the state knows land; capitalist agrarian transition (Marx, Byres, Bernstein, Harvey, Levien), which answers for whom land is made knowable; and institutional economics (Smith, Locke, Coase, North, Demsetz, de Soto, Deininger, Acemoglu), which asks whether secure title is sufficient for development. The four regimes form a palimpsest: pre-colonial tributary records as fiscal-extraction instruments; colonial-capitalist records as the failed installation of capitalist property relations; post-independence redistributive records as unfinished land reform; and the contemporary neoliberal-titling regime as accumulation by dispossession through digital legibility.

### The thesis sentence

The chapter's argumentative spine, which every section should serve and the conclusion of the chapter should restate, is the following:

DILRMP cannot be understood through one lens alone. It is simultaneously a state-legibility project, a capitalist-transition project, and an institutional-economics project — and the friction between these three logics produces the village-level contradictions documented in Chapter 5.

Several supporting claims follow from this. Indian land records are not a single inheritance but a palimpsest, and DILRMP attempts to flatten this palimpsest without acknowledging it. The Mughal zabt-dahsala system was fiscal in nature, not a property-rights system as later economists have read it: colonial settlements introduced juridical "property" as a new category, alien to the tributary

## Three versions of the chapter, in conversation

Across the consultation, three locked versions of the chapter framework were developed. They are not alternatives but layers: each applies a distinct analytical tradition to the same historical material, and the final chapter is written by holding all three in conversation. Treating them as substitutes weakens the argument; treating them as complementary is what gives the chapter its distinctive analytical power.

### Version 1 — state legibility

The lens here is land records as instruments of state power. It answers the question: how does the state make land knowable? The argument, in compressed form: from the Mughal zabt-dahsala through Cornwallis's cadastres through DILRMP's digital integration, the Indian state has progressively produced legibility over land, and each phase of legibility produces specific exclusions and specific possibilities of governance. Scott, Cohn, Foucault are the anchors; Bhattacharya's The Great Agrarian Conquest is the India-specific extension.

### Version 2 — capitalist agrarian transition

Here the lens is land records as instruments of capitalist class formation. The question becomes: why does the state make land knowable — for whose accumulation? The argument: the Permanent Settlement attempted, and largely failed, to install capitalist property relations in India; the post-independence reforms partially redistributed but did not complete the agrarian transition; and DILRMP is the contemporary attempt to make Indian agrarian land legible to capital, in a state that has bypassed the classical agrarian transition. Marx, Byres, Bernstein, Harvey, Levien anchor this version; Lerche provides the framing concept (the bypassed transition).

### Version 3 — classical and institutional economics

The third lens reads land records as institutions that lower transaction costs and shape incentives, but also entrench distributive conflicts. It asks: whether well-defined property rights are necessary, sufficient, both, or neither for development. The dominant institutional-economics view treats secure title as a precondition for growth and welfare; the agrarian political-economy view treats secure title as an instrument of class differentiation; the legibility view treats it as a state project of population management. All three are true at once. DILRMP embodies all three logics simultaneously, in tension — and that is the argument the chapter exists to make.

### A unifying paragraph for the colonial section

A useful model for how all three traditions can converge on the same historical moment is the Permanent Settlement. The paragraph would read:

Three different scholarly traditions converge on a single observation about Permanent Settlement: that it created landlordism without creating productive capitalist agriculture. The agrarian-political-economy tradition (Habib, Guha, Banaji) reads this as the failure of capitalist transition under colonial conditions. The state-legibility tradition (Cohn, Bhattacharya) reads it as a colonial knowledge-construction project that produced "the landlord" as a juridical subject. The new institutional economics tradition (Banerjee and Iyer 2005) reads it as a property-rights configuration whose long-run consequences for agricultural investment can be empirically measured. These are three different questions about the same historical moment, and they yield three complementary, not competing, accounts.

## Section-by-section argumentative moves

### §2.1 — Functions of land records

Open with a four-function typology: fiscal, juridical, economic, and governmental. The fiscal and governmental functions come from Scott and Foucault; the juridical and economic functions come from Marx and Smith. Indian land records do all four simultaneously. Polanyi's notion of land as a "fictitious commodity" is the synthesising idea: land is not naturally a commodity, but is made into one administratively, fiscally, and ideologically. Records do this work.

### §2.2 — Global evolution

A crisp historical arc is the right approach here: medieval and feudal records (Domesday, terriers); early modern cadastres (Napoleonic); colonial cadastres (European empires); post-WWII titling waves in Africa, Latin America, and Asia; the neoliberal titling consensus (post-1990s, World Bank, de Soto). Smith and Locke do the philosophical work for the early modern transition; Coase, North, Demsetz, de Soto for the contemporary one.

### §2.3 — India in three layers

The Indian section is the long one and the one that requires most discipline. In §2.3.1, treat Mughal records as fiscal-tributary infrastructure: Habib is the foundational source. In §2.3.2, treat colonial records as the failed capitalist-transition project: Guha and Banaji provide the political-economic frame, Banerjee and Iyer the empirical bridge, Cohn and Bhattacharya the legibility frame. Banerjee and Iyer is central here precisely because it connects V3 and V2 — both Permanent Settlement and Ryotwari were "bad" in different ways for different reasons, and Banerjee and Iyer give you the long-run empirical fingerprint. In §2.3.3, treat post-independence records as redistributive-reform-as-paper: Appu, Patnaik, Bhaduri, Lerche, Bardhan. The mode of production debate adjudicates whether Indian agriculture is capitalist or semi-feudal; the institutional-economics literature asks whether secure individual property rights would unlock growth. Both speak to the same empirical reality, and your chapter should make that clear.

### §2.4 — Legal and institutional infrastructure

This is the section where explicit transaction-cost analysis pays off. India's deeds-based system has high transaction costs, deters investment, and encourages litigation. The Carnegie India argument gets sharpened by Coase and North. But Bardhan and Ostrom remind us that lowering transaction costs is not the only thing that matters. Distributive politics, commons, customary rights, and gendered exclusions cannot be solved by titling alone. The section should hold both these positions together.

### §2.5 — Genesis of DILRMP

DILRMP appears at the convergence of four currents: the unresolved post-reform obsolescence of records (the bypassed agrarian transition); the neoliberal turn in Indian state policy after 1991; the Washington Consensus and its NIE intellectual basis; and the Digital India / JAM trinity infrastructural turn. The closing paragraph of the chapter should suggest that Chapter 3 will pursue this convergence as a political-economy problem, while Chapters 4 to 6 will examine its material outcomes at village and parcel level in Odisha.

## Chapter 3 — Political Economy of Land Records Modernisation in India

### The question that opens this chapter

“Wonderful. I want to probe further and seek references for Chapter 3. Same effort and judiciousness. Chapter 3 sections are: §3.1 Land governance under neoliberalism; §3.2 Land records modernisation and DILRMP; §3.3 State welfare — land records and DBT; §3.4 Critical reflections on the technocratic reforms.”

### What is at stake

If Chapter 2 was about history and concepts — the "what" and "how" of land records — Chapter 3 is where the political-economy argument finally gets made. It answers the "why now, why this way, and in whose interest." The historical scaffolding from Chapter 2 must do active work in this chapter; without it, Chapter 3 risks reading as commentary on contemporary policy rather than as the analytical core of a doctoral thesis.

### The argumentative spine

The central claim of Chapter 3, which every section serves, runs as follows:

DILRMP is not a technocratic land-administration reform; it is the administrative apparatus through which India's post-1991 neoliberal state seeks simultaneously to (a) commodify land for accumulation, (b) target welfare through digital legibility, and (c) construct a market-compatible juridical subject — and the contradictions between these three projects are what produce the implementation failures, exclusions, and contestations documented in subsequent chapters.

Each of the four sections contributes one move toward this argument. §3.1 establishes the macro-political-economy frame, treating neoliberalism as the conditioning context. §3.2 dissects the policy object itself: DILRMP's design, integration, and titling ambition. §3.3 examines the welfare-targeting function, asking how land records have become the gateway to Direct Benefit Transfer. §3.4 develops the critical synthesis, showing why technocratic reform cannot solve what is fundamentally political.

## §3.1 — Land governance under neoliberalism

### The Indian liberalisation literature

The macro frame for this chapter is the post-1991 neoliberal turn in Indian political economy. Atul Kohli's distinction between the pro-business turn of the 1980s and the pro-market turn of the 1990s is the most useful entry point. The agrarian dimension of this transition — the deflationary squeeze on agriculture, the rise of agrarian distress, the political-economic conditions of farmer suicide — is treated most clearly by Utsa Patnaik. The transformation of land itself into a commodity, particularly through the SEZ regime and post-2005 dispossession, is the subject of Michael Levien's work, which is central here.

### What this chapter is, and what it must not become

- Chapter 4 is not "background on Odisha." It is the analytical bridge between the conceptual chapters (2 and 3) and the empirical chapters (5 and 6). If you write it as descriptive context "Odisha has thirty districts, 51 lakh hectares of agricultural land, a complex tenurial history, and significant Adivasi populations" — the viva committee will tear it apart. If you write it as the one place where the three theoretical traditions from Chapter 2 get tested against a specific historical-political context, the chapter does heavy lifting and the village chapters that follow become unimpeachable.

The second sentence is doctoral. The first is undergraduate.

### The argumentative spine

The chapter's central claim runs as follows:

Odisha's land records architecture is the product of four sedimented institutional histories — Bengal-zamindari, Madras-ryotwari, Central-Provinces-malguzari, and princely-state systems — overlain by post-independence reforms (Estate Abolition, Tenancy, Consolidation, OSATIP for Scheduled Areas, Forest Rights Act) and now by DILRMP's digital integration project. This sedimented complexity is not a bureaucratic accident but a political-economic structure: it produces specific patterns of legibility, illegibility, exclusion, and dispossession that vary systematically across coastal/inland, plains/hills, general/Scheduled Areas, and which the village chapters will document at the parcel level.

First, it operationalises the three theoretical traditions from Chapter 2: Odisha's palimpsest is the empirical site where legibility, agrarian transition, and institutional economics all become visible. Second, it grounds the political economy of Chapter 3: Odisha is where DILRMP, PM-Kisan, KALIA, Subhadra, FRA, and the Niyamgiri-style

## Writing the chapter, section by section

### §4.1 — The historical and tenurial palimpsest

The opening section does the heaviest historical work and should be the longest in the chapter. Begin with the palimpsest claim, then walk through four tenure inheritances, each in two or three paragraphs.

Coastal Odisha (Cuttack, Puri, Balasore) — the Bengal-Permanent-Settlement inheritance. Cornwallis's 1793 settlement was not extended directly to Odisha; short-term settlements were experimented with from 1804 before permanent-settlement-style arrangements were imposed in selected Maratha-era zamindaries. The implications: zamindari intermediation, sub-infeudation, and the kanungo-chaudhuri-muqqaddam structure that produces today's record-keeping vocabulary in coastal Odisha. The Odisha Review article and the DDCE/Utkal source are excellent here.

Southern Odisha (Ganjam, Koraput) — the Madras-Ryotwari inheritance. These areas were under Madras Presidency until 1936. Ryotwari tenure meant direct settlement between cultivator and state — but the administrative practice differed: different revenue officials (karnam-shanbhog rather than patwari-kanungo), different forms of measurement, different cadastral conventions. This matters because Bhulekh today has to reconcile these different vocabularies.

Western Odisha (Sambalpur, Bolangir, Kalahandi, parts of Sundargarh) — the Central-Provinces malguzari inheritance. Central Provinces had a village-mahal system with malguzars as middlemen, different from both zamindari and ryotwari. The Mahadevpalli and Khariar sub-inheritances are documented in the historical literature.

Princely-state Odisha (the Garhjats) — twenty-four ex-princely states merged in 1948. These had their own tenure systems, often more feudal, with no proper cadastral surveys at all. The merger imposed Odisha-wide laws over them, but settlement and re-survey work continues to this day in many of these areas. This is the most consequential inheritance for your thesis, because parts of contemporary Odisha still have no proper cadastral records. DILRMP cannot digitise what does not exist.

Close §4.1 by arguing that this palimpsest is the enabling condition for DILRMP's contradictions in Odisha. Centralised digital integration assumes a uniform substrate that Odisha does not have. Bhulekh today presents itself as a single statewide portal, but underneath that single portal sit fundamentally different tenure logics with fundamentally different evidentiary standards. The claim sets up everything that follows.

### §4.2 — Post-independence reforms (1947–1991)

The post-independence section traces the statutory and policy architecture through five legislative moments. The Orissa Estates Abolition Act 1951 abolished zamindari intermediation, though as Appu (1996) documents, abolition was incomplete; ex-intermediaries retained much land as khas cultivation. The Orissa Land Reforms Act 1960 formally abolished sub-tenancy, fixed ceilings, and provided occupancy rights, but the gap between intent and implementation is precisely where the agrarian political-economy literature from Chapter 2 becomes directly applicable. The Orissa Scheduled Areas Transfer of Immovable Property by Scheduled Tribes Regulation 1956 OSATIP — is the third and analytically the most crucial: it restricts alienation of Adivasi land to non-Adivasis in Scheduled Areas and is the statutory foundation that today interfaces with the Forest Rights Act and with DILRMP's titling project. The Orissa Consolidation of Holdings and Prevention of Fragmentation Act 1972 was the first systematic re-survey in much of post-independence Odisha; where consolidation was completed, cadastral records are relatively better, while where it was not, DILRMP digitisation has had to work with much older Maddox-era records. The Orissa Survey and Settlement Act 1958 is the legal framework under which periodic re-surveys are conducted and is the source of the Hal (current) and Sabik (previous) settlement vocabulary that your fieldwork certainly encounters.

The analytical payoff of §4.2: close by arguing that the post-independence reform project in Odisha was partial and uneven across the palimpsest — strongest in coastal districts, weakest in tribal-majority hill districts. This unevenness is itself the precondition for DILRMP's differential effects, which the village chapters will document.

### §4.3 — The political economy of post-1991 Odisha

This section brings the all-India political-economy frame of Chapter 3 down to Odisha specifically. Three sub-themes carry the section.

Mining, industrialisation, and dispossession. Between 2002 and 2005, the Government of Odisha signed forty-two MoUs with multinational companies allowing the exploitation of natural resources in Adivasi regions. This is the political-economic engine of post-1991 Odisha: Niyamgiri, Kalinganagar, POSCO, Vedanta, Lanjigarh — these are the case material. Padel and Das's Out of This Earth, the Niyamgiri Supreme Court judgement (2013), and the N. C. Saxena committee report on Niyamgiri together do the work. The point is not to narrate these cases but to argue that they are the political-economic backdrop against which DILRMP and other land record reforms must be read.

Welfare regime evolution — KALIA to Subhadra. Odisha is empirically one of the most welfare-experimental states in India. KALIA (2018), the Mamata maternity scheme, the Subhadra Yojana (2024) — all of these create direct linkages between land records and welfare delivery. ### §4.4 — The contemporary digital architecture

The contemporary architecture is what the village chapters will encounter. This section sets it out with both administrative-institutional and political-economy attention.

The portal architecture. ### §4.5 — Scheduled Areas, Forest Rights, and the limits of digital legibility

This is the analytically most distinctive subsection of the chapter — the place where Odisha's specificity does the most theoretical work.

Odisha has the largest Adivasi population in India after MP and Maharashtra; thirteen of its thirty districts, in whole or in part, are under Schedule V; OSATIP regulates land transfer; PESA gives Gram Sabhas authority over land matters in Scheduled Areas; FRA recognises forest dweller rights. None of this maps cleanly onto a Torrens-style conclusive titling system. None of this maps cleanly onto Bhulekh's portal logic, which presumes individual, transferable, mappable parcels.

The argument of this section, accordingly, is that the conclusive titling project that DILRMP carries — and that NITI Aayog's Model Bill 2020 codifies — is fundamentally incompatible with the customary, collective, and statutorily-protected tenure forms that govern over a third of Odisha's land area. ### The argumentative spine

The chapter's central claim:

In the villages studied, DILRMP's promise of legibility, transparency, and security has produced not a single uniform outcome but a stratified set of outcomes — beneficial for some households, irrelevant for others, harmful for still others — patterned by caste, class, gender, tenure status, location within the village's historical-tenurial palimpsest, and proximity to the local revenue bureaucracy. The chapter argues that these stratified outcomes are not implementation failures but the predictable distributive effects of a technocratic reform that abstracts from the very social structures (of caste, class, customary tenure, and bureaucratic dependence) that determine who can convert legibility into security.

That sentence refuses the binary "DILRMP succeeded / DILRMP failed." It insists on who gained, who lost, who was unaffected, and why — the only honest political-economy answer. It also positions the village evidence as supporting an argument that travels beyond the village: about the distributive consequences of technocratic reform in general.

## How to build the chapter

I have read many village-studies chapters and most make the same error: they describe the village exhaustively, then describe DILRMP exhaustively, then describe their findings exhaustively. By the time the reader reaches the analytical payoff, the chapter is exhausted and so is the reader. Organise the chapter instead around types of households interacting with land records, not around descriptive themes. The architecture that follows reflects that principle.

### §5.1 — The village setting

This section should be short and analytical, not exhaustive and descriptive. Three or four pages. Cover the location and history (placing the village within the Chapter 4 palimpsest — was this Bengal/Madras/Central Provinces/princely-state Odisha? when was the last settlement operation? has consolidation happened? is it within Schedule V?); population and social composition (caste composition, key sub-castes, presence or absence of Adivasi households, dominant economic activities — use a clean table); the land profile (total agricultural area, distribution by holding size, irrigation status, key crops, prevalence of tenancy and sharecropping); and the administrative geography (RI circle, Tahsil, distance to the Tahsildar's office, frequency of RI visits, presence of consolidation records, reliability of cadastral maps).

Then add one distinctive feature. Every village has one thing that makes it ethnographically valuable. Identify it here in one paragraph — it could be a recent land conflict, a faction of FRA claimants, a SVAMITVA pilot, a sub-collector's special intervention, anything. Foreground it.

A critical writing tip: do not be tempted to write fifteen pages of village description. Your village is a site of analysis, not the object of analysis. The reader needs to know enough about the village to follow the analytical argument, not enough to write a separate monograph about it.

### §5.2 — The bureaucratic interface

This section describes the operational reality of land records administration in your village, as opposed to the official architecture you described in §4.4. Three sub-themes carry it.

The chain of officials. Who is the local RI? How often does he visit? Where does the villager actually go for a mutation — to the RI directly, to the Tahsildar's office, to the amin (the village-level surveyor)? What does it cost in time, in money, in negotiation? This is where Akhil Gupta's Red Tape becomes your theoretical companion — you are documenting exactly the kind of "structural violence" of bureaucracy that he theorises.

The hybrid paper-digital reality. Bhulekh is now functional, but every villager still maintains paper RoRs, every mutation still requires physical signatures and trips, every dispute still references the patta in the cupboard. The friction between the digital record (what Bhulekh shows) and the paper-and-oral record (what the village knows) is your single most analytically generative observation. Document it carefully.

Brokers and intermediaries. Who helps villagers navigate the system? Pleaders outside the Tahsildar's office, local political workers, relatives in government jobs, the literate son in the family. Brokerage is not corruption — it is an infrastructural feature of the system. Document who serves as broker, what they charge (in money, in loyalty, in vote), and how the introduction of digital records has altered (or not altered) brokerage. Bardhan and Mookherjee's clientelism literature and Akhil Gupta's Red Tape are your theoretical anchors.

### §5.3 — Stratified outcomes by household type

- Type A: The asset-secure cultivator Households with clear, undisputed, properly recorded land of viable size. For these, DILRMP delivers what it promises: faster mutations, easier access to records, smoother PM-Kisan transfers, usable collateral for credit. Document four or five such households. These are typically

— but not always — dominant-caste households with longer institutional access. The political-economy point: digital legibility reinforces existing security; it does not create it.

- Type B: The contested holder Households with land they actually cultivate but whose records are contested — inherited, partitioned, encroached, or simply mis-recorded. For these households, Bhulekh becomes a battleground: it freezes errors in place; it makes correction more difficult by requiring legal action against a digital record that now carries the authority of the state; it gives adversaries (including state land claims) a new tool. Document four or five such households. This is where you tell the legibility-as-vulnerability story most powerfully.

## Chapter 6 — Parcel-Level Findings: Forensic Cadastral Comparison

### What Chapter 6 actually is

- Chapter 5 was the village. Chapter 6 is the parcel. This shift in scale matters more than it might first appear.


Most theses on land records modernisation stop at the village level. They tell you about administrative encounters, household experiences, welfare exclusions, broker networks — all of which Chapter 5 covers. What very few theses do is go one scale below: into the survey number, the khata, the plot, the boundary itself. They do not measure parcels in the field, compare them against the Bhulekh database, cross-check against Bhunaksha maps, examine the actual chain of mutations that brought a parcel to its current state, or document the material discrepancies between the digital record, the paper record, the cadastral map, the registered deed, and the ground reality.

That is what Chapter 6 does. And it is precisely this move that makes the thesis distinctive.

When your committee asks the killer question — "why should anyone care that you spent three years on this rather than read NCAER's evaluation reports?" — the answer lives in Chapter 6. The NCAER, NIPFP, and IGIDR reports work at the village, block, and state level using statistical aggregation. They cannot examine specific parcels. You can. Your parcel-level analysis is what statistical evaluation can never produce: granular, situated, materially-grounded evidence of how the digital land records system actually corresponds — or fails to correspond — to the land itself.

### The argumentative spine

At the parcel level, DILRMP's promise of accurate, transparent, conclusive land records is contradicted by systematic material discrepancies — between digital RoR and paper RoR, between RoR and Bhunaksha, between Bhunaksha and ground measurement, between Sabik (pre-settlement) and Hal (current) records, between recorded boundary and physical boundary, between recorded extent and measured extent, between recorded kissam (land classification) and actual land use, and between formal owner-of-record and actual cultivator. The chapter argues that these discrepancies are not random errors but structured patterns — distributed unevenly across the village's parcels, with particular regularity at the edges of socially marginalised holdings, contested boundaries, customary tenure, and historical settlement-era inaccuracies. The political-economic significance is that DILRMP digitises and authorises these inherited discrepancies, giving them new official force.

###### In one phrase: the digitisation of inaccurate records produces authoritative inaccuracy.

### The comparative-empirical move

For every parcel you study, you compare what the digital record says against what other sources of evidence reveal. Those sources include the paper RoR or khatian; the Bhunaksha map; ground measurement (yours, or the amin's); the registered deed where available; the household's stated possession; the village's collective memory of the parcel; the Sabik settlement record; the FRA patta where applicable; and the mutation chain.

For each parcel, some sources will agree and some will disagree. The pattern of agreement and disagreement is your evidence. When you aggregate across parcels, you discover that disagreements cluster systematically: by caste of holder, by holding size, by location within the village, by historical event (consolidation, settlement re-survey, partition), and by tenure type.

- Chapter 6 is essentially a forensic exercise. Write it that way.


## How to build the chapter

### §6.1 — Methodology and ethics

Open with this. Three to four pages. The reader needs to know, before any findings appear, how the comparisons were done, what was compared against what, and what the limits are.

Cover the sample of parcels (how many at this granular level, why these, what the stratification logic was — you almost certainly want a purposive sample stratified by holder caste, holding size, tenure type, and location within the village). Cover the cross-checking protocol: for each parcel, what sources of evidence were assembled — Bhulekh RoR (digital), paper RoR (where extant), Sabik reference (where retrievable), Bhunaksha screenshot, ground measurement (with whatever tool: GPS, chain-tape, the amin's assistance), registered deeds, mutation files, FRA pattas, oral testimony from the holder, and cross-testimony from neighbours.

Then describe the matching matrix: the comparative table that, for each parcel, lays out what each source says about ownership, extent, boundary, kissam, and mutation history. This is the analytical backbone of the chapter. Make the methodology visible.

Cover ethics: pseudonymisation; protocols for findings that could be politically explosive (disputed parcels, encroachments, livestock or forest land claims). And cover the limits of the method honestly: GPS may not be survey-grade; amin-chain measurement is approximate; Sabik records require help to read; the sample is not exhaustive. Honesty strengthens authority. A reader who knows what you could not do will trust what you did do.

### §6.2 — The Sabik-Hal palimpsest at parcel scale

Begin substantive analysis with the deepest temporal layer. Every parcel in your village carries a record-history that extends back through the Hal settlement (1980s or 1990s in most of Odisha), the Sabik settlement (often the Maddox-era 1900s to 1920s in coastal Odisha, or even later in the Garhjats), pre-Maddox arrangements where retrievable, and possibly pre-colonial customary memory carried orally.

For twenty to twenty-five parcels, document what Sabik showed and what Hal showed. You will find discrepancies — names changed, extents adjusted, kissam reclassified, boundaries shifted. These discrepancies are not corrections in any straightforward sense; they are layered authoritative statements, each made at a different historical moment with different methods and different administrative purposes.

The political-economic analysis then asks: who gained and who lost in the Sabik-Hal transition? In Odisha specifically, where consolidation was incomplete and where the Hal settlement coincided with post-Estate-Abolition allocations to former tenants, the Sabik-Hal comparison reveals whose names were entered and whose were dropped, whose extents grew and whose shrank, whose kissam was upgraded and whose downgraded. This is the historical underbelly of digitisation: Bhulekh inherits the Hal record, which inherits the Sabik record, which inherits the Maddox cadastre, which inherits the colonial revenue settlement. The errors and inequities cascade downward; digitisation locks them in.

### §6.3 — RoR-Bhunaksha disagreement: textual-spatial divergence

This is the most technically distinctive section, because most thesis writers cannot do it — and you can.

Bhulekh separates textual records (the RoR, listing owner, extent, kissam, encumbrances) from spatial records (Bhunaksha, the cadastral map). In a properly integrated system, the two should agree absolutely. In practice, they do not.

For each parcel in your sample, compare the RoR-stated extent against the Bhunaksha-calculated polygon area. Compare the RoR-listed neighbours against the Bhunaksha-shown neighbours. Build a table of disagreements.

The patterns are typically the following: disagreements cluster at the edges of holdings; where village boundaries meet government land; near recently mutated parcels; where consolidation was incomplete.

The political-economic analysis: when RoR and Bhunaksha disagree, which is treated as authoritative, for what purposes, and by whom? For PM-Kisan eligibility, the RoR is used. For physical demarcation in a dispute, Bhunaksha is referenced. For ULPIN assignment, both must agree — and where they do not, ULPIN assignment stalls. This textual-spatial divergence is itself productive of new disputes and new exclusions.

### §6.4 — Digital record vs paper record vs ground reality

Three-way comparison. Walk the parcels with the household, measure with whatever tools you have available, compare against what Bhulekh shows and what the household's paper RoR shows.

Several categories of discrepancy will emerge. Extent discrepancies: the recorded extent does not match the measured extent. Boundary discrepancies: the recorded boundary lines diverge from the physical boundaries, because hedges, aals, bunds, or tree lines have moved with cultivation. Location discrepancies: in extreme cases the parcel cannot be located on the map

at the stated coordinates. Inclusion discrepancies: parts of the actually cultivated area are not in the record at all. Exclusion discrepancies: parts of the record do not exist on the ground because they have been encroached, lost to road-widening, or lost to erosion.

Then, the analytically most important move: across your sample, document which holders experience which discrepancies. Smaller holders, women holders, Adivasi holders, marginal-caste holders — versus dominant-caste larger holders. The argument the section makes is that discrepancy is socially distributed. This is the empirical heart of Chapter 6.

### §6.5 — Mutation chains

This will be the most narratively rich section. For eight to twelve carefully chosen parcels, trace the full mutation chain across decades: who acquired the parcel from whom, when, on the basis of what document, with what disputes along the way. Each mutation is a moment of contact between household and bureaucracy. The chain reveals patterns.

Three kinds of finding are particularly important. Mutations stuck: pending sometimes for years. Document who is harmed by the delay (the heir, the woman to be co-recorded, the buyer in an unregistered transfer). Mutations never recorded: where actual cultivation has shifted but the record has not, producing a class of invisible transactions that the official record cannot see. Mutations retrospectively recorded: the most contentious category, where someone has produced a deed and had a mutation recorded that the original holder disputes. Bhulekh now shows the contested version as authoritative.

The political-economic framing: mutation is the moment of transfer of legibility, and therefore the moment of greatest contestation and greatest bureaucratic discretion. Connect this section back to §5.2's discussion of brokerage — brokers are most active at the moment of mutation.

### §6.6 — Kissam, common land, customary tenure

Bhulekh records private agricultural parcels meticulously. It records government land less well. And it records common land — gochar (grazing), baori (water tank), jangal (forest), padaar (uplands) — in ways that are systematically vulnerable to misrecording, misclassification, and conversion to private use.

Where your villages contain FRA-eligible land, document how it appears in Bhulekh, or fails to. Sometimes pattas have been issued but no Bhunaksha polygon exists. Sometimes a polygon exists but the kissam shows it as forest. Sometimes both exist but conflict over what they say. This juridical incoherence — heritable rights over land that the cadastral system records as forest — is itself a major political-economic finding.

The conceptual frame here links back to Chapter 2 (Scott, Cohn, Tania Li): customary, common, and forest tenure systems are structurally illegible to a parcel-based digital system. This is not a failure of DILRMP. It is a constitutive feature of cadastral modernity.

### §6.7 — The parcel-welfare nexus

This section closes the analytical loop with §3.3 and §5.5. The argument: parcel-level discrepancies translate directly into welfare exclusions.

PM-Kisan exclusions traced to parcel-level problems. For each excluded household identified in Chapter 5, return to the parcel-level record and trace the cause. KALIA vs PM-Kisan comparison. This directly supports the §3.3 substitution thesis at the empirical level. KALIA's broader inclusion criteria (including sharecroppers) reach households that PM-Kisan structurally cannot. Show this for specific households.

Two to three pages. Summarise the typology of discrepancies, the patterns of their distribution, and the welfare consequences. The political-economic significance is that the inaccuracy is now harder to challenge, because it carries digital authority. The contest over land records, once between people and patwaris, has become a contest between people and databases. This shift disadvantages those who have least access to the means of challenge — brokers, lawyers, Tahsildar appointments, digital interfaces.

## Chapter 7 — Conclusion

### What the conclusion actually is

Most doctoral conclusions fail in the same way: they summarise. They walk the reader through what each preceding chapter argued, restate the main findings, end with paragraphs on directions for future research. This is what your committee has been trained to expect — which means it is also what no committee remembers ten minutes after closing the document.

A conclusion that succeeds does something different. It performs four moves, in this order.

First, it states what the thesis has established — not what it argued, but what is now known that was not known before. The verb shifts from "this thesis argues" to "this thesis establishes." The register is one of completed work, not ongoing inquiry.

Second, it makes the theoretical contribution visible: where, in Development Studies, in the political economy of land in India, in institutional economics, in village studies, does your thesis now sit? What can scholars working in these fields not ignore that they could have ignored before?

Third, it makes the practical and political contribution visible. What does this thesis mean for how land records modernisation is, could be, or should be done?

Fourth, it situates the thesis in the broader political moment. Indian land records modernisation is part of a large transformation in how the post-1991 state knows, governs, and distributes resources. The conclusion earns the right to name that transformation and to locate the thesis within it.

The conclusion is your last and best opportunity to be remembered. Treat it as such.

## The six-section architecture

### §7.1 — What this thesis has established

Three or four pages. Open with a clear declarative restatement of what the thesis has shown not chapter by chapter, but organised by what is now known. The temptation to summarise chapter-by-chapter is strong; resist it.

Begin with the central finding: DILRMP is not the technocratic administrative reform it presents itself as, but the administrative apparatus through which the post-1991 Indian state simultaneously commodifies land, targets welfare through digital legibility, and constructs a market-compatible juridical subject — and the contradictions between these projects produce the stratified outcomes that the village and parcel-level evidence documents.

Then organise into specific established findings, not by chapter but by domain of knowledge:

- • On the history of Indian land records: the thesis has established that Indian land records are a palimpsest of pre-colonial fiscal-tributary infrastructure, colonial-capitalist juridical instruments, post-independence redistributive instruments, and neoliberal digital instruments

- — and that DILRMP attempts to flatten this palimpsest without acknowledging its constituent layers.
- • On the political economy of land records modernisation: DILRMP is unintelligible outside the post-1991 neoliberal turn, the World Bank's land policy doctrine, the NITI Aayog Model Bill on conclusive titling, and the techno-patrimonial welfare regime that has emerged since 2014.
- • On the substitution argument: DBT-based welfare schemes (PM-Kisan, KALIA, Subhadra) have substituted for structural reform of agrarian distress, with land records serving as the infrastructure that makes this substitution operationally possible.
- • On the Odisha-specific palimpsest: Odisha's land governance is shaped by four sedimented institutional inheritances overlain by post-independence reforms and now by Bhulekh, producing specific patterns of legibility and exclusion that vary systematically across coastal, inland, plains, hills, general, and Scheduled Areas.

- • On the parcel-level evidence: the digital records system carries systematic material discrepancies that are not random errors but socially structured patterns, with direct consequences for welfare access and credit.


This is the established record. Present it as such. Do not hedge.

### §7.2 — The compressed argument

The most important section in the chapter. Two to three pages. Make the single integrated argument visible in its most powerful and condensed form. Strip away qualifications. Write as if you had ten minutes to convince a hostile but intelligent reader that the thesis is correct.

A model compressed argument might read:

Indian land records modernisation cannot be understood as a technocratic administrative project. It is the contemporary phase of a centuries-long political-economic process through which the Indian state — colonial and post-colonial — has produced legibility over land in ways that systematically structure who can claim, hold, transact, mortgage, and benefit from land. DILRMP, conclusive titling, ULPIN, Bhu-Aadhaar, AgriStack — these are not technical instruments; they are the contemporary form of a project that runs from the Mughal zabt-dahsala through Cornwallis's Permanent Settlement through post-independence land reform to today. What makes the contemporary phase distinctive is that it operates simultaneously as commodification (preparing land for capital), legibility (targeting welfare to populations), and juridification (constructing market-compatible subjects); that it does this through digital infrastructures that abstract from the social structures determining who can convert legibility into security; and that it substitutes for structural reform of agrarian distress through cash transfers whose distribution depends on the very land records it is producing. The thesis has shown all of this — historically in Chapter 2, politically in Chapter 3, contextually in Chapter 4, and empirically in Chapters 5 and 6.

On DILRMP and conclusive titling. One to two pages. Briefly, with some understatement, make the political and intellectual point that the entire thesis has been building toward.

Presented as technologies of inclusion, efficiency, and welfare, they are also — as the thesis has shown — technologies of sorting. They determine who is seen and who is not, whose rights are recorded and whose are not, who receives transfers and who is excluded, whose claim to land carries digital authority and whose does not.

## Writing craft for the conclusion

### Voice

This is the most authoritative voice in the thesis. "I have shown," "the thesis has established," "the evidence demonstrates" — these constructions are now appropriate. Earlier chapters could be cautious; this one cannot. The reader has spent six chapters following you; they want to know what you think, not what the literature says.

### Citation density

Citations should be sparse. The conclusion is not a place to introduce new literature except where strictly necessary. The reader does not need sources in this chapter — they need the argument.

### Length

Eight thousand to ten thousand words. Shorter reads like hedging; longer reads like not knowing where to stop. The proportions above are a guide: §7.1 and §7.3 longest; §7.4, §7.5, §7.6, §7.7 shorter; §7.2 shortest at two pages but carrying the heaviest analytical load.

Spend disproportionate time on it. The last sentence is what your committee will read just before deciding what they think of you. It should not summarise; it should land.

The kind of sentence: "The land beneath the portal is the same land it has always been; what has changed is who can see it, on whose authority, and to whose benefit." Or: "The story this thesis has told is not one of bureaucratic failure or technological success; it is a story of how the digital age inherits the older politics of property, and digitises them."

## The deepest advice on the conclusion

When you sit down to write the conclusion, you will be tired. The thesis will have taken three years, perhaps five. You will be ready to be done.

The temptation will be to write a competent conclusion that closes the document — summary, contribution, limitations, further research, end. Most doctoral conclusions are written this way. Most are forgotten by the committee within minutes of the viva ending.

The chapter you have the opportunity to write is one your committee will quote back to you for the next ten years. It is the chapter where, having earned the right through sixty or eighty thousand words of careful work, you write with full authority about what you have done, what it means, and why it matters.

Almost no one writes this chapter, because almost no one believes, at the end of a doctoral thesis, that they have the right to. You do. You have done the work. You have walked the parcels. You have read the patta in the cupboard. You have measured the discrepancy between what Bhulekh says and what the land says. You have spoken to the Tahsildar and the RI and the amin and the widow whose husband's mutation has been pending for two years and the Adivasi household whose forest land is not on the map. You have read the Maddox Settlement Reports and the NITI Aayog Model Bill and the 15th Finance Commission and the Niyamgiri judgement and the Drèze-Khera Aadhaar studies and the Aiyar-Sircar techno-patrimonialism papers. You have built three theoretical traditions in conversation across four historical regimes. You have mapped Odisha's four-fold tenurial palimpsest. You have constructed the parcel-level forensic method that no one else has brought to Indian land records.

The work is done. The conclusion is where you write as someone who has done it. That is the only advice that matters.
