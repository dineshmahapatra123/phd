## Thesis

**Title**: Land Records Modernisation in Odisha: A Study of History, Policy and Implications

**Core Argument**: India's land records are a colonial administrative inheritance — designed as revenue instruments, not title documents — that the postcolonial state has never fully reformed. The Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme (DILRMP) pursues incremental technological fixes (digitisation, integration, e-mutation) without resolving the foundational disjuncture between records and ground reality. This thesis critically analyses that gap: between the stated objectives of modernisation, the design of the programme, and its outcomes for different sections of rural society in Odisha.

**Disciplinary Home**: Institutional economics (NIE tradition) × agrarian political economy × historical institutionalism. Mixed methods: policy analysis, historical archives, household surveys, semi-structured interviews, physical land survey (Gunter's chain). Observational — not causal inference.

**Field Site**: Two villages in Nayagarh district, Odisha.

---

## Research Questions

1. What is the progress of DILRMP in Odisha — motivation, design, and implementation?
2. Are the objectives and design of the modernisation programme in harmony with outcomes? How do different sections of society use (or misuse) updated RoRs?
3. How does the history of land ownership and tenancy shape current ownership — through inheritance, sale, parcelisation, and partition?
4. What is the source of disjuncture between land records (RoR, cadastral maps) and actual possession/ownership on the ground?
5. Are the recent modernisation efforts technocratic — does computerisation resolve ground-level conflicts, or merely restate them digitally?
6. How far have alternative methods to Survey and Settlement (integration, Bhulekh, Bhunaksha, ULPIN) brought parity and uniformity?

## Active Debates — What This Thesis Is Entering

These are the live intellectual tensions. Contradictions from any paper that touch these are high priority for the KB:

1. **Presumptive vs. conclusive title** — India's unfinished transition; why records remain presumptive 230 years after the Permanent Settlement. Central to Ch. 2 and Ch. 3.
2. **Formalisation as growth vs. formalisation as dispossession** — de Soto's optimism vs. Benjamin & Raman (2011) Karnataka evidence that digitisation aided land alienation. Central to Ch. 3.
3. **Technocratic modernisation vs. political economy of reform** — does computerisation resolve ground-level conflicts or merely restate them digitally? Survey and Settlement as the only substantive fix vs. incremental digital alternatives. Central to Ch. 3.4 and Ch. 4.5.
4. **Colonial revenue record → postcolonial title document** — the structural misfit at the heart of the Indian land records system; records designed to fix revenue liability, not prove ownership. Central to Ch. 2 and Ch. 4.
5. **Spatial-textual disjuncture** — 100 per cent digitisation of RoRs but only 6 per cent resurvey completion in Odisha; old cadastral maps (last updated in most villages in the 1960s) as the governing spatial reality. Central to Ch. 4 and Ch. 6.
6. **RoR as welfare inclusion vs. RoR as exclusion mechanism** — PM-Kisan/CM-Kisan inclusion and exclusion; RoR as a tool that simultaneously enables welfare access and facilitates land acquisition without recognising traditional or informal claims. Central to Ch. 3.3, Ch. 4.4, Ch. 5.8.
7. **NIE efficiency logic vs. agrarian social relations** — whether institutional economics framing for land formalisation can account for class, caste, gender, commons, and informal tenure. Central to Ch. 1 and Ch. 5.
8. **Ownership vs. possession on the ground** — the gap between what RoRs record and who actually possesses, uses, and has social claim to land; shaped by inheritance, partition, encroachment, tenancy, and land use conversion. Central to Ch. 5.5 and Ch. 6.
9. **World Bank and international institutions as agenda-setters** — whether DILRMP and the push toward conclusive titling is India's own policy trajectory or shaped by international development orthodoxy. Central to Ch. 3.1.

## Relevance Test for `/compile-phd`

Before extracting any Concept, Person, or Method from any paper, ask these three questions in order. A candidate must pass all three. This applies to every source — not just the paper currently being processed.

---

### Test 1 — Research Question Link (Direct, not metaphorical)

*Does this concept bear directly on at least one of the six research questions above?*

The connection must be **institutional or empirical**, not structural or metaphorical. One logical step is allowed; more than one is a failure.

**Passes**: A concept about how colonial revenue settlements created presumptive title → directly explains RQ1 and RQ4.
**Fails**: A concept about colonial knowledge and power → connects to land records only through a chain of analogies (knowledge → classification → land classification). Skip it unless the paper explicitly applies it to land administration.

---

### Test 2 — Geographic and Institutional Grounding

*Is this about India, or a context comparable for a specific, named theoretical reason?*

- Indian contexts always pass.
- Non-Indian contexts pass **only if** the paper's argument is being used for a named theoretical purpose in your thesis (for example: Thailand land titling as an empirical benchmark for formalisation effects; Australia's Torrens System as the origin of conclusive title). In these cases, extract the theoretical contribution, not the local empirics.
- Non-Indian contexts that are neither cited in your proposal nor directly comparable to Odisha's institutional setup: skip.

---

*Would the absence of this concept leave a gap in one of the seven chapters?*

Map the candidate to your chapter structure. If you cannot place it in a specific chapter and explain in one sentence why it belongs there, do not extract it. The chapter map is at the bottom of this file.

### What Automatically Fails All Three Tests

These are not domain exclusions — they are illustrations of what typically fails the three tests above, drawn from the kinds of sources this thesis reads. They are not exhaustive.

- Cultural or literary scholarship (colonial or otherwise) whose connection to land runs only through metaphor — *knowledge as power* applied to aesthetics, not administration.
- Macroeconomic growth theory at the aggregate level with no institutional mechanism connecting to land tenure or records.
- Property rights arguments applied exclusively to intellectual property, corporate governance, or financial assets.
- Non-Indian agrarian contexts whose institutional setup (legal system, colonial history, tenure categories) is not directly comparable and whose theoretical contribution is already covered by a source already in the KB.
- Physical or natural science findings unless directly enabling a land administration method (remote sensing for cadastral survey passes; soil science does not).
