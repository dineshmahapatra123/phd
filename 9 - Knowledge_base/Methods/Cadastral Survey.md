## Description
A technical and administrative method of surveying land to determine boundaries, area, and location of specific tracts. The results are recorded in a cadaster, which serves as a public record for taxation and legal purposes.

## Strengths / Weaknesses
- **Strengths**: Provides precise documentation of rights, reduces boundary disputes, and supports conclusive title systems.
- **Weaknesses**: Extremely high fixed cost and time requirement. Requires skilled surveyors and sophisticated record-keeping infrastructure.
- **Political weakness**: Survey coverage and valuation quality are distinct. Sánchez-Talanquer (2020) shows that landholders may support being entered in the cadaster for legal protection while influencing assessment institutions to keep recorded land values low. A technically expanded cadaster can therefore coexist with weak fiscal and distributive capacity.

## PhD Relevance
A critical component of land record modernization in India. The "mutation lag" often results from the difficulty of updating cadastral records after surveys are completed.

The Odisha study should assess cadastral work field by field rather than treating completion as binary. Owner identification, spatial boundary accuracy, possession, land use, valuation, and competing claims may have different update histories. See [[Selective Legibility]].

### Scott's Frame: The Cadastral Map as Legibility Instrument
Scott (1998) reframes the cadastral map not as a neutral administrative tool but as the "crowning artifact" of the state's project of making land legible — and an active instrument of social transformation:

> *"Just as the flora of the forest were reduced to Normalbaume, so the complex tenure arrangements of customary practice are reduced to freehold, transferrable title. The crowning artifact of this mighty simplification is the cadastral map."* (Scott, 1998, p. 36)

The map is designed for the tax official, the land speculator, and the distant administrator — not for the people who live on the land. Its value lies precisely in its abstraction: it ignores the complexity of actual land use so that a uniform standard can be applied across an entire nation. Crucially, the cadastral map does not merely describe a property system — backed by legal force, it *creates* the system it claims to describe. See [[State Legibility]].
