# Access to Land as Social Process

## Standard Definition
Access to land as social process is Sara Berry's argument that land rights cannot be understood as fixed legal entitlements alone. Access is produced through continuing negotiation among claimants, kin groups, chiefs, courts, state officials, markets, and local political networks.

In this view, land tenure is not a settled endpoint on a path from customary to private property. It is an arena in which rights are interpreted, challenged, recognised, and reopened over time.

## Scholarly Debate
Berry intervenes against two simplified positions in land policy:

- The property-rights formalisation position treats ambiguity as insecurity and assumes that registration or nationalisation can make property rights determine resource allocation.
- The colonial-customary-law position treats colonial codification as a rigid closure of custom.

Berry's argument is different: colonial rule, commercialisation, and postcolonial reform intensified disputes over custom, authority, and land, but did not resolve them. Even where land was registered or sold, access often remained subject to multiple claims.

## PhD Application
This concept is directly relevant to the thesis debate on the gap between RoR entries and ground reality in Odisha. Berry helps explain why that gap is not only an administrative lag. It is also a social fact: possession, inheritance, tenancy, partition, caste location, gendered claims, and kinship recognition can continue to organise land access even when the record names a different owner.

For DILRMP, the implication is sharp. Digitisation can stabilise a record, but it cannot by itself settle the social negotiations through which land access is locally recognised. This maps to:

- Chapter 1.1: agrarian political economy and property rights framework.
- Chapter 3.4: technocratic reform versus political economy.
- Chapter 5.5: ownership versus possession.
- Chapter 5.7: discrepancies between records and ground reality.

## Measurement / Methods
Operational signs in village fieldwork include:

- possession not matching RoR ownership;
- inheritance or partition claims not reflected in mutation;
- multi-owner records whose practical possession is caste- or kin-segmented;
- informal tenancy, sharecropping, or encroachment treated as locally legitimate;
- disputes settled through kin, caste, village, or revenue-office mediation rather than formal title alone.
