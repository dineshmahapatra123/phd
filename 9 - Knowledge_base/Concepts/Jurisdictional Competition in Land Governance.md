# Jurisdictional Competition in Land Governance

## Standard Definition
**Jurisdictional competition in land governance** refers to situations where multiple institutions compete or collaborate to define, recognise, and enforce land claims. Lund argues that effective authority is not always located in statutory government alone. Courts, revenue offices, settlement authorities, customary institutions, local leaders, associations, and informal power holders may all become reference points for claims.

Jurisdiction can be territorial, functional, or personal. A land dispute may therefore move across institutions depending on whether the issue is possession, inheritance, registration, mutation, caste status, tenancy, welfare eligibility, or compensation.

## Scholarly Debate
- **Lund (2016)**: Claims to rights invoke public authority in multiple institutions. Jurisdictions are overlapping fields of authority in the making, not fixed legal boxes.
- **Sally Falk Moore**: Semi-autonomous social fields can generate rules and enforcement even when formal law exists.
- **Indian land administration literature**: The separation of revenue, registration, survey, and courts creates institutional fragmentation that sustains the record-reality gap.

## PhD Application
This concept maps directly onto Odisha's land administration:

- Revenue department maintains RoR and mutation records.
- Registration department records transactions.
- Survey and settlement institutions maintain cadastral maps.
- Courts adjudicate title disputes.
- Panchayat, caste/community actors, brokers, and local political intermediaries often shape practical possession and recognition.

DILRMP's promise of integration is, in Lund's terms, an attempt to reduce jurisdictional competition by consolidating recognition across institutions. Its risk is that it may harden one institution's version of reality without resolving conflicts among others.

## Measurement / Methods
- For each village land dispute, identify which institution was approached first and why.
- Trace whether villagers treat RoR, registered deed, physical possession, map boundary, court order, or community recognition as the most authoritative proof.
- Document cases where different institutions recognise different claimants for the same parcel.
