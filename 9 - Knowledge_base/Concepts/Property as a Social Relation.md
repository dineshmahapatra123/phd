# Property as a Social Relation

The understanding that property is essentially a triadic relationship: a person, an object, and all other persons. A person's right to an object only exists because society and the state enforce the duty of all other persons to respect that right.

## Lund Extension: Property as Recognised Claim
Lund (2016) sharpens this concept by defining property as a legitimised claim to something of value sanctioned by political authority. This makes property more than a relation among persons around an object; it is also a recognition relation between claimants and institutions.

For the thesis, this matters because a Record of Rights entry is not a neutral mirror of ownership. It is the state's recognition of a claim as administratively valid. The RoR therefore participates in producing property as a social relation: it identifies the claimant, defines the parcel, and signals which institution has authority to validate or deny the claim.

## Berry Extension: Property as Ongoing Negotiation
Berry (1993) pushes the concept further by showing that property relations may remain open even after sale, registration, adjudication, or state reform. In her account of African agrarian change, land rights are shaped by social networks, customary interpretation, courts, chiefly authority, lineage membership, migration, labour investment, and political connection.

This matters for Odisha because the RoR does not exhaust the property relation. A record may identify a formal owner while possession, partition, tenancy, inheritance, caste-sahi occupation, or encroachment continue to organise actual access. Berry therefore helps explain why land modernisation can digitise a claim without settling the social relation behind it.

## Ferree et al. Extension: Documentary Claims in Social Forums
Ferree et al. (2023) operationalise the relational character of property by asking respondents to judge another person's tenure security. Their central point is that neighbours, family members, customary authorities, and state actors can each uphold or challenge a claim. Written documents matter because they make a claim visible across these social and institutional forums.

This adds a documentary mechanism to Berry and Lund. A record does not end social negotiation, but it changes the resources available within that negotiation. For Odisha, the key question is not only whether a claimant has an RoR, but how that document changes the responses of co-heirs, neighbours, village institutions, revenue officials, banks, and welfare agencies.
