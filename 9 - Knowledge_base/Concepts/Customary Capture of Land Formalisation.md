# Customary Capture of Land Formalisation

## Standard Definition
Customary capture of land formalisation is the process by which local customary or informal authorities retain practical control over formal titling even when the title is ultimately certified by the state. The key issue is not who signs the final certificate, but who controls claim verification, dispute resolution, social approval, and access to the procedure.

Ribar (2025) shows that in devolved land regimes, chiefs can facilitate titling precisely because they can capture the process and use it to advance local political agendas. In centralised regimes, by contrast, strong chiefs may impede titling because it removes land from their authority.

## Scholarly Debate
This concept qualifies both pro-formalisation and anti-formalisation accounts.

- **Against simple formalisation optimism**: A formal title does not necessarily displace local power. It may travel through local power.
- **Against simple state-legibility pessimism**: Formalisation is not always a purely centralising state project. It can be appropriated by customary elites.
- **With Lund**: Recognition of land claims also recognises the authority of the institution that validates them.
- **With Berry**: Formal records do not end negotiation; they become one resource within local struggles over access.

## PhD Application
The direct empirical setting is Sub-Saharan Africa, but the mechanism is useful for Odisha as a comparative warning. DILRMP's design assumes that digitisation, integration, and standardisation reduce discretion. Ribar suggests that discretion can survive inside the local verification process.

For Odisha, the relevant analogue is not chiefly authority but the practical authority of RIs, Amins, brokers, panchayat actors, dominant landholders, caste networks, and local political intermediaries. A digitised RoR may still depend on who can mobilise local verification, obtain a favourable field report, or push a mutation/correction file through the office.

This concept should be used cautiously: it is not evidence that Odisha operates like Côte d'Ivoire. It is a mechanism for asking whether formalisation centralises authority, decentralises discretion, or allows local intermediaries to capture recognition.

## Measurement / Methods
- Trace each step between a household claim and its entry in the record.
- Identify which actor verifies possession, boundary, inheritance, or partition claims in practice.
- Compare official procedure with the household's actual pathway through the revenue office.
- Record whether weaker claimants avoid correction because they expect local verification to favour dominant groups.
- Distinguish certification authority from practical gatekeeping authority.
