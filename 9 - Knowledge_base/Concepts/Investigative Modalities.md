# Investigative Modalities

## Standard Definition
**Investigative modalities** is Bernard Cohn's term for the culturally specific ways in which the British colonial state produced knowledge about India. A modality is not merely a method but a full social practice — including its personnel, its institutional home, its documentary form, and its intended administrative use. Cohn identifies six modalities: historiographic, observation/travel, survey, enumeration, museological, and surveillance.

For the purposes of this PhD, the **survey** and **enumeration** modalities are directly relevant, as they are the colonial state's primary instruments for mapping land, assessing revenue, and classifying property holders.

### The Survey Modality
Starting in 1770s Bengal, the British began systematically investigating how revenue was assessed and collected. Cohn writes: "Out of this grew the most extensive and continuous administrative activity of the British, which they termed the land-settlement process." The survey modality encompassed:
- **Revenue surveys**: Determining the taxable value of each parcel, the identity of the "responsible" tenure-holder, and the conditions of cultivation — the direct ancestor of the *Record of Rights* (RoR)
- **Topographic and cadastral surveys**: Mathematical mapping of territories, parcels, and boundaries — the direct ancestor of *Bhunaksha*
- **Statistical surveys**: Wellesley's south India surveys (Mackenzie, Buchanan, Heyne) gathering information on agriculture, natural resources, and social conditions

The survey modality fused knowledge production and administrative control into a single activity. A revenue survey was simultaneously an act of fiscal extraction, a classification of property rights, and a fixing of previously fluid tenure arrangements into legible, enforceable categories.

### The Enumeration Modality
The colonial census, from the mid-nineteenth century, created and hardened social categories — notably caste — that then became the basis of property classification. Caste enumerated became caste administered: who could hold which type of tenure, who was classified as a "cultivating class" versus a "landlord class", who was eligible for tenurial protection under the Bengal Tenancy Act or the Odisha Tenancy Act. Enumeration thus did not merely describe the social structure of landholding; it recursively produced it.

## Scholarly Debate
- **Cohn (1996)**: The modalities framework challenges the idea that colonial knowledge was neutral documentation. Knowledge production was the mechanism of colonial control — surveys, censuses, and settlements did not record pre-existing realities but constituted new ones.
- **Scott (1998)**: [[High Modernism]] arrives at the same conclusion from a different angle. Scott's *legibility* is what the survey modality produces; the settlement process is Scott's naming mechanism applied to land. The two frameworks are mutually reinforcing, not competing.
- **Appu (1996)**: The land settlement process as analysed by Cohn is the colonial infrastructure that Appu's account of post-independence reform attempts to dismantle. DILRMP can be read as the third wave of the same survey-modality logic: digital re-settlement.

## PhD Application
Cohn's investigative modalities provide the theoretical vocabulary for Chapter 2's claim that colonial land administration was a knowledge project before it was an infrastructure project. The Bengal revenue surveys of the 1770s–1850s are the origin point for:
- The categories inscribed in the Record of Rights (*khatiyan*, *patta*, *revenue-paying*, *non-revenue-paying*)
- The institutional logic of the *settlement officer* — the colonial official charged with the dual task of knowledge production and tenure classification
- The assumption, still present in DILRMP design, that accurate mapping plus clear attribute data equals secure property rights

**Critical purchase**: DILRMP's digital cadaster is a fourth-generation survey modality. The assumptions it inherits — that land rights can be made legible, individualised, and fixed — come directly from the colonial settlement tradition that Cohn anatomises. Chapter 2.2.2 should explicitly situate DILRMP within this genealogy: it is not a break from colonial practice but its computational continuation.

**Odisha specificity**: Odisha's Bhulekh and Bhunaksha systems directly correspond to Cohn's survey modality outputs: Bhulekh = the textual RoR (attribute data on tenure-holders and rights), Bhunaksha = the spatial cadaster (parcel boundary data). Both trace their genealogy through the Odisha Survey and Settlement Act and before that through Bengal Presidency survey and settlement practice from the 1770s.
