# Land Titling

Land titling is the policy project of converting land claims into formally registered, state-backed title. In the Indian debate, it usually means moving beyond presumptive revenue records and registered deeds toward a system where the state either guarantees title directly or creates a title-registration architecture that substantially reduces uncertainty.

## Standard Definition
Land titling treats land as a legally identifiable parcel attached to a determinate rights holder. It depends on three institutional preconditions: reliable cadastral survey, legally updated records of rights, and a registration/mutation system that keeps transactions synchronized with the public record.

## Scholarly Debate
In the pro-formalization literature, titling is expected to increase tenure security, reduce transaction costs, improve collateral value, and support more active land markets. In the critical Indian literature, including Nayak's account of the shift from land reform to land titling, this promise is treated cautiously: formal title can strengthen markets while leaving tenants, customary users, women, and landless claimants outside the record.

Ferree et al. (2023) show why demand for written documentation must not be equated with demand for state title. Malawian respondents valued written rights issued by both chiefs and the state, and low title uptake coexisted with interest in documentary evidence. Titling packages several attributes together: documentation, state authority, taxation exposure, individualisation, registry access, and possible credit benefits. Policy analysis should identify which attribute citizens seek rather than treating title adoption as the only indicator of demand for secure rights.

Ribar (2025) adds a political-economy qualification to demand-side titling. Households are more likely to seek title where land value or expected agricultural-investment returns justify the cost and risk of formalisation. But uptake is not a simple household calculation. In centralised land regimes, strong customary authorities can impede titling because it removes land administration from their authority. In devolved regimes, the same customary strength can increase titling because local elites can capture the process and maintain control.

This means low title uptake should not automatically be read as ignorance, irrationality, or absence of demand. It may reflect a rational assessment of land value, costs, overlapping claims, local elite resistance, and whether formalisation will actually exit the customary or informal governance system.
