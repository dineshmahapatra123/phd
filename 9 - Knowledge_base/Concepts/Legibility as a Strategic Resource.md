# Legibility as a Strategic Resource

## Standard Definition
**Legibility as a strategic resource** is the proposition that citizens can use written representations of rights as portable evidence to strengthen claims across multiple forums. The document need not be a conclusive state title. A receipt, letter, customary certificate, chief's register, or community map may make a claim visible to neighbours, family members, customary authorities, administrators, courts, banks, or other actors.

Ferree et al. (2023) separate documentary legibility from the authority issuing or enforcing a right. In their Malawi experiment, respondents perceived documented land as more secure and desirable whether a chief or the Ministry of Lands supplied the written rights.

## Scholarly Debate
- **[[James C. Scott]]**: Treats legibility primarily as a state technology for simplifying, taxing, and controlling society.
- **Ferree et al. (2023)**: Reverse the analytical direction. Citizens may actively demand legibility because documents provide evidence they can use to defend claims.
- **[[Mariano Sánchez-Talanquer]]**: Shows that landholders can strategically demand some dimensions of state legibility while avoiding valuation and taxation. Ferree et al. broaden the demand-side argument to non-state documentation and ordinary citizens.
- **[[Christian Lund]]**: Emphasises that claims become rights through institutional recognition. Ferree et al. show that documentation can make a claim portable before, or without, final recognition by a single authority.
- **[[Sara Berry]]**: Treats access and property as outcomes of continuing social negotiation. Documentary evidence becomes one resource within that negotiation rather than its final settlement.

This concept does not establish that documents guarantee secure rights. It identifies documentation as one resource whose effectiveness depends on the forum, rival claims, social relations, literacy, and the authority willing to recognise it.

## PhD Application
This concept adds a necessary qualification to the thesis's critique of DILRMP. A digitised RoR or Bhulekh printout may fail to provide conclusive title or match possession on the ground, yet still reduce evidentiary friction for a claimant. Its practical value should therefore be assessed separately from legal conclusiveness and spatial accuracy.

For Odisha, the fieldwork should distinguish:

1. **Documentary legibility**: Is the claim written, retrievable, and portable?
2. **Issuing authority**: Revenue state, Sub-Registrar, panchayat, customary institution, or private agreement.
3. **Recognition forum**: Which offices or social actors accept the document?
4. **Ground correspondence**: Does it match possession, boundaries, inheritance, tenancy, and secondary rights?

The concept is especially relevant to Chapters 3.4, 5.5, 5.6, and 5.7. It permits a calibrated conclusion: modernisation can increase citizens' documentary leverage without resolving the underlying property relation.

## Measurement / Methods
- Inventory every land-related document held by sampled households, including RoRs, registered deeds, mutation orders, rent receipts, partition papers, informal agreements, and court documents.
- Ask respondents where each document has been accepted or rejected.
- Compare document value across groups with different levels of social support, literacy, caste status, gender, migration history, and political connection.
- Construct a **document-forum matrix** showing which forms of evidence travel across revenue, judicial, financial, welfare, family, and community settings.
- Compare perceived security with legal status, recorded ownership, actual possession, and parcel-level correspondence.
