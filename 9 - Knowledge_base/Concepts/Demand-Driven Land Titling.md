# Demand-Driven Land Titling

## Standard Definition
Demand-driven land titling refers to formalisation systems in which households can seek state-recognised land documents on demand rather than being enrolled through a comprehensive, top-down titling campaign. Titles are available, but households decide whether the expected benefits justify the administrative cost, time, risk, and political consequences of formalisation.

Ribar (2025) argues that household uptake is higher where land value or expected agricultural-investment returns are higher. Titling becomes worthwhile when the potential loss from insecure tenure or foregone investment returns exceeds the costs of seeking a title.

## Scholarly Debate
The standard property-rights literature often assumes that households will adopt formal title when it is available because titles increase security, investment, collateral value, and marketability. Demand-driven titling complicates that assumption. Low uptake may reflect rational calculation, not policy failure alone.

Ribar adds two qualifications:

- **Costs are political, not only administrative**: formalisation maps complex overlapping land use into a hierarchy of recognised claims.
- **Local authority mediates demand**: in centralised regimes, strong customary authorities can impede titling; in devolved regimes, the same authorities may facilitate titling because they can capture the process.

## PhD Application
This concept is relevant to the thesis because DILRMP and related land-record reforms are often evaluated through completion rates, digitisation rates, or uptake of formal procedures. Ribar suggests that uptake should be interpreted through a political economy of demand.

For Odisha, the question is not only whether mutation, RoR correction, Bhunaksha use, ULPIN, or registration integration is available. The fieldwork should ask when households find it worthwhile to update or contest records, and when they avoid formal correction because the process is costly, risky, broker-mediated, or likely to expose latent claims.

## Measurement / Methods
- Record whether households have attempted mutation, correction, partition, map verification, or certified-copy retrieval.
- Ask why households did or did not pursue formal correction.
- Compare demand for correction with land value, expected sale/compensation/welfare benefit, conflict history, and social position.
- Track the non-monetary costs of formalisation: time, broker fees, fear of disputes, risk of excluding relatives or tenants, and local elite pressure.
