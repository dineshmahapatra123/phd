# Selective Legibility

## Standard Definition
**Selective legibility** is the strategic production of state knowledge that makes some attributes of property visible while obscuring others. Sánchez-Talanquer (2020) calls the resulting arrangement a "one-eyed state": cadastral institutions recognise who holds property, but fail to register its real value or competing claims accurately.

The concept separates dimensions often treated as a single state capacity. Ownership registration, valuation, taxation, adjudication, and enforcement can develop unevenly. More complete records of named owners therefore do not necessarily produce more accurate records, stronger fiscal capacity, or broader recognition of subordinate claims.

## Scholarly Debate
- **[[James C. Scott]]** treats legibility primarily as a state project imposed on society for administration and extraction.
- **[[Mariano Sánchez-Talanquer]]** adds a demand-side mechanism: powerful social actors may seek inscription when records protect their property, while capturing valuation or assessment processes to reduce taxation and exclude rival claims.
- **NIE formalisation accounts** generally treat clearer registration as an institutional improvement. Selective legibility shows that formalisation can strengthen established owners without improving the state's knowledge of value, possession, or distributive claims.

The key mechanism has three conditions: a credible threat to property, legal benefits from registration, and sufficiently low expected fiscal costs. Where these conditions hold, landholders may prefer visibility as owners while resisting visibility as taxpayers.

## PhD Application
This concept maps directly to Chapters 2.1, 3.4, 4.3, 5.5, and 5.7. It provides a political-economy test for DILRMP: a record can become more complete in identifying recorded owners while remaining weak in representing possession, tenancy, inheritance, parcel boundaries, encumbrances, or land value.

For Odisha, the fieldwork should therefore disaggregate "modernisation" into separate capacities:

- identification of the recorded rights-holder;
- correspondence between RoR, cadastral map, and possession;
- recognition of tenants, women heirs, informal partitions, and competing claimants;
- accuracy and updating of parcel attributes;
- fiscal or welfare consequences attached to the record.

Digitisation may increase the first capacity while leaving the others stagnant. That is not simply technical failure; it may reflect whose interests the record is institutionally designed or politically pressured to recognise.

## Measurement / Methods
- Compare coverage of named ownership with accuracy of parcel boundaries, possession, valuation, tenancy, and inheritance.
- Track whether improvements in Bhulekh visibility are matched by Bhunaksha correction and ground verification.
- Compare administrative benefits received by recorded owners with the treatment of unrecorded or competing claimants.
- Interview revenue officials and landholders about incentives to update some fields while leaving others unchanged.
