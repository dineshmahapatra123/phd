# Governmentality

## Standard Definition
**Governmentality** is Foucault's name for the modern art of governing that takes population as its primary target, political economy as its major form of knowledge, and security apparatuses as its technical means. It marks a shift from rule understood mainly as sovereign command over territory toward rule through the management of people, things, processes, risks, wealth, circulation, and welfare.

In chapter 4, Foucault does not present government as simply "the state doing things." Government is a historically specific rationality: a way of making domains such as population, economy, territory, and welfare thinkable as objects of calculation and intervention.

## Scholarly Debate
- **Foucault (1991)**: Governmentality shifts analysis away from the sovereign's right to command and toward the practical rationalities by which conduct is directed. This does not abolish sovereignty or discipline; it rearranges them inside a broader governmental triangle.
- **[[James C. Scott]] (1998)**: [[State Legibility]] gives a more concrete administrative account of how the state simplifies land, people, and space. Foucault supplies the broader political rationality; Scott supplies the cadastral and administrative mechanism.
- **NIE / formalisation literature**: Treats land records primarily as instruments of security, market efficiency, and transaction-cost reduction. A governmentality reading asks how those same records also produce governable subjects, eligible beneficiaries, taxable objects, and administratively actionable parcels.

## PhD Application
Governmentality is useful for explaining why [[DILRMP]] is not just a better tax-records project. It helps frame DILRMP as a governmental programme that turns land into interoperable data for multiple state functions: revenue administration, mutation, registration, welfare targeting, credit access, land acquisition, litigation management, and spatial planning.

For Chapter 2.1, it supports the argument that land records have multiple institutional functions beyond revenue: they make land and population governable. For Chapter 3.4, it supports the critique that technocratic modernisation does not merely "computerise" existing records; it reorganises how the state sees, verifies, and acts upon land relations.

In the Odisha case, governmentality helps connect:
- Bhulekh textual records and Bhunaksha spatial maps as governmental knowledge infrastructures
- RoR as both ownership evidence and welfare eligibility gate
- ULPIN as an attempt to make every parcel uniquely addressable
- mutation and registration integration as a way to reduce administrative friction while expanding state visibility

## Measurement / Methods
- Policy analysis of DILRMP objectives, components, and implementation architecture
- Institutional mapping of how RoR, cadastral maps, registration, mutation, welfare databases, and ULPIN are connected
- Field comparison of official record categories against possession, tenancy, inheritance, partition, and customary claims
- Interview analysis of how revenue officials describe "correction," "integration," "verification," and "modernisation"
